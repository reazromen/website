The name *Shital Pati* points to a familiar experience: a mat that feels cool to sit or rest on. Its making, however, involves more than choosing a material that suits a warm climate. It draws together knowledge of plants, preparation, pattern, hand skill and the organization of work within a household.

UNESCO describes Shital Pati as a handcrafted mat made by weaving strips of a green cane known as **murta**. The craft is strongly associated with low-lying villages in greater Sylhet, in northeastern Bangladesh, though practitioners also live elsewhere. The mats are used across the country as sitting mats, bedspreads and prayer mats.

## From plant to woven surface

The finished mat can look simple at first glance: repeated strips create a smooth, flexible surface, sometimes with decorative motifs. But a reliable result depends on the material being collected and processed well, and on the strips being woven with consistent tension. The weave has to hold together while remaining suitable for everyday use.

This is a useful reminder that everyday objects often preserve specialist knowledge precisely because they are used so routinely. A mat may be rolled away after use, but its making contains decisions about the plant, preparation, width of strips, pattern and finish. These decisions are learned through repeated practice rather than through a written recipe alone.

## A household craft and a source of income

UNESCO's description notes that men and women both participate in collecting and processing murta, while women are more involved in weaving. The practice is often family-based, with skills passed from one generation to the next. It can provide income while reinforcing family bonds and a shared sense of identity.

Those details matter because the word “traditional” can hide the labour behind an object. If a craft is treated only as a decorative souvenir, the work and time required to produce it become easy to overlook. A sustainable market needs to recognize the people who grow, gather, prepare, weave and sell the material, not only the final product.

## Continuity does not mean standing still

Shital Pati was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2017. UNESCO also describes community efforts, including cooperatives and craft fairs, intended to help practitioners transmit the skill and maintain its economic viability.

Recognition alone cannot guarantee that a craft remains viable. Demand, access to raw material, changing work opportunities and the ability of younger people to earn a living all shape its future. The most meaningful form of preservation is therefore not to freeze one “authentic” design, but to support the people who keep the knowledge useful and adaptable.

A Shital Pati is an everyday object. That is exactly why it deserves attention: it shows how cultural knowledge can live in ordinary household life, not only in ceremonies or museum collections.
