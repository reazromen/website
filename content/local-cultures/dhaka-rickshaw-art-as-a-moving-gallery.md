In Dhaka, a decorated rickshaw is more than a way to move through traffic. Painted panels, bright motifs, lettering, tassels and other ornament turn the vehicle into a small work of public art. Because the rickshaw travels slowly through streets, the artwork is visible to passengers, pedestrians and other drivers. The city becomes its exhibition space.

UNESCO describes Dhaka's rickshaw craft as a combination of making and decorating the vehicle. Paintings can include flowers, birds, animals, natural scenery, historical events, fables, national heroes, film stars and text. The imagery is not limited to one stable set of symbols; it can reflect popular culture as well as inherited visual habits.

## The craft behind the decoration

A finished rickshaw depends on different kinds of labour. Craftspeople build and assemble the vehicle, while painters create designs and apply them to the panels. UNESCO notes that rickshaw-building knowledge is often transmitted in workshops through demonstration and apprenticeship, while painters may pass skills to children and relatives. The work is practical and collaborative, even when a single painted panel draws the eye.

This division of work is important. Calling the result “folk art” should not obscure the specialist roles and working relationships that produce it. Nor should it make the artists invisible behind the vehicle. The painted surface is the visible part of a wider craft economy.

## Images on the move

A moving rickshaw places images into everyday public life rather than keeping them within a gallery. Motifs can be seen in passing, remembered, copied, altered or used as inspiration for other artworks. The form is therefore both material and social: it exists on the vehicle, but it also becomes part of how people recognize and imagine the city.

That does not mean every rickshaw carries the same style or meaning. Designs differ, and visual culture changes with commissions, materials, workshop practices and the tastes of customers. A careful account should leave room for that variety rather than treating one image as representative of all Dhaka.

## Recognition and continuity

UNESCO inscribed “Rickshaws and rickshaw painting in Dhaka” on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2023. The listing describes decorated rickshaws as emblematic of urban life and a dynamic form of folk art that can create a sense of shared identity and continuity.

The word *dynamic* is essential. The practice is part of contemporary city life, not simply a remnant of an older Dhaka. Its future depends on the craftspeople and painters who maintain the skills, the customers who commission their work, and whether the labour remains economically viable.

To read rickshaw art as culture is to look closely at the people and working processes behind the colour. It is an urban tradition made visible in motion.
