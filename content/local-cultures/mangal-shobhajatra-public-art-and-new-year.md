Pahela Baishakh, the first day of the Bengali calendar, is marked in many ways across Bangladesh. One of its best-known public expressions is **Mangal Shobhajatra**, a procession organized by students and teachers of the Faculty of Fine Art at the University of Dhaka. It combines large masks, figures and floats inspired by folk art with a public celebration of the new year.

The procession's documented history is relatively recent. UNESCO records its beginning in 1989, when students and teachers responded to a period of military rule and wanted to offer the community hope for a better future. The procession takes place on 14 April and is open to the public.

## Making art together

Preparation begins well before the procession. Students and teachers collaborate on masks and floats, and works are produced for the event that express themes such as courage, strength and peace. The process connects design, making, organization and public participation. The result is not only a display of finished objects: it is also a shared act of producing and interpreting them.

The visual language draws on folk heritage, but it should not be mistaken for a complete summary of all Bengali culture. Folk motifs can be reworked in new forms, and the procession itself is a modern public tradition with a traceable starting point. Its continuity comes from repeated participation and the passing of practical skills between students and teachers.

## Why a procession matters

UNESCO describes Mangal Shobhajatra as a symbol of pride in folk heritage and of solidarity across differences of caste, creed, religion, gender and age. The procession's public character is central to that meaning: people can encounter the artworks in the street rather than only inside a gallery or institution.

A procession also changes how art is experienced. Large forms move through a familiar urban environment, become visible to different audiences and are interpreted in the moment. The making process, the route and the gathering of spectators all contribute to the event's cultural significance.

## Living heritage in a changing city

UNESCO inscribed Mangal Shobhajatra on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2016. The inscription recognizes the skills and community knowledge involved, but it does not mean that every edition must look the same. New makers, circumstances and artistic choices can change the procession while keeping its central practice recognizable.

The distinction matters: living heritage is not simply a picture from the past repeated indefinitely. It is knowledge people use to make something meaningful in the present. In Mangal Shobhajatra, the Bengali New Year becomes an occasion for collaborative art, public space and a shared statement about the future.
