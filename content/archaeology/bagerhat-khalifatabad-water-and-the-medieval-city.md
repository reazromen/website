# Bagerhat and Khalifatabad: Water, Brick and a Medieval City

Bagerhat’s historic mosque city is more than a collection of old religious buildings. UNESCO describes the area as evidence of a medieval city formerly known as Khalifatabad, associated with the fifteenth-century figure Ulugh Khan Jahan. Its surviving mosques, tombs, water tanks and other structures reveal how architecture and infrastructure helped shape a settlement in the riverine southwest of present-day Bangladesh.

The Historic Mosque City of Bagerhat was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1985. The site is especially important for the early development of Muslim architecture in Bengal, where brick construction and local building traditions produced a distinctive architectural language.

## The city behind the monuments

The Shat Gombuj Mosque is the best-known structure, but focusing only on its roof and pillars risks missing the urban system around it. Religious buildings existed within a wider landscape of water reservoirs, routes, settlement areas and other monuments. Water storage was not a decorative afterthought in a region where seasonal rainfall, groundwater and surface water shaped everyday life. Tanks and reservoirs were practical infrastructure, while also potentially carrying social and religious significance.

The scale of the historic settlement is another reminder that the site should be read as a city rather than a single building. Archaeological and architectural study can examine how different structures relate to one another, what construction techniques they share, and where they differ. Such comparisons help establish chronology and understand the work of builders and patrons.

## Brick and architectural adaptation

Bagerhat’s monuments show how Islamic architectural forms were adapted to Bengal’s materials and climate. Brick was widely used, and the design of domes, arches, columns and wall surfaces reflects both technical choices and aesthetic priorities. The famous mosque’s many domes create a striking rhythm across the roofline, but the structure’s importance also lies in how its interior supports a large covered prayer space.

Architectural interpretation benefits from avoiding simplistic labels. A building can share features with structures elsewhere while still responding to local materials, craft traditions, religious needs and environmental conditions. Similarity is evidence of a relationship to investigate, not proof of a single source or direct copy.

## Conservation of a living landscape

Historic structures face gradual damage from moisture, salt, plant growth, weathering and inappropriate repairs. The wider setting matters too: changes to drainage or surrounding land use can affect foundations and the visual relationship between monuments. Conservation therefore needs documentation, compatible materials, maintenance and clear management of visitor access.

Bagerhat also demonstrates why heritage protection should preserve relationships, not just façades. A mosque, a water tank and a route through the landscape can together explain how a city functioned. Remove that context and the remaining buildings become harder to understand.

The site offers a material history of urban life in medieval Bengal: religious institutions, water infrastructure, brick technology and settlement planning all appear in the same landscape. That combination is what makes Khalifatabad such a valuable archaeological record.
