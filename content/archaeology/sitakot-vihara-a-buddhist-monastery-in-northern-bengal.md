# Sitakot Vihara: A Buddhist Monastery in Northern Bengal

Sitakot Vihara, in Dinajpur district, is one of the Buddhist archaeological sites that helps widen the picture beyond the better-known complex at Paharpur. Its remains are associated with a monastic layout built around a central courtyard, with rooms arranged along the enclosing structure. The plan belongs to a broader architectural tradition, but each site must still be understood through its own construction history and archaeological context.

## Comparison without assuming sameness

When two monasteries share a courtyard plan or similar room arrangement, the resemblance is a starting point for research. It can point to shared religious needs, a common architectural vocabulary, travelling craftspeople or regional connections. It does not automatically establish that one site copied the other, nor does it prove they operated at the same time. Archaeologists compare masonry, foundations, building phases, artefact assemblages and any inscriptions that can be securely linked to the site.

That process is particularly important for a site whose surviving architecture may be less visually complete than a major monument. A low wall or buried foundation can still preserve information about the original plan, the sequence of construction and the use of space. Accurate survey and excavation records help researchers make those traces comparable with better-known sites.

## The monastery and its surroundings

A monastic complex did not exist in isolation. It needed food, water, building materials and connections with the people who lived and worked nearby. Archaeological evidence may illuminate some of those relationships directly, while others remain hypotheses to be tested. The absence of a market building or a written record does not mean an institution lacked economic ties; it means researchers must look for other evidence and remain careful about what can be concluded.

Portable finds are useful only when their context is documented. Pottery, terracotta fragments, metal objects and other materials can help establish chronology and activity, but they must be assessed alongside stratigraphy and architectural phases. A broad date range is often more honest than an exact year unsupported by evidence.

## Protecting the evidence

Vegetation, seasonal water, erosion, cultivation and nearby development can damage exposed remains or disturb deposits that have not yet been excavated. Conservation begins with recording and monitoring, not simply rebuilding walls. A site plan, photographs, measured drawings and a clear record of interventions can preserve information even when physical materials deteriorate.

Sitakot Vihara matters as part of a wider network of Buddhist sites in Bangladesh. Comparison with Paharpur and Mainamati can help researchers ask how monastic architecture varied across regions, how institutions were supported and how religious life changed through time. The goal is not to rank monuments by size, but to understand the different evidence each one contributes.
