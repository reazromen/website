# Paharpur: Somapura Mahavihara and a Wider Monastery Network

At Paharpur in Naogaon district, the remains of Somapura Mahavihara make a large architectural argument visible in brick. A broad quadrangular enclosure surrounds a central shrine, while rows of monastic cells face inward toward the courtyard. The plan is orderly, but the site was not merely an impressive building. It was a religious institution, a place of residence and study, and part of a wider network of Buddhist centres.

UNESCO describes Somapura as an important intellectual centre that flourished under Pala patronage. The complex is associated with the Pala ruler Dharmapala, whose reign is generally placed in the late eighth and early ninth centuries. An inscribed clay seal found at the site helped connect the ruins with the name Somapura Mahavihara. Paharpur was added to the World Heritage List in 1985.

## Architecture as evidence

The plan is one of the site’s most informative features. The enclosing ranges contained 177 cells in the later building phase described by UNESCO. Their repeated arrangement suggests an organised monastic community, while the central shrine established a focal point for ritual and movement. The building’s cruciform design and terracotta decoration make the monument legible as both a religious space and a work of architectural design.

Terracotta plaques and stone sculpture are not just decoration to be admired in isolation. They provide evidence about artistic practice, religious imagery, available materials and the choices made by patrons and craftspeople. The imagery includes a range of subjects, and the sculptures at the base of the central shrine show that the visual world of the complex was not reducible to a single, simple category.

## Bengal was connected

Paharpur is particularly significant because its architectural form has been compared with Buddhist monuments elsewhere in Asia, including sites in Myanmar and Java. Such comparisons do not mean that every building was copied directly from Paharpur. They point instead to a wider world of religious travel, shared design ideas, patronage and the movement of skilled people. Architectural influence is best understood as a relationship that can be adapted locally, not as a one-way chain of imitation.

The monastery’s historical links with other centres of Buddhist learning also remind us that medieval Bengal was not isolated. Religious institutions could connect scholars, texts, donors and travellers across political boundaries.

## Conservation is part of the story

Brick, exposed terracotta and carved stone are vulnerable to water, salts, plant growth and changing environmental conditions. The remains that visitors see are therefore also a record of modern decisions about excavation, conservation and presentation. Protecting a site means more than repairing a wall: it requires managing drainage, vegetation, visitor movement, documentation and the wider setting.

Paharpur rewards close attention to both scale and detail. The plan tells us how the institution organised space; the objects and surfaces preserve traces of artistic labour; and comparisons with other sites place Bengal inside a broader Asian history. Together, they make Somapura Mahavihara a source for studying not only a monument, but a connected world of learning.
