# Mainamati and Shalban Vihara: The Archaeology of Lalmai

The archaeological landscape of Mainamati–Lalmai, near Cumilla, is not one isolated monument. It is a cluster of mounds and excavated remains spread across a ridge, including monasteries, stupas and other structures associated with Buddhist religious life. Shalban Vihara is among the best-known sites, but it makes most sense when considered alongside the wider group rather than as a self-contained attraction.

The remains are commonly associated with early medieval Buddhist institutions in southeastern Bengal. Excavations have produced architectural evidence and portable finds that help researchers study religious patronage, craft production and connections between settlements. The site also appears in Bangladesh’s official archaeological publications, including the Department of Archaeology’s historical list of work on Mainamati and related sites.

## A landscape of institutions

A monastery’s plan can reveal how space was organised, but no single plan tells the whole story. Cells, courtyards, shrines and stupas suggest different patterns of residence, movement and ritual. Differences between sites may reflect chronology, institutional function, patronage or local adaptation. Archaeologists therefore compare building layouts and construction phases rather than assuming every monastery followed one fixed model.

The concentration of sites around Mainamati raises questions about the relationship between religious establishments and their surrounding communities. Who supplied food and building materials? How did patrons support construction and maintenance? What kinds of objects circulated through the area? These questions need evidence from architecture, inscriptions, ceramics, coins and other finds, interpreted in context.

## Objects and their limits

Small artefacts can be especially informative. A seal or inscription may preserve a personal name, title or institutional association. Pottery can help establish a relative chronology when forms and fabrics are compared across securely documented layers. Images and sculptural fragments can illuminate religious practice and artistic choices. But an object without a reliable archaeological context can lose much of its explanatory value; provenance is part of the evidence, not administrative paperwork added afterward.

The same caution applies to dating. A site may have been used, rebuilt or repurposed across several periods. A date attached to one construction phase should not automatically be applied to every structure on the ridge.

## Preservation across a whole ridge

Because Mainamati is a landscape of multiple sites, conservation cannot focus only on the most recognisable building. Drainage, vegetation, settlement pressure, visitor access and the relationship between excavated and unexcavated mounds all affect the archaeological record. Mapping and documenting the wider area are essential if the relationships among sites are to remain interpretable.

Mainamati–Lalmai is important precisely because it resists a single, simple narrative. Its many remains provide opportunities to study how Buddhist institutions developed in southeastern Bengal, how they were supported, and how their material culture changed over time.
