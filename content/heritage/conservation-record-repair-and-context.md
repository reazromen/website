When a historic building looks damaged, the most visible response is often to repair the surface. But a crack, salt deposit, missing ornament or altered doorway may be evidence of a larger story. Before deciding what to replace, a conservation team needs to understand how the building was made, what has changed and what is causing the current problem.

UNESCO's documentation for the Historic Mosque City of Bagerhat describes challenges that include salinity affecting building fabric and the loss of some original features during earlier interventions. These are reminders that conservation is not simply cosmetic improvement. Materials, setting and the history of previous repairs matter.

## Record what is there

A useful record can include measured drawings, photographs, material descriptions, condition mapping, archival sources and interviews with people who know the site. Documentation helps teams compare conditions over time and distinguish original fabric from later work. It also creates a basis for explaining why a particular intervention was chosen.

The record should be honest about uncertainty. A feature may have more than one plausible date or interpretation. Where the evidence is incomplete, a careful account says so rather than presenting a guess as fact.

## Repair is a decision with consequences

Materials that appear visually similar may behave differently when exposed to moisture, heat, salts or structural movement. Incompatible repairs can accelerate deterioration or obscure earlier construction. On the other hand, doing nothing is not automatically preservation: buildings need inspection, maintenance and appropriate management.

Conservation decisions should consider the whole setting as well as the individual structure. A monument's relationship to streets, water, vegetation, neighbouring buildings and ongoing community use can be part of its significance. Removing that context may leave the object standing while making it harder to understand.

## People and use are part of the record

Historic places are not only technical objects. They may be places of worship, work, gathering, memory or local identity. People who use a site can hold knowledge about seasonal changes, recurring problems and long-standing practices that formal records may not capture. Their perspectives should be heard alongside architectural and material evidence.

## A method, not a makeover

UNESCO's World Heritage records and conservation decisions show that heritage management involves documentation, monitoring, appropriate materials, planning and long-term resources. The details differ from site to site; no single recipe can replace a careful assessment.

This article is a general editorial guide based on published heritage documentation, not a conservation specification or a report on the current condition of a particular building.
