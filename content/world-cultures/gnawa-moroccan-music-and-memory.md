Gnawa refers to a set of musical events, performances, community practices and rituals in Morocco. UNESCO's description emphasises the relationship between music and spiritual life, including Sufi brotherhood traditions, lyrics that invoke ancestors and spirits, and ceremonies in which rhythm and collective participation are central.

The history is important. UNESCO connects Gnawa culture to communities shaped by slavery and the slave trade in North Africa from at least the sixteenth century. Its contemporary place in Moroccan identity therefore cannot be understood through sound alone; it also involves memory, religion, social belonging and historical experience.

## More than one performance setting

Gnawa practice is not identical everywhere. UNESCO describes urban ceremonies that may involve all-night music and trance, as well as rural communal meals offered to marabout saints. Instrumentation and clothing can differ between settings. These examples should not be turned into a rigid city-versus-country rule: they illustrate variation in a tradition whose practice is shaped by local communities.

The music is also a collaborative achievement. Musicians, singers, instrument players, organisers and younger learners all contribute to its continuation. Festivals and associations can create opportunities for people to encounter the repertoire and learn instruments, lyrics and related practices.

## Heritage as a living relationship

Gnawa was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2019. Recognition can bring visibility, but it does not by itself explain the tradition or guarantee that every practitioner benefits equally. Questions about who controls performances, how history is presented and how younger people learn are still relevant.

It is also important not to flatten Gnawa into a generic label for “Moroccan music.” Morocco contains many musical and linguistic traditions, and Gnawa itself has distinct histories and practices. A careful account names the tradition, acknowledges the history described by sources and avoids treating one example as a complete portrait of a country.

This article is based on UNESCO's public description and editorial analysis, not field reporting. Listening to specific performers and reading accounts from practitioners would add detail beyond this overview.
