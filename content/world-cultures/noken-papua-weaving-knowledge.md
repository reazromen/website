Noken is a knotted net or woven bag made by communities in Papua and West Papua in Indonesia. UNESCO describes a range of everyday uses: carrying produce, fish, firewood or personal belongings; storing objects at home; and, in some contexts, wearing or giving the bag during ceremonies or as a peace offering.

Calling noken simply a “traditional bag” misses the knowledge held in its making. Fibres are prepared from plant materials, dried and spun into thread, then knotted by hand. The method, materials and finished patterns can vary between communities. The work calls for dexterity and experience, and proficiency can take months to develop.

## Everyday use is part of cultural meaning

The practical and cultural roles of an object are not separate by default. A bag that moves food, tools or other goods through daily life is part of a network of labour and relationships. Its significance may also depend on who made it, how the skill was learned, and the circumstances in which it is given or worn.

Noken should not be treated as a single design that represents every community in Papua. UNESCO's description points to variation in making methods and use. Local practitioners are the essential source for understanding those differences in detail.

## Safeguarding means more than display

UNESCO placed noken on its List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding in 2012. The listing identified challenges including fewer people making and using noken, weakening transmission between generations, competition from factory-made bags and difficulties obtaining traditional raw materials.

These details show why heritage is connected to everyday conditions. Recognition or exhibition can raise awareness, but skills are sustained when people have time to learn, access to materials and reasons to keep practising. Supporting a craft should therefore include the people who make it and the social functions that give it meaning—not only the finished object.

## Read the record with care

This article draws on UNESCO's public documentation. It does not claim to represent all Papuan communities or replace practitioners' own accounts. The source is a useful starting point for understanding the technique and safeguarding concerns, while local voices and more specific regional research are needed for a fuller picture.
