Every year on February 15, communities associated with the John Frum movement on the island of Tanna in Vanuatu mark John Frum Day. The date is not simply a colourful entry on a festival calendar. It belongs to a living religious and political tradition whose history is inseparable from Tanna's encounter with colonial rule, Christianity and the upheavals of the Second World War.

Visitors may encounter organised marches, flags, songs, speeches and displays of collective identity. The details can vary by community and year. Describing the day as a spectacle alone misses what it means to the people who take part: it is also a public affirmation of belonging and of a history told and interpreted locally.

## A movement rooted in Tanna

John Frum is a figure associated with a movement that emerged on Tanna in the early twentieth century. Accounts of its origins and the identity of John Frum differ, and outside descriptions have often simplified the movement. Its adherents have their own understandings, expectations and traditions; there is no single explanation that captures every participant.

The Second World War brought a dramatic military presence to the wider region. The movement's history is often discussed in connection with wartime supplies and the arrival of unfamiliar goods, but that connection should not be treated as a complete explanation of its beliefs. Tanna's political, spiritual and social histories matter too.

## Celebration, not a museum display

John Frum Day makes a tradition visible in public. Yet a festival is not a frozen survival from the past. People enact it in the present, using shared symbols and practices to express commitments that have changed and endured over time.

For outsiders, the most responsible approach is to observe with curiosity while resisting the urge to turn unfamiliar customs into a punchline or a ready-made theory. The people who sustain the celebration are not props in someone else's story about modernity.
