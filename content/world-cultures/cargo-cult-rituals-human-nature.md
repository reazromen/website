The phrase “cargo cult” has often been used to describe Melanesian movements that developed amid colonial encounters and the disruptions of the Second World War. In popular retellings, the label can suggest a simple plot: people see imported goods, imitate the outsiders who possess them, and expect more goods to arrive.

That story is memorable—and incomplete. It risks treating communities as examples of irrational behaviour rather than people responding to profound historical change. To understand ritual, we need to ask not only what an action appears to imitate, but what it means to participants and what it accomplishes in social life.

## Ritual and uncertainty

Anthropologist Dimitris Xygalatas has written about how ritual can shape human behaviour, especially under conditions of uncertainty. Repeated, structured actions may help people manage anxiety, mark important transitions and signal commitment to others. Ritual can also strengthen a sense of shared identity, though its effects depend on the setting and the people involved.

These observations offer a useful lens, not a universal key. They do not prove that any particular John Frum ceremony exists to reduce anxiety or build cohesion. That would require evidence about the practice and the community, not merely a general theory of ritual.

## The observer's blind spot

A practice may look purposeless to someone unfamiliar with its history. But the outsider's first impression is not an explanation. A military-style drill, a procession or a repeated song may carry meanings tied to memory, authority, spiritual expectation or collective belonging. To reduce it to “copying” is to replace inquiry with a stereotype.

The same caution applies to the term “cargo cult” itself. It is a broad label used in different ways, and it can bundle distinct movements into one supposedly exotic category. Historical context and local testimony are essential if we want to understand what people actually believe and do.

## What we can responsibly conclude

Research on ritual suggests that patterned actions can matter to individuals and groups even when their practical outcome is uncertain. It does not license us to diagnose unfamiliar communities from afar. The strongest account combines general insights about human behaviour with the specific history, voices and circumstances of the people concerned.
