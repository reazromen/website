The John Frum movement on Tanna, in Vanuatu, is often introduced to outsiders through its ceremonies: flags, marching, songs and organised gatherings. These visible practices are real, but they are not the whole story. Rituals acquire meaning through the histories and relationships of the people who perform them.

John Frum is a movement with local roots and diverse interpretations. Its adherents' accounts should not be flattened into one formula, nor should every practice be explained as a reaction to foreign goods. The label “cargo cult,” frequently applied by outsiders, can obscure more than it clarifies when it is used as a complete description.

## Repeated actions, shared meanings

Public ceremonies can coordinate a community around a common calendar, symbols and responsibilities. A flag or a march may carry meanings that are not obvious to a visitor. Repetition also makes a tradition recognisable across generations: people learn not only what to do, but when, with whom and in what context.

The historical setting matters. Tanna's communities experienced missionary activity, colonial administration and the disruptions of the Second World War. Those encounters shaped the conditions in which the movement developed. But history does not mechanically determine belief. Local people interpret the past, maintain relationships and make choices in the present.

## Why the “cargo” explanation is too small

Popular accounts sometimes portray movements such as John Frum as people imitating military activity in the hope that manufactured goods will appear. That framing can turn complex religious and political life into a story of naïve imitation.

Material expectations may form part of particular historical accounts, but they do not exhaust the movement's significance. Questions of authority, land, identity, spirituality and relations with outside institutions also belong in the picture. A careful account distinguishes what a source documents from what an observer assumes.

## Looking respectfully

A visitor can notice the choreography of a ceremony without claiming to decode it. The most reliable explanations come from the communities themselves, supported by careful historical work. Cultural practices are not puzzles whose meaning an outsider is entitled to solve from a photograph.
