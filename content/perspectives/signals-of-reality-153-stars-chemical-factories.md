A star looks simple from far away.

A bright sphere.

Inside, nuclear history is being written.

Stars are chemical factories in a loose but useful sense: they transform nuclei, build many heavier elements, and return processed matter to space.

The word *chemical* is slightly imprecise because the core processes are nuclear, not ordinary chemistry.

But their products eventually become chemistry everywhere else.

## Fusion changes identity

Chemistry rearranges electrons around nuclei.

Nuclear reactions change the nuclei themselves.

Hydrogen fusion builds helium.

Later burning stages in sufficiently massive stars produce carbon, oxygen, neon, magnesium, silicon, and other nuclei.

The energy balance changes across the periodic table.

Fusion up to the iron-group region can release energy under appropriate conditions.

Beyond that, other nucleosynthetic processes become essential.

## One star does not make everything

The popular phrase "stars make the elements" compresses a diverse astrophysical story.

Different elements have different major production sites.

Low- and intermediate-mass stars contribute through long-lived evolution and stellar winds.

Massive stars synthesize and eject many elements.

Explosive supernova nucleosynthesis changes abundances further.

Neutron-capture processes create elements heavier than iron, with important contributions from environments including neutron-star mergers and certain stellar explosions.

There is no single cosmic factory line.

There is an ecosystem of nuclear factories.

## Stellar mass determines the production path

A Sun-like star does not evolve like a much more massive star.

Mass controls central temperature, pressure, lifetime, and which nuclear burning stages become possible.

Massive stars live fast.

They reach advanced burning stages.

They can end in core collapse.

Lower-mass stars follow different paths.

The periodic table we inherit is a population product.

Cosmic chemistry depends on many kinds of stars.

## Factories also have waste and exhaust

A factory that makes material but never releases it would not enrich future systems.

Stars lose mass.

Winds carry material outward.

Planetary nebulae return processed gas from lower-mass stars.

Supernovae eject enormous quantities of stellar material.

The interstellar medium becomes enriched.

Next-generation stars form from that mixture.

The galaxy recycles.

## Metallicity is a record of previous stars

Astronomers call elements heavier than helium "metals," a terminology different from chemistry.

A star's metallicity therefore tells us something about how enriched its birth material was.

The earliest stars formed from gas with almost no heavy elements.

Later stars formed from material processed by earlier generations.

Metallicity is a kind of historical metadata.

A star carries evidence about the chemical state of the galaxy when it formed.

## Factories change what later factories can be

Heavy elements influence cooling in gas clouds.

Dust forms.

Star formation changes.

Planet formation becomes possible in richer ways.

Stellar nucleosynthesis therefore does not merely produce ingredients.

It changes the conditions under which future stars and planetary systems form.

Cosmic evolution has feedback.

## The metaphor stops at intention

Factories have production goals.

Stars do not.

They are gravitationally confined plasmas following nuclear physics.

The elements are consequences of stellar evolution, not products made for a customer.

Still, the metaphor captures transformation.

Stars take a universe born chemically simple and make it progressively richer.

The calcium in bone, oxygen in water, carbon in cells, and iron in blood belong to nuclear histories that began long before biology.

Stars are chemical factories only metaphorically.

But the inventory they leave behind is real.
