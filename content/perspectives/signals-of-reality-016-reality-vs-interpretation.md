Imagine two thermometers in a room. One sits on a desk, the other beside a sunlit window. Their displays disagree. Someone calls the first reading the real temperature. Someone else prefers the second. A third person concludes that temperature is subjective.

The third answer sounds sophisticated, but it arrives too soon. Before deciding that reality has split into perspectives, we should ask what each instrument is measuring.

The temperature of which object, at which location, over which interval? Is sunlight warming the sensor? Has either instrument had time to settle after being moved? Are their readings consistent when placed together under comparable conditions? These questions do not banish interpretation. They make it more exact.

The room contains physical processes that do not consult our preferences. Our statements about those processes nevertheless require choices. Confusing those two facts creates a false contest: either knowledge gives us reality without mediation, or every account is merely somebody's version.

There is a more demanding position between them. Interpretations can be indispensable and answerable to evidence at the same time.

## What the number is about

Measurement begins with a target. Metrologists call the quantity intended to be measured the *measurand*. Specifying it can require more than naming a property. “Temperature” leaves open whose temperature and under what conditions. The [International Vocabulary of Metrology](https://jcgm.bipm.org/vim/en/2.3.html) makes this specificity part of measurement itself.

In our imagined room, two readings might differ because the instruments have different errors. They might also differ because they are responding to different local conditions. Those possibilities call for different investigations. Averaging the displays before asking which possibility applies could produce a tidy number that answers no useful question.

Suppose the practical question concerns whether a particular object on the desk is warming. The window reading may be less relevant even if its thermometer is excellent. If the question instead concerns conditions beside the glass, the ranking could reverse. Relevance depends on the question; accuracy depends on how well a result represents its specified target.

That distinction prevents a common slide. Choosing a question does not give us permission to choose its answer.

Nor does stating an uncertainty make the result optional. Measurement uncertainty characterizes the dispersion of values reasonably attributed to the target on the available information. It is part of the result's meaning, not a confession that any number will do. The [metrological definition](https://jcgm.bipm.org/vim/en/2.26.html) imposes discipline on what may otherwise become a vague gesture toward doubt.

## Where interpretation enters

Even a display reading involves a small network of commitments. We assume the device is responding to the intended property, that its calibration is applicable, and that the units mean what we think they mean. If the display changes, we decide whether to treat that change as physical variation, instrument drift, or noise.

In science, this dependence on background understanding is often discussed as the theory-ladenness of observation. The term need not mean that researchers see only what their favorite theory allows. It means, among other things, that turning an instrument's output into evidence requires knowledge of how the instrument and its target interact. The philosophical discussion in [“Theory and Observation in Science”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/science-theory-observation/) explains why a clean separation between theory and observation is difficult to sustain.

There is a danger here, but it is specific. If an instrument is interpreted using the very assumption that the instrument is supposed to test, apparent confirmation may be circular. The remedy is to examine that dependence: test parts of the measurement process separately, use other methods, or identify results that the favored account would struggle to accommodate.

For the thermometers, putting them side by side is informative because it changes the situation in a controlled way. If their disagreement follows the location when the instruments are exchanged, location deserves attention. If it follows one device, the device deserves attention. Real investigations can be messier: several effects may coexist. Still, the comparison gives the rival accounts different consequences.

An interpretation becomes stronger when it survives opportunities to be wrong.

This does not require a final observation free of all assumptions. It requires checks whose weaknesses do not simply duplicate the weakness under investigation. Two thermometers calibrated against the same faulty reference may agree beautifully. Agreement matters most when we understand how it could have been produced.

## A disagreement can contain several disagreements

Now suppose the room's occupants agree about every reading but still disagree about whether it is too warm.

No additional decimal place necessarily resolves that dispute. “Too warm” can combine a factual claim about temperature, a person's bodily experience, and a judgment about what conditions should be provided. People may have different needs. The disagreement is partly about how to act on the measurements.

It would be a mistake to dismiss the bodily experience because it is personal. It would also be a mistake to treat that experience as a direct replacement for the specified temperature measurement. They answer different questions and can both supply relevant information.

This is why the familiar instruction to separate facts from interpretations needs care. A fact is not always a raw item waiting beneath a removable coating of opinion. Some factual claims are established through extensive interpretation. Meanwhile, some disputes presented as factual are really disagreements about definitions, relevance, or acceptable consequences.

An effective conversation makes the transition points visible. Here is the reading. Here is why we think the reading tracks the intended quantity. Here is the conclusion we draw from it. Here is the practical standard we use to decide whether something should change. Each step can be examined without pretending that all steps are the same kind of claim.

This structure also leaves room for partial agreement. We might be confident that one side of the room is warmer while remaining unsure why. We might establish a cause while disagreeing about the best remedy. Knowledge need not arrive as a single package that is either accepted whole or discarded.

The thermometer example has limits. A disputed historical event cannot usually be rearranged for a controlled comparison. An interpretation of a poem need not yield a unique measurable prediction. The transferable lesson is narrower: identify the claim being made and ask what could reasonably support or weaken it. Different subjects permit different tests.

For empirical claims, reality is not a competing opinion at the table. It is what makes some investigations succeed and others fail, sometimes after everyone has agreed on the wrong explanation.

Return to the room. Leave the two numbers on the page. Add the locations, the times, the instrument details, and the question being asked. The disagreement has not disappeared. It has become an investigation someone else can continue.
