# The Saudi-led Intervention in Yemen: Air Power, Regional Rivalry, and Civilian Protection

The Saudi-led intervention began in March 2015, when a coalition led by Saudi Arabia launched military operations against Houthi forces advancing through Yemen. The coalition said it was acting at the request of Yemen’s internationally recognized president and sought to restore his government. The intervention became a major external layer of Yemen’s civil war, involving air operations, maritime measures, support to local forces and, at various points, direct ground involvement.

## Strategic aims and regional context

Saudi Arabia viewed Houthi expansion and the movement’s relationship with Iran as a threat near its southern border. The Houthis, however, are a Yemeni movement with a history of domestic grievances and political-military ambitions; describing the war solely as Saudi Arabia versus Iran obscures local actors and the collapse of Yemen’s transition. Coalition members also had differing priorities, and their relationships with Yemeni factions were not always aligned.

The intervention relied heavily on air power and support for allied forces. Airstrikes hit military positions but also damaged or destroyed civilian infrastructure in multiple documented incidents. The coalition disputed responsibility or the circumstances of some attacks and said it targeted military objectives. Such claims must be assessed against independent investigations, target information and the laws governing distinction, proportionality and precautions in attack.

## Civilian harm and accountability

UN bodies and humanitarian organizations documented extensive civilian casualties, damage to hospitals, schools, roads and water systems, and constraints affecting food and fuel imports. Houthi forces and other Yemeni actors also committed abuses, including attacks, detention and restrictions on humanitarian activity. Recognizing abuses by multiple parties does not make responsibility interchangeable: each incident needs its own evidence and attribution.

Casualty reporting must distinguish deaths directly caused by violence from indirect mortality linked to hunger, disease and the breakdown of services. Both are important, but combining them without explanation creates misleading comparisons. Investigations and public reporting should identify the time period, methodology and uncertainty of each estimate.

## Truces and regional escalation

The 2022 UN-mediated truce reduced some fighting even after its formal expiration, though violence and military preparations continued in different forms. From late 2023, Houthi attacks on commercial and military shipping in the Red Sea and strikes by the United States, United Kingdom and other actors opened a related regional theatre. These operations should be cross-linked to Yemen’s war but tracked separately from the coalition’s 2015 intervention.

## What accountability requires

Accountability includes transparent investigation of civilian harm, preservation of evidence, access for humanitarian organizations, remedies for victims and credible legal review. A political settlement also requires security arrangements and reconstruction funding that do not leave communities dependent on armed patrons.
