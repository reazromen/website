Every morning, the streetlights turn off near sunrise.

Watch for a year and the relationship remains.

More repetition makes the association undeniable.

It still does not show that sunrise directly sends a command to the streetlights.

They may be controlled by a timer.

They may use a light sensor.

A central controller may combine both.

Repetition strengthens the claim that two events are related in time.

It does not, by itself, tell us the causal wiring.

## Replication and causation solve different problems

Replication asks whether an observation can be obtained again.

Causal inference asks why the observation occurs.

The two support one another but should not be confused.

If an association disappears on replication, a causal story built on it becomes weak.

If the association replicates perfectly, several causal stories can still remain possible.

Repeatability removes some kinds of accident.

It does not remove confounding.

## Periodic systems create endless correlations

Many variables share clocks.

Temperature, electricity use, traffic, human activity, sunlight, and network load can all have daily cycles.

Compare enough daily variables and many will correlate strongly.

The shared periodicity can make one look predictive of another.

The correct explanation may be a common driver: time of day.

The same issue appears with seasonal cycles, maintenance schedules, orbital periods, and weekly routines.

A repeating world creates repeating correlations.

## Repetition can make a false explanation feel inevitable

The mind becomes comfortable with a sequence.

A happens.

Then B.

Again.

Again.

Soon the order feels causal.

This is psychologically understandable because repeated prediction is useful.

If B follows A reliably, A can be a practical warning signal even when the mechanism is unknown.

Operational prediction and causal explanation are not identical.

A pressure drop may reliably precede a machine failure without causing it. Both may result from a third process degrading inside the machine.

The pressure signal can still be useful.

## Break the pattern deliberately

The strongest test is often to change one part of the sequence.

If a scheduled job seems to cause a latency spike, move the job.

If a temperature cycle seems responsible, control temperature independently.

If one software component appears causal, replace or disable it in a safe test environment.

A causal model predicts what should happen after intervention.

A merely associated clock may not.

Intervention turns repeated observation into a sharper experiment.

## Natural experiments can help

Sometimes we cannot manipulate the system directly.

Astronomy cannot move planets for a control trial.

Climate science cannot create a second Earth.

Researchers then look for naturally occurring changes that separate variables which usually move together.

A timing shift, environmental discontinuity, instrument orientation, eclipse, or other event can act like a partial intervention.

The logic remains the same: find conditions under which competing explanations predict different outcomes.

## Repetition still matters

None of this makes repetition unimportant.

A result that appears once is fragile.

A result that appears across times, places, instruments, and teams is harder to explain as a one-off artifact.

Replication can reveal boundary conditions.

It can show that an effect depends on one environment.

It can expose hidden variability.

Repetition is one of science's strongest filters against accident.

It simply filters a different question than causation.

## Ask what would happen if we changed it

When A and B repeat together, the causal question is:

If we changed A while leaving the relevant rest of the system comparable, would B change?

That counterfactual is what repeated observation alone cannot answer.

A repeated pattern is evidence.

A mechanism is an explanation.

Causation connects them by saying which changes would produce which consequences.

Repetition can make the clue stronger.

The arrow still has to be tested.
