# The ELN Insurgency in Colombia: A Separate Armed Campaign Within a Wider Conflict

The National Liberation Army (Ejército de Liberación Nacional, ELN) is a long-running Colombian guerrilla organization whose armed campaign has continued through major changes in the country’s wider conflict. Although the ELN and the former FARC have sometimes operated in the same regions and faced the same state forces, they are distinct organizations with separate leadership, histories and negotiating strategies.

## Origins and organization

The ELN emerged in the 1960s and developed a political identity influenced by revolutionary and left-wing currents. Over time, its regional fronts acquired differing local relationships and operational patterns. Its command structure and territorial presence have changed, and negotiations with the government have repeatedly opened, stalled or broken down. The organization should not be described as a static force with identical capabilities across all decades.

## Negotiations and renewed confrontation

Colombian governments have pursued talks with the ELN at different times, often alongside military pressure and local humanitarian initiatives. Negotiations have faced disputes over ceasefires, attacks, kidnappings, verification and the conditions for moving from exploratory dialogue to a comprehensive settlement. A temporary ceasefire announcement is not equivalent to a final peace agreement, and its terms must be checked for duration, geographic reach and compliance.

The ELN’s decisions are analytically separate from the FARC’s 2016 agreement. That accord changed the FARC’s organizational status, but did not bind the ELN. After the FARC demobilization, competition among armed actors for influence in some former FARC areas contributed to new patterns of confrontation. The actors involved and the sequence of events differ by locality.

## Civilians and territorial control

The ELN campaign has affected civilians through combat, kidnapping, threats, recruitment, movement restrictions and the use or presence of explosive devices. In some areas, armed competition has disrupted schooling, commerce and access to health services. Indigenous and Afro-Colombian communities may face layered risks where armed groups contest territory and where state services are limited.

Claims about responsibility require careful sourcing. An attack reported in an area where the ELN operates should not automatically be attributed to it. Official statements, armed-group communiqués, local reporting and human-rights documentation have different evidentiary limits and should be cross-checked. Civilian harm and alleged violations must be recorded separately from combat between organized armed parties.

## How to represent the ELN in the archive

The ELN should have its own actor-specific article linked to the broader Colombian conflict. It must not be double-counted as a separate war in every year or province where it is active. UCDP conflict-year and dyadic records can help establish qualifying relationships with the state; event-level and one-sided violence data are needed for other patterns. Source labels and actor aliases should be normalized before comparing records across years.

Casualty totals must identify whether they count combat deaths, civilians killed, missing persons or other categories. Displacement and coercion are important consequences but are not interchangeable with battle-related deaths. For 2026, the archive should distinguish verified historical data through 2025 from provisional current-event reporting.
