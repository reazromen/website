# Libya’s Civil Wars and Rival Governments, 2011 Onward

Libya’s armed conflict after 2011 is best understood as a sequence of overlapping phases. The uprising that toppled Muammar Gaddafi, the fragmentation of state institutions, the renewed civil war beginning in 2014, and the 2019–2020 battle for Tripoli were connected, but they involved changing coalitions and political objectives. Treating every episode as one unchanged war obscures who fought, when the balance of power shifted and why ceasefires repeatedly proved fragile.

## From uprising to fragmented security

The 2011 uprising and international intervention ended Gaddafi’s rule but left Libya without a durable, unified security sector. Revolutionary brigades and local armed groups retained weapons and influence. Rival political institutions competed over legitimacy, resources and control of state facilities. Local disputes could escalate into organized clashes, while armed factions sometimes entered formal state structures without losing their independent chains of command.

## Renewed civil war and competing centres of power

In 2014, fighting intensified between rival coalitions, including forces aligned with the House of Representatives and Khalifa Haftar’s Libyan National Army, and forces associated with western Libya and competing political institutions. The conflict was not a simple division between two unified camps: local commanders, city-based groups and political allies changed relationships over time. Foreign states supplied different forms of political, military or material support, internationalizing the conflict further.

The 2019 offensive against Tripoli opened another major phase. Forces aligned with Haftar attempted to take the capital, while western-based forces defended it with support from external partners. The fighting involved air power, drones, artillery and foreign personnel, increasing risks to urban residents and critical infrastructure. A ceasefire in 2020 reduced large-scale front-line fighting, but it did not resolve the division of political authority or the proliferation of armed groups.

## Civilian impact and accountability

Civilians have faced displacement, explosive remnants of war, damage to hospitals and infrastructure, arbitrary detention, and abuses by armed actors. Migrants and refugees in Libya have faced particular risks in detention and along migration routes. These harms should be documented with specific dates and sources; general insecurity does not prove that every incident belongs to a single organized campaign.

## How the archive classifies Libya

This entry links the 2011 uprising, the 2014–2020 civil-war phases and subsequent localized clashes while preserving separate timelines. The archive should distinguish the main state-based conflict from non-state clashes and one-sided violence against civilians. A ceasefire is not the same as a comprehensive peace settlement, and political division alone is not evidence of active fighting in every location.
