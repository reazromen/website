# Colombia’s Armed Conflict Since 2000: Insurgency, Paramilitaries, and an Uneven Peace

Colombia’s armed conflict entered the twenty-first century with guerrilla organizations, paramilitary formations, state security forces and communities caught between them. The conflict’s roots stretch back decades, but its post-2000 history is not a single unchanging war. Military offensives, paramilitary demobilization, the 2016 peace agreement with the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC), and the continued activity of other armed organizations have repeatedly changed the map of violence.

## A conflict with several armed systems

The principal actors have included the Colombian state, the FARC before its demobilization process, the National Liberation Army (ELN), paramilitary organizations and successor or successor-like armed networks. These organizations differed in ideology, command structures, territorial reach and relationships with illicit economies. Local communities could face pressure from multiple actors, and control of roads, rural settlements, coca-growing areas, mining sites and transport corridors often mattered as much as national political declarations.

The United Self-Defence Forces of Colombia (AUC) demobilized in the mid-2000s, but demobilization did not remove all armed networks or the conditions that supported them. Some former members entered civilian life; other networks fragmented or reconstituted. The label “paramilitary successor” therefore needs a dated, actor-specific explanation rather than being applied indiscriminately to every criminal organization.

## The 2016 peace agreement and what followed

The 2016 agreement between the government and the FARC created a major transition: the FARC’s principal organization disarmed and entered electoral politics. Implementation involved reintegration, rural reform, political participation, illicit-crop policy and guarantees for former combatants and community leaders. These provisions have faced uneven implementation and local security challenges.

Some former FARC members rejected the agreement or later rearmed, creating several dissident structures. They should not be treated as one unified organization: leadership, alliances and operational presence vary by region and over time. Meanwhile, the ELN continued its own campaign, and other armed groups competed for territory and revenue. A peace agreement with one party can transform a conflict without ending all organized violence.

## Civilian impact and evidence

Civilians have endured displacement, confinement, threats, recruitment, land dispossession, attacks and restrictions on movement. Indigenous and Afro-Colombian communities have faced particular risks in some territories. Social leaders and people involved in land restitution, environmental advocacy or implementation of the peace accord have also been targeted. The causes and perpetrators differ by incident; attribution must be based on evidence, not assumptions about a community’s politics.

Casualty and displacement totals require careful handling. A figure may cover a specific period, a subset of actors or a particular legal category. The archive should record the source, date range and definition for each figure rather than combine incompatible totals.

## Classification for the archive

The Colombian conflict is best represented as an umbrella chronology with separate actor- and period-specific records. FARC-era conflict, post-agreement dissident activity, the ELN campaign and non-state armed violence can overlap geographically without being interchangeable. UCDP state-based and dyadic data can identify qualifying conflict relationships and years; non-state conflict and one-sided violence data address different categories. None alone captures all displacement, coercion or criminal governance.

For 2026, reports should remain provisional until event records, actor attribution and inclusion criteria are checked. High levels of organized crime or homicide are not, by themselves, proof that every incident belongs to an armed conflict.
