Put four things on a table: a stone, a bank statement, a photograph of a dead star and a laptop running a virtual machine. Which of them is real?

The stone seems easy. It has mass, occupies space and can break your toe. The bank balance is stranger. There is no pile of "balance" sitting inside a server, yet the number constrains what you can buy. The photograph is made of pixels on a screen, but the light from which the astronomical data were derived may have crossed space for millions of years. The virtual machine is software, but it can run a database, consume memory, fail, recover and generate a bill.

Ordinary language asks one word—real—to do several jobs.

That is why arguments about reality sometimes become confused before they become difficult. Two people can disagree loudly while using different tests for what counts as real.

### Physical does not mean directly visible

One common test is physical existence. Electrons pass it even though we do not see individual electrons with unaided eyes. Their reality is supported by a large network of reproducible effects and successful theories. Gravitational waves were inferred from a particular pattern in LIGO's detectors; nobody had to point to a wave as one points to a chair. The evidence was mediated by instruments, calibration and analysis. It was not therefore fictional.

Direct visibility is a poor boundary for reality. Most of the electromagnetic spectrum is invisible to human eyes. Neutrinos can pass through enormous amounts of matter with little interaction. Atoms were scientifically useful explanatory entities before imaging techniques gave us modern ways of representing structures at atomic scales.

The distinction worth preserving is not "seen versus unreal." It is **what evidence licenses the claim?**

### Social things are not imaginary things

Now consider money. A currency depends on human institutions, conventions and enforcement. If every relevant institution disappeared, a bank balance would not continue operating like a mountain.

Does that make money unreal?

Only if real has been defined to mean "independent of all human practices." But that definition throws away too much. Contracts, languages, borders, corporations and universities are not geological objects, yet they produce consequences that can be studied. A court ruling can transfer property. A change in an interest rate can alter borrowing. A password policy can prevent a person from accessing a system.

Philosophers often distinguish kinds or levels of dependence rather than forcing all entities through one test. Social ontology asks how facts about institutions can depend on collective rules and practices while remaining more than individual fantasy.

The difference between "socially constructed" and "made up" matters. A chess rule is constructed. During a game, it genuinely constrains legal moves. You cannot refute the rule by discovering that wood contains no molecules called checkmate.

### A simulation can contain real events

Virtual systems create another useful case.

Suppose a virtual machine runs out of memory and a database process is killed. The machine is implemented through layers of software and hardware. Its memory addresses are not tiny physical boxes arranged exactly as the guest operating system depicts them. Yet the failure is real enough to lose a transaction.

The event is real **at the level at which the system is organized**.

This does not mean virtual and physical descriptions are interchangeable. A software fault and a burned voltage regulator require different explanations even if either can make an application disappear. The point is that dependence on a lower layer does not automatically make a higher-level phenomenon unreal.

Temperature offers an older scientific example. A gas has a temperature even though an individual molecule does not carry a miniature thermometer reading. Temperature is associated with statistical properties of many particles. Calling it an emergent quantity does not demote it to illusion. It tells us what kind of description it belongs to.

### Experiences complicate the word again

A dream of a tiger is not a tiger in the room. But the experience of dreaming is still an event in a person's life and nervous system.

Pain makes the distinction sharper. A person's pain need not correspond to visible tissue damage in a simple one-to-one way for the pain to be real as an experience. Conversely, sincerely experiencing something does not establish that the external object imagined in the experience exists.

So two propositions can be separated:

1. The experience occurred.
2. The experience accurately represents an external object or event.

Confusing them produces bad arguments in both directions. Treating all experiences as reliable photographs of the world overstates perception. Treating an experience as unreal because its interpretation is mistaken erases the phenomenon we were trying to explain.

### "Real" needs a predicate

When technical discussions stall over the word, it helps to make the sentence longer.

Instead of asking, "Is this real?" ask whether it exists independently of this particular observer, whether it is physically instantiated, institutionally maintained, measurable through repeatable procedures, causally consequential, accurately represented, and at what level of description it exists.

These questions can produce different answers without contradiction.

A city on a paper map is not physically located inside the sheet. The city is real; the ink is real; the spatial relationship represented by the map can be accurate; and the drawing is still not the territory.

This vocabulary also protects us from a fashionable mistake: assuming that because human knowledge is mediated by models and instruments, reality itself is only a model. That conclusion does not follow. An instrument can be fallible precisely because there is a difference between what it reports and what actually happened.

Scientific realism and its critics disagree over how far successful theories justify belief in unobservable entities and structures. The debate is serious because success, evidence and representation are complicated. It is not a debate between "everything is obviously real" and "anything can be true."

Perhaps real is less useful as a gate than as the beginning of an investigation.

The stone, the balance, the virtual machine and the astronomical image do not have to be real in the same way.

What matters is being able to say **which claim we are making, what would support it, and what kind of thing the claim is about**.
