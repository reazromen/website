Two fabric samples can match in a shop and disagree beside a window. Neither needs to have faded during the walk. The new illumination can expose a difference that the old illumination concealed.

For a buyer, this is a nuisance. For a theory of color, it is an excellent constraint. Any account that treats a perceived color as a fixed substance stored inside the fabric has to explain why the match depends on the lamp. Any account that treats color as an arbitrary invention of the mind has to explain why the change is predictable from physical measurements.

A useful explanation must keep both obligations.

### A match is a compressed agreement

A surface sends a distribution of wavelengths toward the eye. For a simple matte, nonfluorescent sample, the outgoing spectrum depends on both the spectrum illuminating it and the fraction of light it reflects at each wavelength. Geometry matters too: where the lamp is, how the surface is oriented, and where we stand.

The eye does not retain a separate reading for every wavelength. In ordinary daylight color vision, three classes of cone provide overlapping spectral sensitivities. Their responses compress the incoming spectrum into three initial channels. Subsequent neural processing compares and transforms those signals. The cones are often called red, green and blue receptors, but these names invite the mistaken picture of three miniature color meters. Each class responds across a range of wavelengths. [The neuroscience account of cones and color vision](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK11059/) explains the biological basis of trichromacy.

The compression has a consequence: different spectra can generate the same color match for a particular observer under particular conditions. Such stimuli are called metamers. Agreement in appearance does not require identity in spectral composition.

Imagine two simplified reflectance curves. One sample returns light fairly evenly through a band; another returns more at some wavelengths and less at others. Under one illuminant, those differences can balance in the observer’s three responses. Change the illumination and the balance can fail. This is a conceptual example, not a measurement of particular fabrics. It explains why the shop-to-window transition can uncover a mismatch without any alteration of the dyes.

Metamerism is sufficiently practical that standards distinguish mismatches caused by changing the illuminant from those caused by changing the observer. [The CIE’s definition of a metamerism index](https://www.cie.co.at/eilvterm/17-23-007) makes both dependencies explicit.

The point reaches beyond shopping. A successful reproduction does not always preserve the physical signal. A display can make a patch that resembles a painted surface while emitting a different spectrum. That success is real, but it is a success against specified perceptual criteria. If the task were to identify the pigment chemically, a visual match would be inadequate evidence.

### The visual system also discounts change

The fabric example shows that illumination can disrupt a match. Everyday vision also displays the opposite tendency: surfaces often retain a fairly stable appearance despite changes in the light falling on them.

A sheet of paper does not ordinarily become a new kind of object every time a cloud passes. Our visual system uses adaptation and relationships within the scene to maintain some stability. Color constancy is the name for this family of effects, not a promise of perfect invariance.

There is a difficult inference underneath it. A change in the spectrum arriving from a surface might reflect a different lamp, a different surface, or both. One isolated patch cannot uniquely settle the issue. Surrounding surfaces, spatial boundaries and the wider illumination pattern provide additional constraints. A research review by David Foster and colleagues discusses sensory, computational and cognitive contributions without reducing constancy to a single universal trick. [Their review is available here](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1609194/).

It is tempting to describe this as the brain “correcting the lighting.” That analogy is useful if it means the visual system achieves partial stability across changing conditions. It becomes misleading if it suggests that the brain knows the lamp’s exact spectrum, recovers the true reflectance of every surface, and then paints an infallible inner image. The inference is limited by the evidence available.

A practical consequence follows. Two claims that sound contradictory can both be accurate: the light from a surface changed substantially, and the surface looked approximately the same. The first concerns a measured spectrum; the second concerns appearance in context. Color discussion goes wrong when those are treated as interchangeable reports.

### Objectivity needs conditions

Manufacturers still have to decide whether two batches match. “Everyone sees differently” would be a useless answer, and color science does not stop there.

Colorimetry specifies conditions under which comparisons can be calculated and reproduced. Standard illuminants and standard colorimetric observers provide shared reference points; illuminating and viewing geometry also matter. A standard observer is a mathematical reference based on human color matching, not a claim that every person has identical vision. [The CIE’s Colorimetry report](https://www.cie.co.at/publications/colorimetry-4th-edition) sets out this framework.

Such a measurement answers a bounded question. It can support a statement that two samples fall within an agreed tolerance under the specified method. It does not establish that no observer could distinguish them under any conceivable light. That limitation is the reason to state the method clearly, rather than a reason to abandon measurement.

We can separate three questions that ordinary speech packs into “What color is it?” What wavelengths does the surface reflect? What colorimetric coordinates follow under a chosen reference condition? What appearance does a person experience in this scene? These questions are related, but one answer cannot silently substitute for all three.

There remains a philosophical dispute about what color itself should be identified with. Some accounts connect colors to physical surface properties; others emphasize dispositions to produce experiences or relations involving observers. The scientific dependencies constrain those positions without automatically selecting one. [The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy surveys the competing theories](https://plato.stanford.edu/archIves/fall2024/entries/color/).

None of this forces us to stop calling a coat blue. Everyday color language usually carries background assumptions about viewing conditions and human observers. The trouble begins when those assumptions disappear from a technical argument while their conclusions remain.

The two fabrics at the window have not exposed a fraud in perception. They have exposed the terms of an agreement. Under the shop’s light they matched; under daylight they did not. With spectra, viewing conditions and an observer model, we can investigate why. A color judgment becomes more useful when we know what would make it change.
