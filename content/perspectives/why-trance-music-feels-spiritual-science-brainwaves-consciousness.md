A steady kick drum, a bassline that seems to pulse through the body, and a synthesiser pattern returning again and again can make an ordinary listening session feel unusually absorbing. Some listeners describe the experience as hypnotic; others talk about unity, emotional release or something they regard as spiritual. The sensation is real as an experience, but what produces it is a more complicated question than the popular idea that a particular sound frequency can unlock consciousness.

The science points towards an interaction among rhythm, prediction, movement, attention, emotion and cultural expectation. Trance music does not need a supernatural frequency to affect the listener. Its musical structure already works with systems the human brain uses to organise time and anticipate what comes next.

## Frequency is not the same as a brainwave

Discussions of trance music often mix together three different meanings of frequency. Acoustic frequency describes the vibration rate of a sound wave and is measured in hertz. Musical tempo describes how often beats occur, usually in beats per minute. Brainwave frequency refers to rhythmic patterns of electrical activity measured with electroencephalography, or EEG.

These are not interchangeable. A track playing at 138 beats per minute has a quarter-note beat rate of 2.3 cycles per second. If its rhythm is subdivided into sixteenth notes, those events occur at 9.2 per second. The number happens to fall within the commonly used alpha EEG range, but numerical coincidence does not demonstrate that the music has pushed the listener's brain into an alpha state. That would require direct measurement, and neural activity is not governed by a single frequency at a time.

The sound itself occupies a much wider spectrum. A producer may place sub-bass energy in the low tens of hertz, give the kick and bass most of the low-frequency weight, and build the melodic and atmospheric layers through the midrange and treble. The exact distribution depends on the sound design, notes, samples and mixing choices. What matters to the listener is not one isolated number, but how these layers combine into a recognisable and evolving pattern.

## Why repetition can become absorbing

The brain is constantly predicting what it is likely to hear next. A steady four-to-the-floor kick gives it a reliable temporal reference, while bass patterns, hi-hats and arpeggios add movement around that foundation. When the arrangement repeats but changes gradually, the listener can recognise the pattern without knowing exactly how it will develop.

That balance between expectation and variation is central to musical pleasure. A breakdown can feel powerful because it interrupts a structure the listener has learned; a drop can feel satisfying because it restores the pulse after a period of tension. Repetition therefore does not simply make music monotonous. Used carefully, it creates a framework in which small changes become perceptible and emotionally meaningful.

Researchers use the term *neural entrainment* to describe the alignment of neural activity with a regular external rhythm. It is one possible mechanism through which the brain tracks musical beats. Rhythm processing also involves systems associated with hearing, movement preparation and timing, which helps explain why people often begin tapping, nodding or dancing before they consciously decide to move.

A 2024 review in *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* describes musical groove as the pleasurable urge to move with music and examines the psychological and neural processes that support it. The phenomenon is not confined to electronic music, but trance makes extensive use of the stable pulse and gradual variation that can sustain it.

## What an EEG experiment found

A particularly relevant experiment appeared in *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* in April 2025. In *The strength of neural entrainment to electronic music correlates with proxies of altered states of consciousness*, researchers recorded EEG activity while 19 participants listened to six one-minute electronic music excerpts.

The excerpts represented three beat rates: 1.65 Hz, equivalent to 99 beats per minute; 2.25 Hz, or 135 beats per minute; and 2.85 Hz, or 171 beats per minute. Neural entrainment was stronger at 1.65 Hz than at 2.85 Hz. Participants also reported a higher feeling of unity after the slower condition than after the fastest one.

The qualifications are as important as the findings. The researchers found no significant tempo-related difference in the participants' scores for spiritual experience or disembodiment, and they did not find a significant individual-level relationship between the strength of entrainment and reported subjective experiences. The experiment also used short laboratory listening sessions, not a dance floor, and participants were not moving in synchrony.

It is therefore evidence that beat rate can influence measurable neural responses and may relate to particular aspects of subjective experience. It is not proof that 99 BPM, or any other tempo, induces spiritual awakening. Nor does it establish a universal formula for trance.

## The bass is heard and felt

Trance is not only an auditory experience. Low-frequency sound can also be felt as vibration when the sound level and playback system make that possible. In two experiments reported in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, researchers found that presenting high-groove music through both auditory and tactile channels increased reported enjoyment and groove, and influenced body movement.

This helps explain why a powerful sound system can change the character of electronic music. The kick and bass provide physical as well as musical information, while the steady beat gives the body a predictable structure for movement. The experience may become more immersive, but louder is not automatically better: excessive sound exposure can damage hearing.

## Why people connect trance with spirituality

Repetitive sound and rhythmic movement long predate electronic dance music. Chanting, devotional repetition and ceremonial drumming appear in different cultural traditions, although their meanings and purposes are not interchangeable. A systematic review published in *Wiley Interdisciplinary Reviews: Cognitive Science* in December 2025 examined 24 neuroimaging studies of chanting, mantra and repetitive prayer. It reported activity in brain regions associated with attention and emotional regulation, alongside findings of reduced activity in some areas linked to self-referential thought. The authors also stressed that differences among the studies prevented a formal meta-analysis.

Modern Goa trance and psychedelic trance developed a distinct cultural relationship with ecstatic dance, alternative communities and ideas of self-transcendence. Anthropologist Graham St John traced this culture to seasonal dance-party scenes that evolved in Goa during the 1970s and 1980s, before Goa trance and its wider psychedelic culture emerged in the 1990s. The spiritual language surrounding the scene is therefore partly cultural and historical, not simply a claim about acoustics.

A July 2026 review in *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, *The rhythms of trance: Cultural phenomenology and neural mechanisms of music-induced non-ordinary states of consciousness*, brings together cultural and neuroscientific perspectives. It argues that music can provide temporal structure for trance experiences across cultures, while highlighting possible changes in neural networks and self-related processing. The authors also call for more rigorous interdisciplinary research. The review does not reduce trance to a single brainwave or beat rate.

## What about 432 Hz and 528 Hz?

The reference pitch used for standard musical tuning is A4 at 440 Hz, as specified by ISO 16. Tuning an entire recording so that A4 is 432 Hz shifts the musical pitch; it does not mean that every sound in the recording vibrates at 432 Hz. A small 2019 double-blind crossover pilot study involving 33 volunteers compared music tuned to 432 Hz and 440 Hz. It reported a modest difference in heart rate, but the blood-pressure changes were not statistically significant and the authors called for larger studies.

That result is not evidence that 432 Hz is a universal healing frequency. Claims that 528 Hz repairs DNA or reliably produces spiritual transformation are not supported by robust, replicated clinical evidence. A listener may prefer one tuning because of its sound or because of the context in which it is heard, but preference should not be mistaken for a proven biological mechanism.

## The experience is larger than the frequency

Trance music can feel spiritual because it brings together several powerful elements: a predictable pulse, carefully managed variation, bodily movement, bass vibration, emotional anticipation and a social setting in which people may experience themselves as part of a group. For some listeners, that combination becomes a meaningful experience of unity or transcendence. For others, it is simply an absorbing form of dance music.

Neuroscience is beginning to identify some of the mechanisms involved, but the available studies remain limited and do not support a magical-frequency explanation. The more interesting possibility is also the more grounded one: a well-constructed rhythm can engage the brain's systems for timing, prediction and movement so effectively that music changes not only what a person hears, but how that person experiences the moment.
