Put a steel rod on a bench and ask two laboratories to report its length. The request sounds complete. It is not.

Length at what temperature? Between which surfaces? Along which axis? With what force applied by the measuring instrument? A rough end and a rounded end do not offer the same obvious boundary. A rod that is warming during the measurement is not presenting a single, unchanging target.

The rod does not become unreal because these questions exist. The questions specify which fact about the rod the laboratories are trying to establish.

Metrology, the science of measurement, has a word for the quantity intended to be measured: the *measurand*. Specifying it can require conditions and a description of the object, not merely a noun such as “length.” That is the point of the [International Vocabulary of Metrology's definition](https://jcgm.bipm.org/vim/en/2.3.html).

### First, decide what the number is about

For an illustrative case, define the target as the distance between two identified end faces along the rod's axis at a reference temperature. This is a choice, but it is not an arbitrary answer. Once the question is specified, the material object constrains the result.

An engineer interested in whether the rod fits a hot assembly might need a different target. A laboratory characterizing the rod at a reference temperature could produce an excellent measurement that does not answer that engineer's immediate question. Neither party has to be careless for the number to travel badly.

This is a common source of trouble with apparently objective figures. The number survives copying; the definition attached to it does not. A result loses its conditions in a spreadsheet, a report or a conversation, then acquires a broader meaning than the measurement supports.

Notice what would resolve the disagreement. It would not be a declaration that both laboratories have their own reality. It would be a shared specification of what they intend to measure, followed by an examination of whether their methods can measure it.

### Then, distinguish the display from the result

A device can show many digits without justifying all of them. The display is one part of the chain. Calibration, repeatability, alignment and environmental conditions affect what can responsibly be inferred from it.

Suppose a hypothetical report gives a rod's length as 100.00 millimetres with a standard uncertainty of 0.04 millimetres. Another gives 100.03 millimetres with a standard uncertainty of 0.05 millimetres. Those invented values illustrate a calculation; they are not experimental data.

Under the simplifying assumption that the uncertainty contributions in the two results are independent, the standard uncertainty of their difference is the square root of the sum of the squared uncertainties: about 0.064 millimetres. The observed difference, 0.03 millimetres, is less than that. There is no obvious conflict on this comparison alone.

If both laboratories used the same reference with a shared error, independence would be the wrong assumption. Correlation changes the uncertainty of the difference. The little calculation is useful precisely because its assumptions can be stated; without them, it becomes another authoritative-looking number.

The vocabulary of metrology treats a [measurement result](https://jcgm.bipm.org/vim/en/2.9.html) as more than a bare displayed value. It also distinguishes [measurement uncertainty](https://jcgm.bipm.org/vim/en/2.26.html) from a known mistake. Uncertainty describes the dispersion of values reasonably attributable to the measurand on the available information. It is not a confession that the laboratory did the job badly.

Nor is a standard uncertainty automatically a guaranteed interval around the truth. Coverage claims require additional assumptions and a stated procedure. Writing a plus-or-minus sign cannot do that work by itself.

### A convention that does not command nature

The millimetre introduces another human contribution: a unit system. A different civilization could count lengths in differently sized units. That would change the numerical description of the rod without changing whether it fits through a particular opening.

The modern metre is defined by fixing the numerical value of the speed of light in vacuum at 299,792,458 metres per second, with the second defined through the caesium frequency. The definition establishes an exact relationship between units and a physical constant. It does not grant every laboratory an exact ruler. Realizing the unit and transferring it to a bench measurement remain practical operations. [BIPM's definition of the metre](https://www.bipm.org/en/si-base-units/metre) states the relationship directly.

This combination is easy to misunderstand. If the unit is conventional, one might conclude that the measured fact is merely conventional. But choosing a unit resembles choosing a scale for a drawing only in a limited respect: it fixes how a quantity is expressed. It does not let us choose the rod-to-opening ratio or erase thermal expansion.

There are conventions in the language of measurement and constraints in the behavior of what is measured. Both can be present in the same result.

### What survives a change of laboratory

A measurement becomes more useful when another laboratory can connect its own procedure to the same reference framework. Metrological traceability supplies such a connection through a documented calibration chain, with uncertainty contributions along it. Traceability alone does not guarantee that the final uncertainty is small enough for a particular use, or that the operator chose the right measurand. Those limitations appear in the [formal definition and notes](https://jcgm.bipm.org/vim/en/2.41.html).

For our rod, a calibration certificate cannot rescue a measurement taken between the wrong surfaces. Equally, a perfectly specified target cannot rescue an instrument whose response has not been characterized. Definitions and experiments do different jobs, and a good result needs them to meet.

Where, then, is the fact? There is a physical arrangement on the bench. There is a proposition about that arrangement: the rod has such a length under such conditions. There is evidence supporting a numerical estimate of that length. Confusing these three makes measurement seem either infallible or hopelessly subjective.

We can instead say something more useful: this result is the best-supported estimate under an explicit definition, with an uncertainty appropriate to the evidence. That statement permits confidence and correction at once.

Before the rod enters an assembly, someone still has to make a decision. The tolerance may be generous enough that the measurement settles the matter. It may be so tight that a better procedure is needed. Reality supplies no extra decimal place simply because a decision would be easier with one.

The responsible report carries enough information for its next reader to know the difference.
