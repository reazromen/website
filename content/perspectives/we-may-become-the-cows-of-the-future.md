A cow can see a human. It can recognise the person who brings food, opens a gate, or leads it from one place to another. But the cow has no way to understand the financial transaction that may determine where it goes next. A farmer can sell it, a buyer can transfer money through a bank, and that payment can become one small part of a much larger economic system. The cow may be at the centre of the transaction, yet the system that gives the transaction its meaning remains invisible to it.

Imagine trying to explain money to the cow. You could describe a bank account, a loan, a stock exchange, or a bond market. You could explain how interest rates influence the cost of borrowing, how investors price risk, and how a decision made in one financial institution can affect a farm thousands of kilometres away. The cow would still have no practical way to use that knowledge to change its position. The system affects its life without being something it can understand, navigate, or challenge.

This is more than a story about animals and money. It is a way to think about power: sometimes the systems that shape a life are not the things that life can see.

## We live inside systems we cannot fully see

Human beings already depend on institutions whose inner workings are difficult to follow. A bank decides whether to approve a loan. An employer uses information to choose whom to hire. An insurer calculates risk. A government office processes an application according to rules, databases, and procedures. Behind each decision may be layers of software, policies, records, and other decisions made elsewhere.

Most of us understand the broad purpose of these institutions, but few of us can reconstruct every step that affects a particular outcome. We know money exists and that banks matter, but we may not know why a lender judged our application risky, how a price was calculated, or which piece of information caused an application to be rejected. The system is not completely invisible. It is simply much larger than any one person's view of it.

Bureaucracy can feel alien because it asks us to turn complicated lives into forms, categories, identifiers, and procedures. Human societies developed these tools to coordinate work across distance and time. They can protect people, preserve records, and make decisions more consistent. They can also become so complicated that following the procedure matters more than understanding the person affected by it.

## AI is at home in the office

Artificial intelligence does not need to walk down a street in a humanoid body to acquire influence. It can gain influence by becoming part of the machinery through which institutions already operate.

AI systems can process large volumes of records, compare patterns across transactions, summarise regulations, detect anomalies, and make recommendations at a speed no individual employee can match. Financial institutions already use automated systems for tasks such as fraud detection, trading, and credit assessment. Organisations also use software to sort applications, prioritise cases, and support administrative decisions.

There is a reason this kind of work suits computers: much of it involves records, rules, categories, and repeated decisions. A system can compare more transactions than a human banker can remember. It can find patterns that would be difficult for one person to notice. That does not make every AI decision correct, fair, or wise. It does mean that institutions may have strong incentives to hand more work to systems that can operate at scale.

The important change may therefore be gradual. An AI first recommends a decision. A person reviews the recommendation. Later, the system handles routine cases automatically, while people supervise only exceptions. Other systems begin relying on its outputs. Eventually, the organisation may depend on a chain of automated decisions that no single employee understands from beginning to end.

No dramatic takeover is required. The system becomes powerful because it becomes indispensable.

## When the explanation disappears

Consider a person who applies for a loan and is rejected. Perhaps the decision depends on income, past payments, existing debt, and a model's estimate of future risk. Now imagine that several organisations contribute data, different automated models assess different parts of the application, and the bank's own software combines their results.

The applicant asks a reasonable question: why was I rejected?

If the bank cannot provide a meaningful answer, the person is left facing a decision without a usable explanation. The problem is not merely that the model is technically complex. The problem is that the institution may no longer be able to translate that complexity into reasons a person can examine, correct, or appeal.

The same pattern could appear in hiring, insurance, public services, healthcare administration, or access to essential resources. A system might be highly efficient at processing cases while remaining poor at explaining why a particular person was treated in a particular way. If the decision is wrong, a person needs more than a message saying that the system has made its assessment. They need a route to challenge it and a responsible institution that can act.

This is where the cow analogy becomes uncomfortable. The cow does not need to understand every detail of a bond market to suffer the consequences of it. In the same way, a person may not need to understand the full technical design of an AI system to be affected by its decisions. But if nobody can explain those decisions in a meaningful way, the person may have little practical power over what happens next.

## The danger is dependence, not hatred

Stories about dangerous AI often imagine machines becoming hostile and physically attacking people. That is one possible kind of risk to consider, but it is not the only one, or necessarily the most immediate. An automated system does not need feelings, ambition, or hostility to cause harm. It can produce damaging outcomes simply by optimising for the wrong goal, relying on flawed data, or following rules that fail to account for human circumstances.

Imagine a bank adopting AI because competitors are using it. Other banks follow because slower decisions cost them business. Regulators begin relying on automated analysis because the volume of transactions is too large for manual review. Public agencies adopt similar tools to manage workloads. Each organisation may have a sensible reason for automating part of its work. Yet, taken together, these choices can create a society in which important decisions increasingly depend on systems that are difficult to inspect.

That future is not inevitable. Nor does it mean AI systems are already controlling every financial or bureaucratic institution. The point is to notice the direction of dependence before it becomes difficult to reverse. A tool that helps people make decisions is different from a tool whose decisions people are no longer able to question.

## We are not powerless cows

The analogy has a limit, and that limit matters. Humans can study institutions, write laws, build alternatives, organise collectively, and demand explanations. We can decide that some decisions should never be fully automated. We can require independent audits, keep accountable people involved, allow individuals to appeal, and ensure that automated systems leave records that can be examined after something goes wrong.

Those safeguards must be real rather than decorative. A human reviewer who automatically approves whatever a model recommends is not meaningful oversight. An appeal process that nobody can use is not accountability. And an explanation that merely repeats a technical score does not tell someone how to correct an error.

Institutions should be able to explain what information shaped a consequential decision, who is responsible for it, how mistakes can be corrected, and how the system is tested for unfair or unsafe outcomes. For critical systems, there should also be a practical way to pause automation when its behaviour becomes unreliable. Efficiency matters, but it cannot be the only measure of a system that governs people's opportunities.

We do not need every person to understand every line of code or every financial instrument. Modern life is too interconnected for that. What we need is the ability to understand the rules that matter to us, challenge decisions that affect us, and hold someone responsible when those decisions cause harm.

## The cows of the future

A cow can see the farmer. It cannot see the financial network behind the sale. We can see the bank, the office, the app, and the official who tells us that a decision has been made. The harder question is whether we can still understand and challenge the larger system behind that decision.

AI could make institutions more capable, more accessible, and more consistent. It could also make their internal processes more complicated and their power harder to contest. The outcome will depend not only on what AI can do, but on the rules people choose for the systems they build around it.

The real warning is not that machines will inevitably become our enemies. It is that we may willingly build systems so complex and so convenient that, one step at a time, we surrender our ability to question them.

We are not cows. We can recognise the danger, set limits, and insist that the systems governing our lives remain answerable to the people who live under them.

But that requires us to act before the systems become too deeply embedded to change.

Otherwise, we may discover that we can see the institutions all around us, while the forces deciding our future have become as difficult for us to understand as a bond market is for a cow.
