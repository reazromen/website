# The Sinai Insurgency in Egypt, 2011 Onward

The insurgency in Egypt’s Sinai Peninsula intensified after the 2011 uprising that ended Hosni Mubarak’s presidency. Armed groups attacked security personnel and infrastructure, while Egyptian forces expanded military operations in the peninsula. The conflict changed as local militant networks reorganized and a faction pledged allegiance to the Islamic State. Its history cannot be reduced to a single organization or a uniform level of violence throughout the period.

## Armed groups and changing phases

Ansar Bayt al-Maqdis emerged as a prominent militant organization in Sinai, conducting attacks against Egyptian security forces and other targets. In 2014, the group pledged allegiance to the Islamic State and became known as Islamic State Sinai Province. The affiliation changed its public identity and operational framing, but the organization’s local roots and the wider political conditions in Sinai remained important. Other armed actors and smuggling networks should not automatically be treated as part of the same command structure.

Egyptian authorities responded with raids, checkpoints, arrests and large security campaigns. Militants used ambushes, improvised explosive devices, shootings and attacks on fixed positions. Attacks also affected civilians, including members of religious minorities and residents accused by militants of cooperating with the state. Official casualty and operational claims need independent corroboration where possible.

## Geography and civilian life

Sinai’s border position, uneven public services, restrictions on movement and long-standing grievances shaped the conflict environment. Military operations and security restrictions disrupted livelihoods, access to land, schooling and medical care. Residents faced danger both from militant attacks and from the effects of counterinsurgency operations, including displacement and demolitions reported by human-rights organizations.

The conflict also intersects with Egypt’s wider security policies and events in neighboring Gaza, but these should not be merged into one theatre without evidence. Cross-border geography can create connections without making every actor part of a single war.

## Conflict status and archive method

The intensity of fighting has varied over time, and the public visibility of attacks is not a complete measure of the security situation. The archive should record dated attacks, government operations, civilian harm and shifts in armed-group activity. It should avoid assuming that a reduction in reported attacks means every underlying grievance or security risk has disappeared.

This entry treats Sinai as an actor- and region-specific insurgency nested within Egypt’s broader security context. The classification of particular events should be checked against UCDP and other event-level datasets, whose definitions and coverage differ.
