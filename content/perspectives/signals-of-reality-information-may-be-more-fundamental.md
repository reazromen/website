We normally think of information as something that describes the world.

A photograph contains information about a person. A database contains information about transactions. DNA contains information involved in constructing and maintaining biological systems.

But physics raises a more interesting possibility: perhaps information is not merely something humans extract from physical systems. Perhaps information is deeply embedded in the structure of physical reality itself.

A physical system can be distinguished from another system because it is in a different state. Those differences can, in principle, carry information.

A bit is therefore not merely an abstract symbol floating in mathematics. In computing, a bit is represented by a physical state. Memory cells, electrical charges, magnetic orientations, optical states, and quantum systems can all encode information.

This connection becomes particularly profound in quantum theory.

Quantum information treats quantum states as objects that can carry information, but quantum information behaves differently from classical information. Quantum states can exist in superpositions, become entangled, and cannot always be described as collections of independently existing classical properties.

The strange part is that information has physical consequences.

Landauer’s principle, for example, connects the irreversible erasure of information with thermodynamic cost. The boundary between information theory and physics is therefore not merely philosophical. It can be expressed experimentally and mathematically.

Black holes push the question even further.

The black-hole information problem asks how information behaves when matter falls beyond an event horizon and the black hole eventually evaporates through Hawking radiation. Decades of theoretical work have made information preservation a central issue in fundamental physics.

This does not prove that “the universe is made of information.” That stronger statement remains an interpretation rather than an established fact.

But there is a compelling signal here.

The physical world is not simply a collection of objects. It is also a collection of distinguishable states, correlations, constraints, and transformations.

A tree is different from a rock because their physical configurations differ.

A brain differs from another brain through enormous patterns of physical organization.

A computer program exists because physical states are arranged so that transformations correspond to logical operations.

Information therefore sits strangely close to the foundations of description itself.

Perhaps the deeper question is not whether reality contains information.

It clearly does.

The harder question is:

Why does physical reality permit information to exist, persist, transform, and become organized into observers capable of asking what reality is?

That question remains open.

And that is precisely why it is interesting.
