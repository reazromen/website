# Brazil’s Organized Armed Violence: Factions, Milícias and State Operations

Brazil has experienced persistent lethal violence involving organized criminal factions, local militias, police operations and territorial disputes. These forms of violence vary greatly by state and municipality. The scale of homicide is an important public-safety measure, but it should not be used alone to describe the entire country as one armed conflict.

## Factions and local power

Major criminal organizations such as the Primeiro Comando da Capital (PCC) and Comando Vermelho (CV) have developed networks that extend beyond their places of origin. In some areas, disputes involve prison organization, drug markets, territorial control and competition with other factions. Armed groups described as milícias have also exercised coercive power, including in parts of Rio de Janeiro. Their composition, revenue sources and relationships with local institutions require case-specific analysis.

State police operations can produce substantial civilian harm and may interact with factional conflict, but a police raid is not automatically part of a continuous armed campaign. Nor can every homicide in a city be attributed to organized groups. Incident-level data and transparent definitions are needed to distinguish factional clashes, one-sided violence, state force and ordinary criminal killings.

## Geography and uneven evidence

Brazil is too large and diverse to be represented accurately by a single conflict label. Patterns in metropolitan Rio de Janeiro, São Paulo, the Amazon region and border states can differ in organization and political context. Reports should identify the location and period and avoid extrapolating a local conflict dynamic to the entire federation.

## Archive treatment

This entry is a research index for documented organized armed campaigns, not a declaration that Brazil has one national war. Any future standalone case should identify the parties, demonstrate continuity and organization, and explain which definition of violence is being applied. Where evidence is insufficient, the archive should retain a public-security classification rather than force the case into an armed-conflict category.
