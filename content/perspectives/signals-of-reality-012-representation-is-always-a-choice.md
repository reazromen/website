Before a James Webb Space Telescope image becomes a field of colored clouds and stars, someone must decide how invisible light will appear on a human screen.

Webb observes infrared wavelengths that our eyes cannot see. Its exposures through different filters are assigned visible colors and combined. Image specialists also adjust the display of brightness so that recorded structures can be seen across an enormous range of intensities. NASA's [explanation of the process](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/science-overview/science-explainers/how-are-webbs-full-color-images-made/) describes these deliberate transformations, including the usual ordering of shorter wavelengths toward blue and longer wavelengths toward red.

The resulting picture is constructed. That fact alone does not make its clouds imaginary.

The important distinction lies between a transformation that remains accountable to measurements and an invention presented as measurement. An assigned color can communicate a recorded difference. An added feature with no observational basis cannot claim the same status. “Someone chose this” starts the inquiry into a representation; it does not settle whether the representation deserves trust.

Even the wish to see an unprocessed image contains a choice. A file of detector values still needs rules for turning numbers into screen brightness. A display that leaves faint structures invisible may be less informative for a particular purpose than a carefully documented adjustment. The absence of a conspicuous editing gesture does not guarantee the absence of a convention.

The choice can also be inherited. An author may accept a software default without considering alternatives. A reader may learn a color association so early that it seems natural. In both cases, a decision has become difficult to notice. Calling representation a choice need not imply that every maker freely redesigns every convention; it means that the form is one possible way of encoding the subject, with consequences that can be examined.

### The surface that will not lie flat

A world map makes the constraint unusually clear. The curved surface of the Earth cannot be flattened while preserving every relevant geometric relationship. A projection can preserve area, or preserve angles locally, or preserve selected distances, but cannot preserve all these properties everywhere. The US Geological Survey's [guide to map projections](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/70047422/report.pdf) sets out the tradeoffs.

On a Mercator map, a route maintaining a constant compass bearing appears as a straight line, while areas become greatly exaggerated toward the poles. An equal-area projection serves comparisons of extent, while distorting shapes. Neither property makes a projection universally honest or dishonest. Trouble begins when the viewer uses a map to answer a question its geometry does not support.

Suppose an editor is illustrating how much land two regions occupy. Choosing an equal-area projection has an evidential reason: area is the quantity readers are being invited to compare. If the editor instead wants to explain the advantages of a navigation chart, another projection may deserve attention. Purpose supplies a criterion, and geometry determines whether the criterion is met.

This is a constrained choice. The continents do not acquire different actual areas when the editor changes projections. The representation changes; the relationship being represented remains available to check.

It is tempting to extend this example into the claim that every disagreement is merely a difference of projection. That analogy fails quickly. Many disputes concern incompatible factual claims, not alternative ways to display agreed facts. Two maps that deliberately preserve different properties can both be appropriate. A map placing an island where there is no island has made a different kind of mistake.

### Decisions inside one small chart

We can see the same issue without leaving a spreadsheet. Consider an invented dataset: a service records 100 completed requests on one day and 110 on the next. These numbers are a worked example, not reported measurements.

Draw bars rising from zero and the increase looks modest. Crop the vertical scale tightly around 100 and the difference can dominate the image. Display the two values as points joined by a line and attention moves toward change. Write “10 additional requests” and the reader sees an absolute difference. Write “10 percent growth” and the reader sees a relative one.

The arithmetic does not choose among these presentations. The question does.

For a comparison of total volume, bars with a common zero make their lengths proportional to the quantities. For close inspection of small variation, a restricted scale on a clearly labeled line chart may help. The same restriction on bars can undermine the visual comparison their lengths invite. Disclosing an axis is necessary, but good design also considers what readers are likely to infer before reading every label.

Now add a missing detail to the hypothetical case: the service was available for ten hours on the first day and eleven on the second. The total increased, while the average number completed per available hour remained ten. Neither calculation invalidates the other. They answer different questions about the same stipulated events.

A title such as “Demand Accelerates” would go further than either calculation warrants. Completed requests are not automatically a measure of demand, and two totals do not establish acceleration. Here the representational choice includes a claim about what the quantity means. A correct number can be recruited into an unsupported story by the words placed above it.

This is why a legend, denominator, and definition belong to the argument. They tell the reader which comparisons the figure permits. Without them, a chart may be visually precise while leaving its central assertion ambiguous.

### Responsibility survives selection

No representation carries all the circumstances of its making inside its visible surface. A photograph has a frame. A table has columns. A sentence has a subject. Trying to include everything would usually destroy the communication the representation was made to enable.

But the inevitability of selection does not excuse any selection whatsoever. We can ask whether important alternatives were considered, whether omissions would change the conclusion, and whether another person can recover the path from evidence to display. These are practical standards of accountability, not a demand for a view from nowhere.

There is a difference between simplifying the route to a supported conclusion and arranging the display so that competing evidence cannot be noticed. The former helps an audience think. The latter makes the audience's judgment depend on an absence it has no opportunity to inspect.

Sometimes the best representation is therefore a pair: an overall view beside a detail, totals beside rates, an astronomical composite beside its filter key. The added view need not repeat the first. It can expose the precise tradeoff that the first makes difficult to see.

The final color in a telescope image is a decision at the end of a long chain. We should be able to follow that chain backward: from hue to filter, from filter to recorded signal, from signal to the instrument and its uncertainties. Beauty need not obstruct that journey. A beautiful image becomes more informative when the viewer can discover what its colors mean.
