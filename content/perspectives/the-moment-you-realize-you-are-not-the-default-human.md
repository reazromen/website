Most of us move through daily life with a quiet assumption: the way things are done around us is, more or less, the way things are done. We may know intellectually that other cultures have different customs, languages, and habits. Yet the routines we know still feel like the default setting for human life.

An unfamiliar environment can unsettle that assumption in small ways. A greeting may carry a different expectation. A shop may organize its goods in a way we do not understand. A question that seems perfectly ordinary to us may sound strange or incomplete to someone else. Even the pace and shape of a transaction can differ from what we expect.

These moments are not proof that one way is better or worse. They reveal that many behaviors we experience as natural are learned conventions. We acquire them so early and repeat them so often that they stop feeling like conventions at all.

This is one reason perspective is difficult. We can recognize difference in the abstract while continuing to interpret every unfamiliar situation through our own habits. When another person behaves unexpectedly, we may assume they are being rude, inefficient, or unreasonable. Sometimes that interpretation is correct. Often, however, we have not yet learned the local context needed to judge fairly.

The same problem appears in institutions and technology. Designers may build a form around assumptions about names, addresses, literacy, connectivity, or family structure. If those assumptions fit the designer's environment, the form can seem obvious. For someone outside that environment, it may be confusing or even impossible to complete. What looks like a universal solution can quietly encode a local worldview.

Noticing this does not require abandoning judgment or pretending that every practice is equally good. It requires separating what we have observed from what we have inferred. It asks us to be curious before turning unfamiliarity into a verdict.

There is a useful humility in discovering that we are not the default human. Our habits are real, but they are not the only possible habits. Our expectations may be reasonable, but they are not universal laws. Other people are not incomplete versions of ourselves; they are navigating the world through different histories and learned systems.

The discovery can be uncomfortable because it shifts us from the center of our own map. It can also be liberating. Once we stop treating our local conventions as invisible truths, we become better able to understand other people—and more willing to question the assumptions built into our own world.
