# Korean Peninsula Armed Incidents, 2000–2026

The Korean Peninsula remains divided by an unresolved armistice rather than a peace treaty formally ending the 1950–1953 Korean War. Since 2000, the peninsula has experienced military standoffs, missile tests, maritime confrontations and occasional lethal incidents. These events are strategically connected, but they do not amount to uninterrupted warfare throughout the period.

## Incidents and escalation

The 2002 and 2009 naval clashes near the Northern Limit Line, the sinking of the South Korean corvette Cheonan in 2010, the shelling of Yeonpyeong Island in 2010, and later exchanges of fire and military demonstrations illustrate different types of incidents. Attribution and interpretation have varied by case; for example, the Cheonan investigation and North Korea’s responsibility have been disputed by Pyongyang. A careful archive records the evidence and the parties’ positions without presenting contested claims as settled fact.

Missile launches, military exercises and hostile rhetoric can raise the risk of conflict but are not themselves necessarily armed clashes. Similarly, a temporary increase in alert status is not proof of a new war. A dated chronology helps distinguish lethal use of force from coercive signalling and political crisis.

## Why this is not one continuous war entry

The armistice context explains why each incident can carry strategic significance. But a research archive covering 2000–2026 should not assign every year to a single active armed conflict without checking the dataset definition and the events recorded. Individual incidents differ in scale, casualties, attribution and whether they involve direct force between state militaries.

## Archive treatment

This page is an incident index. Each event should be recorded with a date, location, participants, casualties where reliably established, and links to official or independent investigations. Where sources disagree, the disagreement belongs in the record. The page should connect the historical armistice to present-day security without inflating the number of armed-conflict years.
