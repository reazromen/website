Switch off every lamp in a room at night. Wait until your eyes adjust. The furniture dissolves into darkness.

It is tempting to describe what happened as "the light is gone." In everyday language that is fine. In physics it is incomplete.

The room is still full of electromagnetic activity. Walls, furniture and your own body emit thermal radiation, much of it in the infrared. A Wi-Fi access point may be producing radio-frequency electromagnetic waves. Broadcast signals can pass through the building. The cosmic microwave background exists around us at microwave wavelengths, although in an ordinary room it is overwhelmed by radiation from much warmer local surroundings.

Darkness is not necessarily the absence of electromagnetic radiation. It is usually the absence—or insufficiency—of radiation that human vision can detect under the circumstances.

## Your eyes define a narrow window

Visible light occupies only a small part of the electromagnetic spectrum.

Electromagnetic radiation spans radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. These are useful names for frequency ranges, not different fundamental substances. Frequency and wavelength change; all are electromagnetic radiation.

Human photoreceptors are sensitive to a limited range centered roughly on wavelengths from about 400 to 700 nanometres, with exact boundaries depending on how visibility is defined.

Radiation outside that range does not become nonexistent.

It becomes invisible to us.

A thermal camera makes the distinction vivid. In a room that looks black to your eyes, warm objects can stand out strongly in long-wave infrared. The camera is not adding visible illumination to the scene. Its detector responds to wavelengths your retina does not.

The resulting display often uses visible colors chosen by software so humans can inspect measured infrared intensities. Those colors are a representation of an invisible measurement, not hidden paint covering the objects.

## Warm things radiate

Any ordinary object above absolute zero emits thermal electromagnetic radiation. The spectrum depends strongly on temperature and on how the material emits and reflects radiation.

A room-temperature object emits strongly in the infrared. Heat an object enough and its thermal emission reaches visible wavelengths: first a dull red glow, then brighter colors as temperature rises.

This is why a stove can be visually dark while an infrared detector sees strong radiation.

The idealized blackbody spectrum gives a foundational model for thermal radiation, but real surfaces are not perfect blackbodies. Their emissivity varies with material, surface condition and wavelength. They can also reflect infrared radiation from their surroundings.

That is where the phrase "everything glows in infrared" reaches its limit. A thermal camera does not simply read an object's true temperature from a colored pixel. Correct interpretation can require assumptions about emissivity, reflected temperature, atmospheric transmission and the instrument itself.

The detector has a model too.

## Darkness belongs to a receiver

Imagine three observers in the same unlit room.

The first is a human.

The second has a thermal infrared camera.

The third has a radio receiver.

They do not encounter three different rooms. They couple to different parts of the physical activity in the same room.

For the human, the room may be visually dark.

For the thermal detector, a person is a structured warm source against cooler surfaces.

For the radio receiver, faces and chairs may be irrelevant; the dominant events could be transmissions, reflections and interference.

"Nothing is there" would be the wrong conclusion from any one detector failing to respond.

A more precise statement is:

**Nothing above this detector's threshold was observed in this band, at this time, using this method.**

That sentence is less dramatic and far more useful.

Astronomy depends on the same discipline. Dust can block visible light while infrared measurements reveal structures that optical telescopes miss. Radio telescopes detect phenomena invisible to the eye. X-ray telescopes reveal high-energy plasmas around compact objects and galaxy clusters.

The universe did not change each time a new detector was invented.

Our observational bandwidth changed.

## Darkness is still real

There is an overcorrection to avoid. If invisible radiation is common, is darkness merely an illusion?

No.

Visual darkness describes a real relation between available visible photons, the eye's sensitivity and neural processing. A functioning eye cannot construct a detailed visible image from arbitrarily few photons. Detection has limits.

Dark adaptation changes those limits. Pupils dilate, and retinal sensitivity changes substantially, especially as rod-mediated vision becomes more important. Features that were initially invisible can emerge after time in low light.

The process does not create the furniture. It changes the observer.

Cameras make similar tradeoffs. A longer exposure can collect more photons, but movement may blur and sensor noise matters. A wider aperture admits more light but changes optical depth of field. Better seeing always comes with physical constraints.

## A better phrase

"Light is everywhere" is beautiful but too broad for careful physics. There are environments and frequency bands where electromagnetic radiation can be extremely weak, and shielding can dramatically attenuate signals.

The useful correction is smaller:

**The absence of visible light is not the same thing as the absence of electromagnetic radiation.**

Turn off the lamp again.

The room does not vanish.

Your eyes have simply lost most of the channel through which they normally learn where the room is.
