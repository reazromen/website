A photon leaves a distant galaxy.

Billions of years later, it enters a telescope.

How did it avoid hitting something?

Because the universe is mostly empty in a very literal sense.

Intergalactic matter is extraordinarily sparse.

A photon traveling through the right wavelength range can cross immense distances without being absorbed or scattered.

But the journey is not unchanged.

## Empty space is not nothing

Intergalactic space contains gas.

Plasma.

Radiation fields.

Magnetic fields.

Dark matter gravitationally.

Large-scale structure.

The densities are tiny compared with Earth's atmosphere or a star.

That makes the mean free path for many photons enormous.

Opacity depends on wavelength and intervening material.

Some photons travel freely where others are absorbed.

## Expansion stretches the light

While the photon travels, the universe expands.

Its wavelength grows with the cosmic scale factor.

The photon arrives redshifted.

This means a telescope observing infrared light can be detecting radiation emitted at much shorter wavelengths in the distant past.

The journey changes the observed spectrum.

Cosmological redshift becomes part of the message.

## Matter can interrupt the path

A cloud of neutral hydrogen can absorb particular wavelengths.

Dust can absorb and scatter visible light.

Ionized gas leaves spectral signatures.

Galaxies can obscure background objects.

The photon path therefore contains information about both source and foreground.

Astronomers sometimes use absorption lines from intervening material to study matter the photon encountered along the way.

An obstruction becomes another measurement.

## Gravity bends the path

Mass curves spacetime.

Light follows that geometry.

Massive galaxies and clusters can gravitationally lens background objects.

The photon still locally travels at the speed of light in vacuum, but its trajectory through curved spacetime can be deflected.

Lensing magnifies, distorts, and sometimes produces multiple images.

The universe can act as an optical system.

## "Travel time" is subtle in an expanding universe

It is tempting to say:

the galaxy is ten billion light-years away, therefore the light traveled ten billion years through a static distance.

Cosmology is not that simple.

The distance between source and observer changes while the photon travels.

Different distance definitions—comoving distance, proper distance, luminosity distance—answer different questions.

Lookback time is usually the clearest quantity when discussing how old the observed light is.

The geometry matters.

## Photons do not get tired

An old historical idea called tired light proposed that photons lose energy simply because they travel.

That is not the standard cosmological explanation for observed redshift.

Expansion provides a consistent framework connected to many other observations.

The redshift is not fatigue.

It is spacetime history.

## Survival creates selection

The photons we detect are the ones that survived their paths and entered the detector.

Other photons were absorbed.

Scattered.

Shifted outside the instrument band.

Blocked by foregrounds.

Observation is therefore filtered by propagation.

Astronomy always sees the universe through a transmission function.

## A telescope receives a history

One photon cannot tell the whole story.

A spectrum of many photons can reveal:

source temperature,

chemical elements,

redshift,

intervening gas,

dust,

motion,

and gravitational effects.

How can light travel for billions of years?

Because much of the path is extraordinarily transparent.

And because the light that survives does not arrive untouched.

It arrives carrying marks from the journey.
