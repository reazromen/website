# The Pig War: The International Crisis Started by One Pig

*The pig did not create the border dispute. It became the spark that exposed how dangerous an unresolved dispute had already become.*

In the summer of 1859, the United States and Great Britain came close to a military confrontation over an island in the Pacific Northwest. The episode is remembered as the Pig War because the immediate incident involved a pig killed in a potato patch. The name is funny. The political circumstances were not.

San Juan Island lay between competing interpretations of the boundary established by the 1846 Oregon Treaty. The treaty described a channel separating the mainland from Vancouver Island, but the geography offered more than one plausible channel. The island's sovereignty was therefore contested. British interests, including the Hudson's Bay Company, operated there, while American settlers also claimed land.

## A pig in the wrong potato patch

On June 15, 1859, American settler Lyman Cutlar found a pig rooting in his garden and shot it. The animal belonged to the Hudson's Bay Company. Cutlar reportedly offered compensation, but the disagreement quickly became entangled with the larger question of whether American settlers had a right to occupy the land.

British authorities threatened action against Cutlar. American settlers feared that British officials would arrest him and remove them from the island. They appealed to American military authorities, and troops were sent to protect U.S. claims. British forces responded by bringing warships and marines into the area.

A pig had become the centre of an international crisis—not because a pig was worth a war, but because the two countries were already competing over territory and jurisdiction. When the rules are unclear and trust is low, a small incident can become a test of national resolve.

## When pride starts making decisions

The military buildup was alarming. American troops landed on the island, and British naval forces arrived with considerably greater firepower. Commanders faced pressure to defend their positions, while political leaders far away had to decide whether they wanted a local dispute to become a war between major powers.

Fortunately, several officials chose restraint. British Rear Admiral James Baynes resisted the idea of launching an attack simply to remove the Americans. The United States sent General Winfield Scott to help calm the crisis. The two sides eventually agreed to a joint military occupation while diplomats worked toward a final settlement.

For years, British and American forces remained on the island without fighting one another. The dispute was eventually referred to Kaiser Wilhelm I of Germany for arbitration. In 1872, the decision favoured the United States, and British forces withdrew.

The episode is often summarized as a war in which the only casualty was a pig. That is a memorable line, but the more important fact is that the armed forces did not turn the crisis into a battle. The island experienced a long joint occupation, and the boundary question was resolved through diplomacy rather than a shooting war.

## Why this story is more than a joke

The Pig War shows how escalation can happen. A local disagreement becomes a question of law. The legal question becomes a matter of national honour. Military deployments create new pressure: once soldiers and ships arrive, leaders may feel that backing down would look weak. Each side's defensive action can look offensive to the other.

This is a classic security dilemma. Both parties may claim to be protecting their people, yet their responses make the other side feel less secure. The result can be a crisis neither side originally intended.

The pig is the comic detail that makes the story easy to remember. It is also a useful warning about headlines. If we say the countries nearly fought over a pig, we risk missing the unresolved treaty language, territorial competition, settler expansion, and command decisions that made the confrontation possible.

The most admirable part of the story is not the absurd trigger. It is the restraint that followed. Officials had reasons to escalate and plenty of weapons available. Instead, they created time for negotiation. In this case, the pig did not become the opening act of a major war.
