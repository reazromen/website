# FARC Dissident Groups After Colombia’s 2016 Peace Agreement

The 2016 peace agreement between Colombia’s government and the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC) was a turning point, but it did not produce a single, uniform post-conflict landscape. Most of the FARC’s principal organization demobilized and entered a political transition. Some former members rejected the agreement, while other networks later rearmed or formed new alliances. These organizations are often collectively called FARC dissidents, but the term can conceal substantial differences.

## Why dissidence emerged

Reasons for rejecting or abandoning the peace process varied. They included opposition to the agreement, distrust of state guarantees, local command disputes, fear of prosecution, and the economic and territorial incentives associated with illicit markets. These explanations should not be mistaken for justification of abuses. They help explain why demobilization at the national level did not immediately remove armed structures in every region.

Dissident groups have changed names, leadership and alliances. Some have sought to present themselves as continuations of the historic FARC; others operate more like regional armed networks. Government designations and media labels may also change. A reliable chronology therefore needs to specify which organization, faction and period a report concerns.

## Territorial competition and civilian exposure

Armed groups have sought influence over rural communities and corridors linked to coca cultivation, trafficking, illegal mining and cross-border movement. Their presence may intersect with ELN units, other armed organizations and state operations. In some places, communities face threats, forced recruitment, restrictions on movement or pressure to comply with local rules imposed by armed actors.

A report of violence in a former FARC area does not automatically establish that FARC dissidents were responsible. Attribution should use incident-specific evidence, including credible local reporting, official documentation and independent human-rights investigations. Likewise, the label “dissident” should not be used to merge unrelated criminal groups into one actor.

## Relationship to the peace process

The existence of armed dissident groups is one measure of the challenges facing the peace agreement, but it is not a complete measure of the agreement’s success or failure. Reintegration, rural development, security for former combatants and community leaders, land access and state presence are distinct policy questions. Former combatants who comply with the agreement must not be conflated with those who return to armed activity.

Peace implementation also varies across regions. A national statement about a ceasefire or negotiation may not apply to every faction, and splinter groups may not be able to enforce a decision across all local units. Each ceasefire should be logged with its parties, dates, geographic scope and verification mechanism.

## Dataset and archive treatment

This entry should be linked to the wider Colombian armed conflict rather than counted as a wholly separate national war. For UCDP reconciliation, the relevant task is to identify actor names and aliases, qualifying state-based dyads and years, and any distinct non-state conflict or one-sided violence events. Those records must be compared without assuming that a group label remains stable throughout the period.

Casualty claims should include a date range and definition. Displacement, threats and territorial control are important impact indicators but should not be added to battle-death totals. Data for 2026 should be marked provisional pending event-level verification.
