The title needs a qualification before it needs a defence. In ordinary language, you certainly can see a cup directly: it is there on the table, without a photograph or a video call standing between you and it. Nothing in physics makes that sentence improper.

Here, “directly” means something narrower: without a physical process carrying information from the object to a sensory system. In that sense, vision is always mediated. The cup does not enter the eye. Light affected by the cup does, and the nervous system responds to that light.

This distinction is easy to state and easy to misuse. It does not establish that we are trapped behind a private screen, unable to know whether cups exist. It identifies the mechanism through which cups become visible. Philosophical theories of direct perception disagree about what experience consists of; the existence of a causal chain alone does not resolve those disagreements. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's discussion of perception](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/perception-problem/) is careful about distinctions that the word “direct” can conceal.

We can set that dispute aside long enough to examine an illuminated table.

### A cup, a pane and a polished spoon

The ceramic cup, the windowpane and the spoon are all material objects. They are not equally conspicuous, and their appearances disclose different things.

The cup sends some of the room's illumination toward the eye. The pane transmits much of the visible light arriving from beyond it while reflecting some at its surfaces. The spoon's polished surface can show distorted images of other parts of the room. Looking in its direction does not simply reveal a local patch of silver-coloured substance. It can reveal the lamp behind your shoulder.

In each case, what arrives at the eye has a history. A bright area on the spoon may owe much more to the position of a lamp than to any difference between that area and the adjacent metal. A nearly invisible pane can still block a hand. Optical conspicuousness and mechanical resistance are different properties.

At a microscopic level, light interacts with the charged constituents of materials. The collective response helps determine reflection, transmission and absorption. Classical electromagnetic theory describes many everyday optical effects extremely well; quantum theory is needed for a fuller account of matter and its allowed energy changes. The [Caltech treatment of reflection from surfaces](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_33.html) develops the electromagnetic account rather than treating a surface as a wall that light simply bumps into.

The familiar bouncing-ball analogy is useful for drawing a reflected ray. It becomes unreliable as a microscopic explanation. A photon is not a tiny hard pellet ricocheting off a hard atom, and ordinary reflection should not be pictured as each atom swallowing a photon, waiting, then independently firing a replacement in a random direction. The organized response of a material matters.

### An appearance has more than one cause

Suppose, as a deliberately simplified example, two matte cards have the same shape and are viewed under identical geometry. One returns about half as much light toward the observer as the other under the same illumination. If we illuminate the less reflective card twice as strongly, we can arrange for similar amounts of light to reach the eye from both.

This is not a report of an experiment. It is an illustration of an ambiguity: an observed brightness depends on illumination as well as on the surface. The arithmetic assumes a linear response and ignores complications such as glare. Those assumptions make the ambiguity clear without claiming to describe every real card.

One measurement can therefore support several explanations. The observer may resolve the ambiguity using shadows, nearby surfaces or a change in viewpoint. An instrument might use controlled illumination. None of these methods abolishes mediation. Each adds constraints on what could have produced the observation.

The same logic applies when two liquids look alike. A clear appearance does not identify their chemical composition. It tells us something about the interaction of the sample, the light and the observer under those conditions. For a question about composition, a visual inspection may simply be the wrong measurement.

That limitation should make us more specific, not more suspicious of all appearances. The cup's visible outline may be excellent evidence of where to reach while its colour is poor evidence of what minerals were used in its glaze. Reliability belongs to a claim made under particular conditions. It is not a single rating stamped on vision as a whole.

### Matter can reveal itself by changing the light

Spectroscopy makes the dependence on interaction especially productive. Instead of collecting light into one familiar colour impression, an instrument separates it by wavelength. Absorption and emission features can then be compared with laboratory measurements and physical models. Characteristic patterns constrain which substances could have produced the spectrum. NASA's [introduction to light and matter](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/science-overview/science-explainers/spectroscopy-101-light-and-matter/) explains why these predictable interactions make remote investigation possible.

A spectrum is still conditional evidence. Temperature, mixtures, overlapping features and the instrument's resolution can complicate identification. The strongest case is usually a pattern of mutually consistent features, with alternatives checked, rather than one conveniently placed line.

Yet the important result is positive. The light can disclose material properties that an ordinary view leaves hidden. A more elaborate chain of measurement can produce a better-supported conclusion than a glance. The number of intermediaries is not a measure of ignorance; what matters is how well their behaviour is understood.

Imagine two claims about the cup: it stands near the table's edge, and it contains a particular pigment. A second viewpoint may settle the first. A spectral measurement may help settle the second. Neither claim becomes arbitrary because it requires a procedure. The procedures differ because the properties differ.

We also gain confidence when independent consequences agree. An object seen on a table can be reached, weighed and viewed from another side. These interactions need not return identical kinds of data. Their value lies in fitting a common account of the object while placing different demands on that account.

The phrase “we only see light” can make this achievement sound smaller than it is. The light has been affected by the world. That is precisely why it can tell us about the world. Replacing that relationship with an impossible demand for perception without any interaction would remove the means of learning, not improve it.

The cup stays on the table. What changes is the precision of our question: which of its properties does this particular encounter with light allow us to know?
