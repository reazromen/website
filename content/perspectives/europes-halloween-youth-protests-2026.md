Europe has seen enough demonstrations to know that a crowded street does not necessarily mean a political turning point. But the protests that unfolded on October 31, 2026, carried an unusual visual contradiction. On a night when people traditionally wear masks to become someone else, thousands of young Europeans went into the streets to make a point about the lives they are actually living.

Halloween did not cause the protests. It happened to coincide with a wave of mobilisation that had already been building across several countries. France was entering a third consecutive weekend of demonstrations over education, public services and the cost of living. Germany saw youth-led rallies organised under the name *Gen Z regelt*. Portugal and Albania also saw demonstrations, while other countries had their own distinct political tensions. The scenes were not one coordinated European uprising, but they revealed a set of anxieties that increasingly cross national borders.

## France: A third weekend in the streets

The largest mobilisation took place in France. The Interior Ministry counted 320,000 demonstrators across 291 public actions on October 31, including around 70,000 in Paris. Organisers gave a higher estimate of approximately 500,000. The official turnout was lower than the previous two weekends, when authorities counted 550,000 on October 17 and 410,000 on October 24.

The movement had begun with students protesting deteriorating school facilities, teacher shortages and concerns about the university admissions system. Its demands widened to include purchasing power, public services and dissatisfaction with the country's political leadership. What began as a dispute over schools had become a broader expression of frustration with the conditions young people expect to inherit.

Most demonstrations were peaceful. Yet confrontations occurred at the edges of some marches, particularly in Paris and other cities. Police reported projectiles and incendiary devices being thrown; officers used tear gas and other crowd-control measures. By 9 p.m., authorities had recorded 565 arrests during the day's mobilisation. The Interior Ministry later reported 779 arrests across the day and night combined, including 214 after 9 p.m. in connection with Halloween-night disturbances. Those figures describe different reporting cut-offs, not competing totals.

The distinction between the daytime demonstrations and later disorder matters. The ministry itself separated the declared protest marches from smaller groups it said had sought to disrupt Halloween festivities. Treating every protester as part of the same violent crowd would erase the difference between a mass political demonstration and the actions of people who used the gathering or the evening's festivities as an opportunity for confrontation.

## Germany: What does Gen Z want?

Germany's demonstrations were organised under the banner *Gen Z regelt!*—roughly, "Gen Z has got this." Rallies were called in more than thirty cities, with demands that included greater investment in education, healthcare, climate protection and affordable living. Opposition to a possible return to compulsory military service was also part of the movement's platform.

The concerns were concrete. Students and young demonstrators pointed to underfunded schools, inadequate facilities and the sense that public institutions were failing to provide the foundations on which future opportunity depends. Housing and living costs added another layer: education may open a door, but it does not guarantee that a young person can afford to build a life near the opportunities that follow.

The demonstrations were largely peaceful during the day. Later, clashes were reported in several cities. In Cologne, police used water cannon and pepper spray after some demonstrators refused to disperse. In Frankfurt, officers were attacked with stones, bottles and fireworks, according to police accounts reported by the Associated Press and Agence France-Presse. Organisers had called for peaceful participation and rejected violence.

This is an important distinction in understanding youth movements. A movement can bring together people with shared concerns without having control over every person who enters a crowd. Violent incidents deserve scrutiny, but they do not automatically explain the motivations of everyone who came to demonstrate.

## A continent, but not one movement

The events were described in some headlines as protests sweeping Europe, but that phrase can make separate national stories appear more unified than they are.

France's demonstrations were shaped by education, public spending and living costs. Germany's youth mobilisation included opposition to conscription. In Lisbon, thousands protested Portugal's housing crisis. Youth-led demonstrations were also reported in Albania. Italy, Spain and other countries had experienced their own protests in the preceding weeks, with different local demands and political contexts.

These events share a broad concern about the future, but there is no basis for treating them as a single organisation with one leadership or one programme. The useful comparison is not that every country is experiencing the same crisis. It is that housing, education, wages and public services have become recurring points of tension in societies with very different political systems.

## The economics of a frustrated generation

For much of the post-war period, the European social model rested on an expectation that economic growth, education and stable institutions would gradually improve living standards. That promise was never fulfilled equally, and it was never guaranteed. But it helped sustain a belief that each generation could expect a reasonable improvement on the conditions of the previous one.

For many young people, that expectation is now harder to recognise.

Housing is one of the clearest examples. In many cities, rents and property prices have moved beyond what early-career wages can comfortably support. A person may have a degree, a job and access to a dynamic urban economy, yet still struggle to afford independent accommodation in the place where that job exists. The problem is not simply the price of a flat; it is the way housing costs shape decisions about work, family, mobility and independence.

Education presents a related contradiction. Governments describe schools and universities as essential to national competitiveness, but students can experience ageing buildings, staff shortages and unequal access to resources. When the institution that is supposed to prepare a generation for the future appears unable to maintain its own basic infrastructure, the political promise begins to sound abstract.

There are also structural pressures. Ageing populations increase demand for pensions and healthcare, while governments face difficult choices about taxation, public debt, defence and investment. These constraints are real, but they do not settle the question of how costs and opportunities should be distributed between generations.

Economic pressure does not mechanically produce protest, much less violence. Political organisation, public trust, policing, local history and the choices of government all influence how dissatisfaction develops. Still, the sense of generational unfairness is politically significant: younger citizens can feel that they are being asked to sustain institutions while having too little influence over the decisions that shape their own prospects.

## When protest and disorder become one story

Images of burning objects, masked figures and police lines travel quickly. They are dramatic, easy to understand at a glance and often more memorable than the demands written on a placard. That can create a distorted picture of a demonstration.

Large crowds are rarely uniform. A peaceful march may include students, parents, unions and people who have never attended a protest before. Smaller groups may seek confrontation, while opportunistic individuals may use disorder to vandalise or loot. Organisers can call for restraint without having the ability to enforce it across an entire city.

The conduct of police also matters. Authorities have a responsibility to protect people and property, but the restrictions imposed on marches and the use of force can themselves become subjects of public dispute. In France, the debate around the October 31 demonstrations included not only violence by some participants but also complaints about restricted routes and police tactics. Those issues should be examined through evidence rather than folded into a single story of chaos.

None of this excuses attacks on people, emergency workers or public property. It means that the causes of a protest, the conduct of participants and the response of the state are separate questions. A serious account has to consider all three.

## The future being negotiated in public

The most revealing feature of these demonstrations may be the range of issues that brought people outside. Education, housing, healthcare, employment, public spending and military obligations appear to be separate policy debates. For a young person trying to plan a life, however, they are connected. They determine whether education leads to opportunity, whether work leads to independence, whether public institutions can be trusted and whether the future feels open or already constrained.

European governments face genuine choices under pressure. Ageing societies, geopolitical insecurity, slow growth and public debt complicate decisions about investment. The demands of demonstrators do not make those constraints disappear. But explaining a constraint is not the same as resolving the political question of who bears its costs.

Democratic institutions are tested not only by elections but also by their ability to hear grievances before those grievances become detached from formal politics. Protests can be disruptive, and they can expose the limits of a government's authority. They can also provide information: a signal that policies, institutions or public explanations are no longer convincing to a substantial part of the population.

Halloween passed. The costumes went away, the streets were cleared and the extraordinary police deployments ended. The questions that brought people outside did not disappear with them.

The important story is not that Europe had a frightening Halloween. It is that, across several different societies, a generation is asking whether the institutions around it can still deliver the future they were built to promise.
