# The Baloch Insurgency in Pakistan: Autonomy, Resources, and State Violence

Balochistan, Pakistan’s largest province by area, has experienced recurring armed rebellions and insurgencies over decades. The current period is not a single uninterrupted campaign with one command structure. It includes different waves of Baloch nationalist militancy, political movements, state operations and local grievances. Armed groups have demanded greater autonomy or independence, while Pakistan’s state has defended its territorial integrity and used security operations to suppress insurgent organizations.

## Historical roots and renewed insurgency

The conflict draws on disputes over provincial authority, representation, natural resources and the distribution of revenue from gas, minerals and major infrastructure. Many Baloch nationalists argue that local communities have not received a fair share of the province’s resources and that security institutions dominate political life. The state and its supporters emphasize separatist violence, attacks on public infrastructure and the need to protect civilians and economic projects. These competing narratives describe real political disagreements but do not justify attacks on non-combatants or abuses by security forces.

A renewed insurgency gathered force in the early 2000s and intensified after the killing of Baloch nationalist leader Nawab Akbar Bugti in a military operation in 2006. Several organizations have operated under different names and with different strategies, including the Balochistan Liberation Army (BLA) and other separatist groups. Their structures and alliances have changed, and an archive should avoid treating every Baloch political organization as an armed actor.

## Tactics, counterinsurgency, and civilian harm

Insurgent groups have attacked security personnel, government facilities, transport infrastructure and people they identify as opponents. Some attacks have targeted workers and travelers from other parts of Pakistan, including attacks on buses, trains and highways. Such violence harms civilians and undermines the claim that the conflict is only a contest between armed organizations and the state.

Human-rights organizations and families have also raised concerns about enforced disappearances, extrajudicial killings, arbitrary detention and restrictions on journalists and activists. The government has disputed some allegations and says security operations target armed militants. Each incident requires evidence and attribution; allegations should not automatically be treated as adjudicated facts, but they should not be omitted from the record simply because the state contests them.

The consequences extend beyond direct deaths. Insecurity affects education, health care, employment, mining, transport and public trust. Families of missing people face long uncertainty, while communities may be pressured by armed groups and security agencies alike. The province’s strategic location and resource potential make it a focus of national development plans, but infrastructure alone cannot resolve political grievances or rebuild confidence.

## A conflict with multiple layers

Balochistan’s insurgency overlaps with sectarian violence, criminal activity, political repression and wider Pakistan security challenges, but those categories should not be merged automatically. Attacks attributed to separatist organizations differ in purpose and organization from sectarian attacks or violence by jihadist groups. Similarly, political protest is not itself armed conflict. The archive should record actor, location, dates, stated aims and evidence for each episode.

The conflict’s future depends on more than military operations. Credible investigations into attacks and disappearances, protection of civilians, accountable policing, meaningful provincial representation and transparent resource arrangements are central to reducing recruitment and restoring legitimacy. A durable settlement would need to address both violence by insurgents and allegations of state abuse.

## Scope and sources

This article treats the post-2000 insurgency as a recurring, evolving conflict rather than a continuous battle of constant intensity. Casualty totals vary by source and time period, and should not be compared without checking whether they count civilians, combatants, security personnel or missing people.
