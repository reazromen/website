# Myanmar’s Nationwide Civil War Since the 2021 Coup

Myanmar’s nationwide conflict escalated after the military seized power on 1 February 2021, detaining elected leaders and overturning the results of the 2020 election. Mass protests and a civil-disobedience movement met lethal repression. In many areas, opponents of military rule turned toward armed resistance, while long-established ethnic armed organizations continued their own struggles for autonomy and security. The result was not one unified rebel army confronting one stable front, but a fragmented conflict system spread across much of the country.

The post-coup war must be distinguished from Myanmar’s older ethnic conflicts. The coup linked those longstanding wars to a new nationwide resistance movement, but it did not erase each region’s history, leadership, aims or command structures. The military, known as the Tatmadaw or Myanmar Armed Forces, remains the central state armed actor; its opponents include ethnic armed organizations, People’s Defence Forces and local resistance groups whose alliances vary over time.

## From protest to armed resistance

In the weeks after the coup, large demonstrations and strikes challenged military rule. Security forces used arrests, beatings and lethal force against protesters. As political opposition was driven underground, some activists travelled to areas controlled by ethnic armed organizations for training and refuge. The National Unity Government, formed by elected lawmakers and other opponents of the coup, supported a People’s Defence Force framework, but local PDFs did not all operate under a single effective chain of command.

Existing ethnic armed organizations had different positions. Some cooperated with resistance forces; others maintained their own campaigns, negotiated, or pursued territorial goals that did not map neatly onto the nationwide anti-coup movement. In borderlands, armed groups also competed over taxation, administration, trade routes and control of territory. The conflict’s political language can obscure these local calculations unless the record identifies actors and dates carefully.

## Expansion across the country

Fighting intensified in Sagaing and Magway in the northwest and across the southeast, including Kayin and Kayah states. Long-running conflicts in Kachin, Shan and Rakhine also changed as alliances, offensives and ceasefires shifted. The military relied on ground operations, artillery, airstrikes, arrests and the destruction or burning of villages. Resistance forces used ambushes, improvised explosive devices, small-unit attacks and assaults on security posts. The capabilities and conduct of individual groups varied substantially.

The large-scale offensive launched by allied ethnic armed organizations in northern Shan in October 2023 changed the military balance and exposed weaknesses in the armed forces’ territorial control. Subsequent operations in other regions intensified pressure on military positions. The Arakan Army’s campaign in Rakhine expanded from the end of the 2023 ceasefire into a major territorial conflict, and by 2025 the fighting had spread beyond Rakhine into neighbouring regions. These campaigns are linked to the nationwide war but deserve separate regional chronologies.

Territory is not a simple measure of political legitimacy. A group’s capture of a town may change who collects revenue, operates checkpoints or administers services, but it does not by itself settle the future constitutional order. Control can also be fragmented: a township may contain a town centre, roads and rural areas controlled by different actors.

## Civilian harm and displacement

Civilians have borne a heavy burden. Airstrikes, shelling, raids, landmines, unexploded ordnance and the destruction of homes have caused deaths, injuries and repeated displacement. Restrictions on humanitarian access and communications complicate independent verification. In many areas, people face shortages of food, clean water, medical care and education while trying to avoid recruitment, arrest or retaliation.

The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs reported in February 2025 that more than 3.5 million people were displaced inside Myanmar and that 19.9 million people were in need of humanitarian assistance that year. These are dated humanitarian planning figures, not a current count for every later month. UNHCR’s 2026 operational information continues to describe widespread displacement and protection risks, while estimates change as people flee, return temporarily or move again. Any article update should preserve the data date and definition.

Conflict has also intersected with economic collapse, disasters and public-service breakdown. The March 2025 earthquake compounded an already severe emergency. People may be displaced multiple times, and a count of current internally displaced people is not equivalent to the cumulative number of people who have ever fled. The archive should keep these measures separate.

## Human rights and the conduct of armed actors

Human-rights reporting has documented serious violations by the military, including attacks affecting civilians, arbitrary detention, torture and restrictions on civic life. Armed opposition groups have also been accused of abuses, including killings, coercion and recruitment practices. Allegations should be attributed to the investigating body and described according to the evidence available. A documented allegation, a UN finding, a domestic charge and a final court judgment are different evidentiary categories.

The laws of armed conflict apply to parties according to the legal classification of hostilities. Parties must distinguish civilians from combatants, take feasible precautions and protect medical and humanitarian activity. The use of air power or explosive weapons in populated areas raises serious civilian-protection concerns, but each incident’s legal assessment depends on the target, expected civilian harm, precautions and available evidence. The fact that one party commits violations does not release another from its obligations.

## Fragmented authority and the search for a settlement

The opposition to military rule includes actors with different political projects, territorial bases and views of federalism. Some ethnic organizations have long sought self-determination or autonomy; many post-coup activists advocate a federal democratic order. These aims overlap in important ways but are not identical. Trust, command relationships, resource sharing and the treatment of minorities within areas controlled by opposition groups remain consequential questions.

Regional diplomacy has struggled to produce a settlement that ends the fighting and restores accountable government. ASEAN’s Five-Point Consensus called for an end to violence, dialogue, mediation and humanitarian assistance, but implementation has been limited. Bordering states have their own interests in trade, refugees, security and access to resources. A ceasefire in one region can reduce immediate harm without resolving the nationwide political crisis.

## How the archive classifies Myanmar

This entry is the umbrella chronology for the post-coup nationwide conflict. It should link to regional records for Kachin, Karen and Karenni areas, Rakhine and the Arakan Army, the Rohingya crisis, and other ethnic theatres. Those entries are not automatically separate wars: the archive must explain whether each is a regional phase, a distinct actor dyad, one-sided violence, persecution or a humanitarian crisis linked to armed conflict.

UCDP’s annual datasets version 26.1 cover state-based conflict, dyadic conflict, non-state conflict and one-sided violence through 2025. Candidate event data for 2026 is provisional and can be revised. The archive must not treat all civilian deaths, criminal violence, protest repression and battle-related deaths as one interchangeable total. Its event audit remains a separate task from this narrative overview.
