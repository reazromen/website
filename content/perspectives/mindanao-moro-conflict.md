# Mindanao and the Moro Conflict: Autonomy, Peace Agreements, and Armed Splinter Groups

The Moro conflict in the southern Philippines grew from a long history of political marginalization, land dispossession and competing claims to self-determination among Muslim communities in Mindanao and the Sulu archipelago. The conflict has never been a single organization fighting continuously in the same way. The Moro National Liberation Front (MNLF), the Moro Islamic Liberation Front (MILF), government forces and later splinter groups have played different roles across different periods.

The peace process has transformed the political landscape, especially through the 2014 Comprehensive Agreement on the Bangsamoro and the establishment of the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM) in 2019. But autonomy is a political transition, not an instant end to every form of armed violence. Disarmament, reintegration, local security, land disputes and the authority of new institutions remain important questions.

## From separatist war to negotiated autonomy

The MNLF led an important phase of the Moro struggle and signed a 1996 peace agreement with the Philippine government. The MILF later became a central party to negotiations, culminating in the 2014 agreement and the legal framework for BARMM. These processes sought to replace armed confrontation with representative regional government, fiscal arrangements and a transition process that included decommissioning MILF combatants.

Implementation has been complex. Political authority is divided across local, regional and national institutions; armed networks do not all respond to the same leadership; and communities have different expectations of autonomy. Elections, governance reforms and the transition timetable can affect trust in the settlement. A signed agreement should be recorded as a major political milestone, but not treated as proof that every local conflict has ended.

## Splinter groups and extremist violence

Some armed factions rejected negotiations or continued operating outside the main peace framework. Other groups adopted the language or symbols of the Islamic State and conducted attacks that are analytically distinct from the Moro autonomy movement. The 2017 siege of Marawi, for example, involved pro-Islamic State militants and a large-scale government operation; it should have its own chronology rather than be folded into all Moro-related conflict.

Local clan feuds, known as rido, can also intersect with political competition, private armed groups and wider security operations. Not every violent incident in Mindanao is a continuation of the Moro insurgency. Attribution requires evidence about actors, motives and organizational links.

## Civilians, displacement and peace implementation

Civilians have experienced decades of displacement, disrupted schooling, property loss, land disputes and the risk of being caught between armed actors. Some communities have moved repeatedly as fighting flared or as local feuds escalated. Displacement data need a clear date and definition: people displaced by conflict, disasters and mixed causes cannot be combined without explanation.

The peace transition also creates opportunities and risks. Decommissioning weapons and reintegrating former combatants require livelihoods, credible security guarantees and local acceptance. If people perceive that promised services or political inclusion are not delivered, armed recruitment and local violence can remain attractive to some actors. The success of autonomy should therefore be evaluated through both formal milestones and lived security.

All parties remain responsible for protecting civilians under applicable law. Reports of extortion, recruitment, attacks, abuses in detention or violence against minorities should be documented with source, location and date. Broad ethnic or religious labels should never substitute for identifying the responsible actor.

## How the archive should count the conflict

This entry describes the broader Moro conflict and autonomy transition. It should link to, but not double-count, the MNLF and MILF phases, the Marawi siege and separate extremist campaigns. UCDP’s actor-dyad and conflict-year data can help identify the periods meeting state-based conflict criteria. One-sided violence and non-state conflict records should be reviewed separately, while displacement and rights violations remain distinct impact measures.

The 2026 picture must be treated cautiously until provisional event records are checked. A drop in organized battle activity does not mean that local armed violence, political intimidation or unresolved displacement has disappeared.
