# Iraq’s Insurgency and Sectarian War, 2003–2011

The fall of Saddam Hussein’s government in April 2003 did not end organized violence in Iraq. The invasion was followed by occupation, state restructuring, attacks on coalition forces and Iraqi institutions, the rise of armed factions, and a devastating period of sectarian killings. The term “Iraq insurgency” covers multiple organizations and motivations, not one unified rebellion. Some groups opposed foreign occupation; others sought sectarian dominance, pursued jihadist aims, or fought rival Iraqi actors.

## From invasion to insurgency

The US-led invasion began on 20 March 2003. The Iraqi military and government collapsed quickly, but the occupation faced looting, insecurity, shortages and a struggle to establish legitimate political authority. The Coalition Provisional Authority’s decisions to remove many Ba’ath Party members from public institutions and dissolve the Iraqi army remain debated, but they contributed to a rapid disruption of state employment and security capacity. Former soldiers, political opponents, nationalist networks and foreign jihadists entered a conflict whose objectives were far from uniform.

Insurgent attacks targeted coalition forces, police, government offices, infrastructure and civilians. Suicide bombings and improvised explosive devices became prominent tactics. Some organizations sought to make the occupation untenable; others attacked Iraqis who cooperated with the new authorities or belonged to rival religious communities. Treating every armed opponent as the same group obscures how these factions competed and sometimes fought one another.

## Sectarian escalation, 2006–2007

The February 2006 bombing of the al-Askari shrine in Samarra accelerated sectarian violence. Armed groups carried out mass-casualty attacks, kidnappings, executions and forced displacement. Sunni and Shia civilians were targeted by identity, neighborhood and perceived affiliation. Some security personnel and state institutions were accused of involvement in abuses or factional violence, further weakening public confidence.

The US troop surge in 2007, cooperation with Sunni tribal groups often described as the Awakening, changes in insurgent tactics and exhaustion among communities all contributed to a marked decline in violence. Analysts disagree about the relative weight of these factors. The decline did not mean that the conflict’s political problems had been settled: disputes over power-sharing, security, corruption, militia influence and the distribution of state resources continued.

## Withdrawal and the unfinished settlement

US forces withdrew from Iraq at the end of 2011 under the timetable then in force. Yet the political settlement remained fragile. Communities continued to distrust security institutions, armed networks retained influence, and unresolved grievances left space for later escalation. The Islamic State’s expansion after 2013 developed from a different phase of the wider post-2003 crisis and the Syrian war; it should be linked to this page without treating it as merely the same insurgency under a new name.

## Civilian cost and measurement

The conflict killed large numbers of civilians and combatants, displaced millions and damaged essential services. Estimates differ depending on whether they count documented violent deaths, excess mortality, missing people or a particular geographic and temporal window. A responsible account should never combine these measures without explanation. Civilians experienced not only direct attacks but also loss of housing, work, medical care, education and access to safe neighborhoods.

## Classification for the archive

This page covers the insurgency and sectarian-war phase from 2003 through 2011. It is related to, but distinct from, the invasion and occupation overview and the later Islamic State campaign. The archive treats those pages as linked phases of the post-2003 Iraqi conflict, not as three independent wars to be added together without qualification.
