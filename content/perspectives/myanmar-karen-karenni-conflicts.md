# Karen and Karenni Conflicts in Myanmar: Borderlands, Resistance, and Federalism

The Karen and Karenni conflicts are closely connected to Myanmar’s long-running struggle over ethnic equality, autonomy and state power, but they are not one interchangeable insurgency. Karen organizations are rooted largely in the southeast and borderlands adjoining Thailand. Karenni movements are associated with Kayah State and neighbouring areas. Each has its own leadership, constituencies, alliances and political history.

Both theatres predate the 2021 coup. The nationwide resistance that followed the military takeover brought new relationships and shared opponents, but it did not erase older disagreements about federalism, territory, representation and the authority of ethnic armed organizations.

## Karen armed politics

The Karen National Union (KNU) and its armed wing, the Karen National Liberation Army (KNLA), have pursued self-determination and political rights through a prolonged conflict with the central state. Fighting has involved shifting fronts, village displacement, military operations and periods of negotiation. The border with Thailand has shaped refuge, trade, humanitarian access and civilian movement.

The KNU has not been the only actor in Karen areas. Other armed groups and breakaway factions have had different relationships with the state and with the KNU. Describing all armed activity in Karen areas as one organization’s actions obscures both alliances and internal divisions.

Ceasefires and negotiations reduced fighting in some periods without delivering a comprehensive settlement. Political participation, security guarantees, territorial administration and the future structure of the armed forces remained contested. Local clashes can resume even when national negotiations appear to be progressing.

## Karenni forces after the coup

In Kayah State, Karenni organizations have included the Karenni National Progressive Party and its armed wing, the Karenni Army, alongside other local forces. After the 2021 coup, new resistance organizations emerged and worked with some established ethnic actors. The Karenni Nationalities Defence Force and local People’s Defence Forces became part of a changing military and political landscape.

Fighting expanded as opponents of military rule challenged state forces. The conflict has included attacks on military positions, air and artillery strikes, displacement and disruption to civilian services. Control of towns, roads and border approaches has shifted, while civilians face risks from explosive remnants, movement restrictions and limited access to healthcare and education.

Karen and Karenni actors may cooperate with nationwide resistance networks, but their political priorities and command structures remain important. A future settlement would need to address representation, federal power-sharing, local security and the relationship between regional forces and a national military.

## Civilian impact and archive method

Displacement is a defining feature of both theatres. People have fled villages and towns during offensives, returned when fighting eased and moved again when violence resumed. Some crossed into Thailand; others remain internally displaced in areas with difficult humanitarian access. These populations should not be combined without clear definitions and dates.

Civilian harm can result from airstrikes, shelling, small-arms exchanges, landmines, forced recruitment and abuses during security operations. Humanitarian agencies face access constraints, and independent verification can be difficult in remote border areas. Reports should distinguish confirmed incidents from estimates and allegations, identifying actors and sources wherever possible.

This entry groups Karen and Karenni theatres for regional navigation, not because they are a single conflict. The master register should retain separate subrecords for the KNU/KNLA, Karenni organizations and post-coup resistance coalitions whenever evidence supports that distinction. It should record overlap with the national Myanmar conflict without double-counting the same battle or civilian harm event.
