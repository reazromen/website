# The War in Darfur: Rebellion, Counterinsurgency, and Mass Displacement

The war in Darfur erupted in western Sudan in 2003, when rebel movements attacked government positions and challenged the political and economic marginalization of the region. The Sudanese government responded with military operations and support for Arab militia forces commonly known as the Janjaweed. The resulting violence included attacks on villages, killings, sexual violence, destruction of livelihoods, and mass displacement. Darfur became one of the defining humanitarian crises of the early twenty-first century.

Darfur's conflict cannot be explained as an ancient, inevitable struggle between Arabs and Africans. Communities in the region have complex identities, and relationships among farmers, pastoralists, local authorities, and political elites changed over time. Competition over land and water, drought and environmental pressure, weak state institutions, unequal development, and disputes over political representation formed part of the context. These factors did not automatically cause war; political decisions and the organization of armed force turned grievances into large-scale violence.

## Rebellion and the state's response

Two major rebel movements, the Sudan Liberation Army/Movement and the Justice and Equality Movement, emerged with different leadership structures and political priorities. They argued that Darfur had been excluded from power and resources. Their attacks challenged a central government already involved in other conflicts and concerned about territorial control.

Rather than relying only on regular forces, the government armed and supported militia groups. Human rights organizations and international investigators documented systematic attacks on civilian communities, including the burning of villages and forced displacement. Rebel groups also committed abuses. Recognizing abuses by multiple actors does not mean that their scale, organization, or command responsibility was identical. Careful analysis distinguishes documented patterns from claims about particular incidents and identifies what investigations could establish.

## Displacement and the limits of international response

Millions of people were displaced within Sudan or fled across the border into Chad. Camps and temporary settlements became long-term places of refuge, often with inadequate water, food, healthcare, and security. People who lost land, livestock, homes, and community networks faced barriers to returning even when fighting diminished. Sexual violence and attacks on civilians had lasting consequences for survivors and families.

The international response included humanitarian operations, sanctions, diplomatic pressure, and the deployment of an African Union mission, later replaced by the joint United Nations–African Union mission known as UNAMID. The International Criminal Court issued warrants for Sudanese President Omar al-Bashir, including charges of war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide. Legal proceedings and international action did not quickly end the violence, illustrating the gap between documenting abuses and preventing them.

## Peace agreements and a conflict that changed shape

The 2006 Darfur Peace Agreement was rejected by important rebel factions and did not establish a durable settlement. Later agreements and local arrangements reduced some forms of fighting but left armed groups, insecurity, and unresolved questions about land, accountability, and return. The overthrow of Omar al-Bashir in 2019 changed Sudan's national political context but did not resolve Darfur's local and national security problems.

In April 2023, a separate nationwide war began between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces. That conflict had its own immediate causes and command structures, but it overlapped with Darfur's earlier history and brought renewed mass violence and displacement to the region. It is important not to treat every phase as identical: the 2003 conflict, later Darfur violence, and the 2023 national war are connected historically but differ in participants and political context.

## What Darfur teaches

Darfur shows how political exclusion, competition over resources, and weak institutions can be transformed into mass violence when leaders organize armed groups and civilians are treated as instruments of war. It also shows that humanitarian access and peacekeeping can reduce some immediate dangers without resolving disputes over power, land, justice, and safe return. A lasting settlement requires civilian protection, accountability, political inclusion, and conditions under which displaced people can make a genuinely voluntary and safe decision about returning.
