Science is often described as a collection of facts.

That is the least interesting part.

Its deeper strength is procedural.

Measurements can be repeated. Methods can be inspected. Claims can be challenged. Models can be replaced. Data can be reanalyzed. Instruments can improve. Old conclusions can be narrowed or abandoned.

Science is a correction system.

## Error is expected

Scientists are human.

They make arithmetic mistakes, choose bad models, overfit data, misunderstand instruments, follow fashionable ideas, and sometimes behave badly.

If scientific reliability depended on individual infallibility, the project would have failed centuries ago.

Instead, science tries to externalize error detection.

A claim is not supposed to be trusted merely because a respected person made it.

The evidence and method should be available for others to test.

The ideal is stronger than actual practice, but the architecture matters.

## Replication is one correction channel

A result that cannot be reproduced loses credibility.

Replication can reveal chance findings, hidden conditions, fragile methods, and inflated estimates.

The process is imperfect. Replication is expensive. Incentives often favor novelty. Some experiments cannot be repeated exactly.

Still, the possibility of repetition changes what counts as a durable claim.

Knowledge has to survive movement.

## Criticism is productive infrastructure

Peer review is not a truth machine.

Reviewers miss errors.

Editors make judgment calls.

Bad papers get published and good papers get rejected.

The more important corrective mechanism often happens after publication: other researchers use the method, compare results, write critiques, collect larger datasets, and propose alternatives.

A scientific paper is not the end of an argument.

It is an address others can send evidence to.

## Instruments correct theories, and theories correct instruments

Sometimes a surprising result breaks a theory.

Sometimes it exposes a broken detector.

Distinguishing the two requires cross-checks.

Use another instrument.

Calibrate against a reference.

Predict a second effect.

Change the experimental design.

This mutual correction is powerful because theories tell us what instruments should see, while instruments constrain what theories are allowed to claim.

Neither side gets permanent authority.

## Corrections can be slow

Self-correcting does not mean instantly self-correcting.

Wrong ideas can persist.

Weak results can be cited for years.

Institutional incentives can resist change.

Textbooks lag behind research.

A scientific consensus can be incomplete.

The phrase "science corrects itself" should therefore not be used as magic absolution.

Correction requires people, incentives, archives, methods, and time.

Systems only correct if correction channels remain open.

## The record should preserve the mistake

Retractions, corrections, version histories, and updated datasets can look embarrassing.

They are also evidence that the correction mechanism is operating.

A field that never changes its mind is not necessarily more reliable.

It may simply hide error better.

Versioned knowledge is more honest than pretending the first publication was perfect.

## Engineering has the same architecture

Production engineering works best when incidents leave traces.

Postmortems.

Regression tests.

Version control.

Monitoring.

Rollback.

Runbooks.

Change review.

These are correction mechanisms.

They do not prevent every failure.

They make failure reusable.

A system that can learn from breakage becomes more reliable than one that merely hopes not to break.

## Reliability comes from revisability

The public sometimes interprets changing scientific guidance as evidence that science is weak.

Sometimes change does reveal earlier overconfidence.

But revision is also exactly what an evidence-based system should do when evidence changes.

A model that cannot be corrected is doctrine.

A model that can be corrected is a tool.

Science is powerful because its conclusions are provisional in a disciplined way.

The goal is not to be wrong less often by pretending certainty.

It is to build processes that make being wrong discoverable.
