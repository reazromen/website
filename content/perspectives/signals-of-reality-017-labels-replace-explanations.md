“The organization failed because it was dysfunctional.”

The sentence has the grammar of an explanation. An event appears before *because*, a cause after it. Yet if “dysfunctional” means only that the organization repeatedly fails, the sentence has traveled in a circle. The outcome has acquired a more impressive name and returned disguised as its own cause.

Not every explanation containing a label is circular. “The road flooded because the drain was blocked” names a condition that can be investigated independently of the flood. We can inspect the drain, trace the flow, and ask whether clearing the obstruction would change what happens during comparable rainfall. The explanatory work comes from those relationships. The word *blocked* is useful because it points toward them.

The difficulty is that a strong label and an empty one can sound equally complete.

### A name can arrive before a cause

Classifying a phenomenon is often an achievement. Before researchers understand why something occurs, they need ways to recognize it, compare cases, and avoid treating every occurrence as unrelated. A descriptive category can make a problem visible.

The history of peptic ulcer disease illustrates the distance between recognition and causal understanding. The lesions were known before the role of *Helicobacter pylori* was established. Barry Marshall and Robin Warren's work connected the bacterium with gastritis and peptic ulcer disease; the discovery was recognized by the 2005 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The [Nobel Assembly's account](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2005/press-release/) describes evidence that went beyond finding a suggestive name, including studies of infection and treatment.

This is not a story in which every ulcer acquired one universal explanation. Nor is it a claim that earlier descriptions were worthless. The useful contrast is between identifying a lesion and establishing a causal contribution to its development. A name can remain valid while the understanding behind it changes profoundly.

The same distinction matters outside medicine. An analyst might reliably identify a recurring pattern of demand without knowing what drives it. That classification can support forecasting. It does not automatically show what intervention would alter demand. Prediction and explanation can overlap, but neither should quietly inherit all the authority of the other.

### Open the noun

Consider an invented workplace example. A team misses several deadlines. Management calls it a “coordination problem.” That could be a useful provisional description. It could also be the last sentence in a report nobody learns from.

To open the label, describe a process. Perhaps each task requires two approvals, the approvers operate on different schedules, and work cannot proceed while either approval is pending. Delays then accumulate even when everyone performs their assigned role. This account identifies arrangements whose presence can be checked and whose effects can be compared.

It also permits error. If the deadlines are missed before any approval is requested, the proposed process cannot explain the whole pattern. If approvals are immediate but delivery remains slow, another constraint deserves attention. “Coordination problem” alone provides much less resistance to being used for everything.

Philosophers studying mechanisms examine explanations in terms of components, activities, and their organization. The aim is not necessarily to reduce a system to tiny physical parts. A mechanism can involve institutions and information flows as well as molecules. The [Stanford Encyclopedia's survey](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2025/entries/science-mechanisms/) also makes clear that mechanisms are a family of explanatory approaches, not a settled rule for every science.

A detailed story is still only a candidate. Inventing a plausible approval bottleneck is easy. Establishing that it operated in a particular organization requires records and comparisons. Replacing one noun with five paragraphs does not, by itself, improve the evidence.

### What the label permits us to miss

Suppose the same missed deadlines are instead attributed to “laziness.” If that term is inferred solely from late delivery, the circularity returns. But something else has changed: responsibility has been located inside the workers before the workflow has been investigated.

The problem is not that individual effort can never matter. It is that the word can bundle a behavioral description, a causal hypothesis, and a moral judgment into one compact package. A reader may accept the first and unknowingly accept the other two.

Try replacing it with a narrower statement: tasks remained untouched for two days after assignment. That statement still needs verification, but it preserves room for several explanations. The work might have been ignored. It might have depended on unavailable information. The task tracker might have failed to record activity. The narrow description makes the next question easier to locate.

Technical labels can conceal similar shifts. “Complexity” may refer to a measured property, a particular mathematical framework, or merely the speaker's difficulty understanding something. “Emergence” can introduce a precise account of collective behavior, or mark the point where such an account is missing. These words do not become illegitimate because they can be misused. Their value depends on what commitments accompany them.

For any proposed explanation, it helps to ask which neighboring outcome it explains. Why did this team miss the deadline while another met it? Why did the same process work last month? A label shared by both cases cannot alone explain their difference, though it may remain part of a larger account.

### Not everything needs a microscopic story

There is an opposite mistake: refusing to accept any explanation until every underlying detail is supplied.

Imagine a booking system with ten indivisible seats and eleven people who each require a seat on the same departure. The capacity constraint explains why they cannot all be accommodated. We do not need a molecular account of the seats. Nor do we yet know which person will be left without one; that requires information about allocation. The explanation is powerful within its scope and silent outside it.

This thought experiment shows why “tell me the mechanism” is a useful demand rather than a universal password. Sometimes a constraint, a probability model, or a mathematical relationship does the relevant work. What matters is that the account connects the outcome to something beyond a restatement of the outcome, and that its limits remain visible.

A short explanation can therefore be excellent. Experts use labels to compress extensive shared knowledge. In a conversation where everyone understands and accepts the relevant evidence, unpacking every term would obscure the point. The risk appears when a compressed explanation travels to an audience that cannot distinguish the contents from the packaging.

“Coordination problem” may be the right heading for the report. Underneath it should be the dependency that delayed the work, the evidence that it did so, and the conditions under which changing it would help. Without those, the heading is still waiting for its article.
