Eight dots, then sixteen. For an adult, the difference can be described with a number, a ratio, a sentence. A six-month-old infant cannot give any of those answers. An experiment must ask the question another way.

In a study published in 2000, Fei Xu and Elizabeth Spelke investigated infants' responses to arrays of dots while controlling continuous features that might otherwise explain discrimination. The infants distinguished eight from sixteen, but not eight from twelve, under the study's conditions. The result supported a capacity to represent approximate numerosity before language and symbolic counting. It did not show that infants silently recited numbers. [Xu and Spelke, “Large number discrimination in 6-month-old infants”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10594312/).

This is a small experimental opening into a large question. What remains of a world when its names have not yet arrived?

The answer cannot simply be “nothing.” The infant's behavior already responds to differences. But neither can an adult step backward out of language and deliver an unmediated report of infant experience. We have observations of behavior and competing ways to explain them. That gap is where care is needed.

A looking-time experiment is an inference-making device. Researchers arrange displays and compare responses. Longer looking can help reveal that a change has been detected, but it does not translate directly into an adult proposition such as “there are twice as many objects.” Attention is an outcome with possible explanations, not a spoken answer hidden inside the child.

This limitation does not make the evidence useless. It determines the claim the evidence can support. Showing a discrimination is already enough to challenge the strongest assertion that words must precede every meaningful distinction. It is not enough to settle how all concepts develop, or whether infant experience has the same organization as adult experience.

## Before naming is not before structure

The phrase “before language” folds together several different situations. There is a child's life before productive speech. There is an adult's activity without inner verbal narration. There is the evolutionary history before human languages. And there is the much longer physical history before any organism could speak.

These situations cannot be substituted for one another. An adult silently tying a knot has a history of instruction and practice; the absence of current words does not remove that history. An infant has sensory and bodily capacities that a rock lacks. A prehistoric physical process need not be an experience for anyone at all.

Keeping the cases apart prevents a seductive mistake: imagining the absence of language as one great, undifferentiated blank.

Consider an ordinary conceptual example. A person can fail to know the name of a tool while still noticing that its edge is sharp, avoiding that edge, and discovering that it cuts string. Several capacities are involved: perceptual discrimination, anticipation, practical learning. Naming can make the discovery easier to communicate, but communication is not identical to the discovery.

Conversely, someone can know a tool's name and have no idea how to use it. A vocabulary entry is not a complete practical relationship with the world.

Neither example proves a theory of nonlinguistic thought. They expose an ambiguity in the claim that language creates reality. Does “creates” mean brings physical things into existence, organizes attention, enables a particular concept, or makes knowledge publicly shareable? Those are four different proposals. Evidence for one does not establish the others.

## What words add

A name makes a distinction portable. Once two people have agreed on it, they can discuss an absent thing, teach a procedure, or record a mistake for someone who has not yet encountered it. A sentence can specify a condition: use the larger piece unless the slot is narrow. The instruction combines a comparison with an exception and carries both beyond the original demonstration.

This is more than attaching labels to a finished inventory. A new term can direct sustained attention toward a feature previously overlooked. It can also hide differences by placing unlike cases in the same category. Whether the effect improves understanding depends on the task and the category, not on naming alone.

Color research offers a testable case. In a 2007 experiment, Jonathan Winawer and colleagues compared Russian and English speakers on a speeded color-matching task. Russian makes a basic lexical distinction between lighter and darker blues that English groups under blue. Russian speakers showed a speed advantage for comparisons across that linguistic boundary. A concurrent verbal task eliminated the advantage; a spatial task did not. [“Russian blues reveal effects of language on color discrimination”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1876524/).

The result concerns performance under particular experimental conditions. It does not mean English speakers cannot distinguish the shades, or that Russian speakers receive different wavelengths from the same screen. It supports an influence of linguistic categories on the task, not the claim that words manufacture light.

Even the phrase “language changes perception” needs unpacking. A measured response can involve attention, categorization, memory and a decision as well as sensory processing. Establishing an effect is not automatically locating every mechanism responsible for it. That caution leaves room for language to matter substantially without turning the finding into a universal law.

There is also a difference between a useful category and a natural boundary. Imagine sorting a continuous row of paint samples into two named groups. The sorting rule may help people remember and coordinate. It need not reveal a sharp break in the physical series. If the task changes to matching a repaired wall, differences within each named group may become the ones that matter most.

The paint example is an analogy about classification, not a model of the visual system. Human color experience cannot be reduced to placing labels along a single physical scale.

Words therefore change what we can do with an experience, and sometimes how we perform while encountering it. They do not have to be the original source of every distinction to have that power.

Return to the infant and the dots. There is no need to award the infant a miniature adult arithmetic, and no reason to withhold every form of cognition until a number word appears. The evidence allows a more interesting middle ground: capacities can precede the language that later reorganizes, extends and communicates them.

A first word enters a life already responsive to the world. It opens new possibilities for that responsiveness to become a question, an explanation, an instruction, or a claim someone else can challenge.
