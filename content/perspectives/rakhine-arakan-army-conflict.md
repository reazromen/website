# Rakhine and the Arakan Army: Autonomy, Territorial Control, and Civilian Risk

Rakhine State in western Myanmar has experienced a shifting conflict involving the Myanmar military and the Arakan Army (AA), the armed wing of the United League of Arakan. The AA seeks greater autonomy for Rakhine and has built military and administrative capacity over time. Its conflict with the state is connected to Myanmar’s wider ethnic wars, but Rakhine’s political history, geography and communal tensions require their own chronology.

The Rohingya crisis is closely linked to events in Rakhine, yet it is not synonymous with the Arakan Army’s conflict. Rohingya persecution and mass displacement predate the recent territorial campaigns. The archive must record attacks against civilians, forced displacement and restrictions on aid separately from battle events, while explaining how these harms interact.

## From expansion to renewed war

The Arakan Army grew into a significant armed actor during the 2010s. Fighting with the military intensified in 2018 and 2019, with clashes, arrests, displacement and disruption to civilian life. A ceasefire around the 2020 election period reduced major fighting for a time, but it did not settle autonomy or political authority.

The February 2021 coup changed the national setting, while the AA pursued its own political and territorial strategy. Fighting resumed on a large scale in late 2023 after the Rakhine ceasefire collapsed. The AA then made substantial territorial gains against the military, challenging its control across much of the state.

Territorial control is complex. Control of a township, road or border crossing does not necessarily mean all surrounding villages are governed in the same way. A group’s ability to administer an area can change access to services, taxation and security, but does not settle the legitimacy of its authority or the rights of minority residents.

## Civilians caught between armed actors

Renewed fighting has exposed civilians to airstrikes, shelling, restrictions on movement, property destruction and repeated displacement. Humanitarian access has been limited in parts of Rakhine, and communications restrictions complicate independent verification. The conflict affects Rakhine Buddhists, Rohingya and other communities, though risks differ by location and identity.

Rohingya people face a distinct history of statelessness and systematic discrimination. During renewed fighting, reporting has raised concerns about killings, forced recruitment, displacement and abuses attributed to different armed actors, including the military and the AA. Such claims should be attributed to the organization documenting them, with dates and locations preserved. They should neither be treated as proven solely because alleged nor dismissed because access is restricted.

All parties must distinguish civilians from combatants, take feasible precautions and facilitate humanitarian relief as required by applicable international humanitarian law. Protection of Rohingya civilians cannot depend on citizenship status or the actions of armed groups claiming to represent them.

## Governance and the future of Rakhine

The conflict concerns more than battlefield gains. Rakhine’s relationship with the central state, access to natural resources, border trade and local administration all shape the stakes. The AA’s expansion raises questions about policing, courts, taxation and the treatment of communities under its authority.

A durable settlement would need to address federal arrangements and the rights of all communities. A ceasefire alone would not resolve statelessness, displacement, property claims or humanitarian access. For Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh, any return must be voluntary, safe and dignified; a change in military control is not by itself evidence that conditions for return exist.

## Classification and overlap

This article is the regional record for the Rakhine–AA theatre. It links to the nationwide Myanmar war and the Rohingya persecution and displacement entry, but the three should not be counted mechanically as three separate wars. The archive should identify actor dyads and events, record one-sided violence separately, and avoid adding displacement totals to death totals.

UCDP annual datasets through 2025 provide one structured reference for organized conflict and violence against civilians. Candidate event data for 2026 is provisional. A source-level audit remains necessary to classify events and resolve overlap across linked pages.
