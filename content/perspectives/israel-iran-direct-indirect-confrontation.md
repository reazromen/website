# Israel and Iran: Direct Exchanges, Regional Networks, and Escalation Risk

Israel and Iran have confronted each other for decades through hostile rhetoric, intelligence operations, cyber activity, attacks attributed to state-linked actors and support for armed partners across the region. The relationship changed significantly in 2024, when the two states exchanged direct attacks openly. Those episodes marked a shift from a pattern dominated by indirect confrontation, but they did not make every conflict involving Iran-aligned groups a single Israel–Iran war.

## A network of related theatres

Iran has supported armed and political organizations in Lebanon, Iraq, Yemen and the Palestinian territories, with the form and extent of support varying by group and period. Israel has targeted Iranian personnel and infrastructure in Syria and elsewhere and has described these actions as efforts to prevent weapons transfers or threats from consolidating near its borders. Iran has framed many of Israel’s actions as violations of sovereignty and has supported groups it describes as resistance movements. These positions should be attributed to the parties, not treated as neutral descriptions.

The actors involved have their own leadership, domestic constituencies and strategic interests. Hezbollah, Houthi forces, Iraqi armed factions and Palestinian organizations should not be assumed to operate as a single command structure. Nor should every attack by a partner group be automatically attributed to Tehran without evidence.

## Direct exchanges in 2024 and later risk

In April 2024, Iran launched a large drone and missile attack toward Israel after a strike on an Iranian diplomatic compound in Damascus that Iran attributed to Israel. Israel and partner states intercepted many incoming weapons. Israel later carried out a direct strike on Iran, and further exchanges in October 2024 intensified the confrontation. These events demonstrated how quickly a regional shadow conflict could become direct state-to-state violence.

The later trajectory is time-sensitive. Any claim about a new round of strikes, ceasefire, casualty total or damage assessment in 2025–2026 must be supported by dated sources. A historical overview should not imply that a temporary pause is a permanent settlement or that every regional incident is part of a single campaign.

## Civilian protection and escalation management

Direct attacks create risks to civilians, aviation, energy infrastructure and densely populated areas. Interceptions can reduce casualties but do not remove the obligation to distinguish military objectives from civilian objects or to take precautions. The risk of miscalculation increases when multiple armed actors operate across several countries and public claims about responsibility are made before evidence is available.

De-escalation depends on communication channels, clear limits on targeting, respect for sovereignty and arrangements that address the security concerns of states and communities. Durable stability also requires political solutions in the local conflicts that regional actors influence.
