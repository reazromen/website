# The Rohingya Crisis: Persecution, Mass Displacement, and Armed Violence

The Rohingya crisis cannot be reduced to a single military campaign or a single refugee movement. It combines long-running discrimination and denial of citizenship in Myanmar, episodes of communal and state violence, the mass expulsion of Rohingya people to Bangladesh in 2017, and renewed armed conflict in Rakhine State after 2023. The Rohingya are a predominantly Muslim minority whose historical ties to Rakhine have been contested by nationalist narratives. Myanmar authorities have generally denied the community’s recognized ethnic status, and citizenship restrictions have left many Rohingya stateless.

For the conflict archive, it is essential to distinguish persecution, forced displacement, attacks on civilians, organized armed conflict and refugee protection. These categories overlap in people’s lives but are not identical statistical measures. The Rohingya crisis is also connected to the wider Myanmar civil war, without being reducible to the post-2021 coup alone.

## Structural exclusion and earlier violence

For decades, Rohingya communities faced restrictions on movement, marriage, education, access to healthcare and political participation. The 1982 citizenship law and its implementation created severe barriers to recognized citizenship for many Rohingya. Periodic operations and communal violence drove waves of displacement into Bangladesh and elsewhere, including in 1978 and 1991–1992. The lack of secure legal status left the population vulnerable to arbitrary restrictions and made it difficult to access services or pursue remedies.

The history is often presented through competing national narratives, but historical disagreement does not justify collective punishment or denial of basic rights. Rohingya civilians are not interchangeable with armed groups that claim to represent them, and attacks by militants do not remove the state’s obligations toward the population.

## The 2016–2017 attacks and mass exodus

In October 2016 and August 2017, attacks by the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA) on security posts were followed by large-scale security operations by Myanmar’s military. In 2017, operations involved widespread killings, sexual violence, village burnings and the forced flight of hundreds of thousands of Rohingya to Bangladesh. The UN human-rights investigators described patterns of grave violations and concluded that the evidence warranted investigation of senior military officials for the gravest international crimes. Myanmar’s authorities disputed many allegations; those responses must be recorded, but they do not erase the extensive documentation by survivors, investigators and independent organizations.

More than 700,000 Rohingya fled to Bangladesh in the months following August 2017, joining refugees from earlier waves. The refugee population became concentrated in and around Cox’s Bazar, including the Kutupalong-Balukhali settlement complex. The exodus created one of the world’s largest refugee situations, with major needs for shelter, food, healthcare, education, sanitation and protection from trafficking and gender-based violence.

The number of refugees changes with registration, births, deaths and movement. It should not be confused with the number who fled in the initial 2017 wave, nor with Rohingya who remained inside Myanmar or later crossed borders. Any figure should carry a date and source.

## Rakhine after the 2021 coup

The military coup in February 2021 changed the national political environment, but the Rohingya situation followed its own trajectory. A ceasefire between the Myanmar military and the Arakan Army reduced some fighting in Rakhine for a period; hostilities resumed in late 2023 and expanded dramatically. The Arakan Army captured territory and challenged military control, while civilians of multiple communities faced shelling, airstrikes, displacement and restrictions on movement and aid.

Rohingya civilians were caught between armed actors and within a conflict economy marked by coercion and insecurity. Human-rights reporting covering June 2025 to May 2026 raised serious concerns about abuses by both the Myanmar military and the Arakan Army, including killings, torture, forced recruitment and displacement. Communication restrictions and limited access make verification difficult. Such allegations need to be attributed carefully, while avoiding the false implication that every member of an armed organization or community has the same responsibility.

The renewed conflict has also produced additional displacement from Rakhine and neighbouring areas. Some Rohingya have attempted dangerous journeys by sea or land, including from Bangladesh, as insecurity, limited livelihood options and restrictions in refugee camps continue. These journeys should not be treated as evidence that refugee protection is unnecessary; they show how prolonged displacement without durable solutions can generate further risk.

## Bangladesh and the long refugee emergency

Bangladesh has hosted large Rohingya refugee populations for decades and received the overwhelming majority of those who fled in 2017. Refugees have generally lacked the legal right to work, and access to formal education and livelihoods remains constrained. Aid reductions, overcrowding, fire risk, monsoon hazards and insecurity inside camps have compounded the crisis. Host communities in Cox’s Bazar also face pressure on land, services and local livelihoods.

UNHCR’s refugee data reported approximately 1.2 million registered Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh as of 30 June 2026. That is a dated operational estimate and may differ from other counts based on registration methods or coverage. Refugees’ rights to seek asylum and protection from forced return remain central; non-refoulement prohibits returning people to a place where they face a real risk of persecution or other serious harm.

Repatriation cannot be considered voluntary and sustainable merely because a political announcement has been made. Conditions would need to include safety, dignity, freedom of movement, access to citizenship or secure legal status, livelihoods, education, property and meaningful choice. Refugees should not be pressured to return to conditions where the causes of flight remain unresolved.

## Accountability and legal distinctions

The UN Independent Investigative Mechanism for Myanmar collects and analyses evidence of serious international crimes for use in future proceedings. The International Court of Justice case brought by The Gambia concerns state responsibility under the Genocide Convention; provisional measures are not the same as a final merits judgment. The International Criminal Court has also pursued jurisdictional questions relating to alleged crimes involving deportation from Myanmar into Bangladesh. Each proceeding has a distinct mandate, parties and legal threshold.

A careful archive must distinguish a finding by a UN fact-finding body, a prosecutor’s allegation, a court’s provisional order and a final judgment. It should also separate documented violence from estimates of indirect mortality, demographic change or the long-term consequences of deprivation.

## Classification in the archive

This entry focuses on persecution, displacement and organized violence affecting Rohingya people. It cross-links to the Myanmar nationwide conflict and the Rakhine/Arakan Army theatre. It does not treat all Rohingya displacement as battle deaths, nor does it treat the refugee crisis as a separate war. Events should be coded by date, location, perpetrator where supported, form of violence, source and degree of verification.

UCDP state-based and non-state conflict datasets help identify organized armed fighting; one-sided violence datasets address deliberate attacks on civilians. Neither dataset alone captures statelessness, restrictions on movement, all forms of persecution or the full humanitarian impact of refugee life. Those records must be integrated without adding unlike categories into a misleading total.
