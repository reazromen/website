# The Philippine Communist Insurgency Since 2000: Negotiations, Rural Conflict, and Fragmentation

The communist insurgency in the Philippines is one of the world’s longest-running internal armed conflicts. The New People’s Army (NPA), the armed wing associated with the Communist Party of the Philippines (CPP), has fought the Philippine state since the late 1960s. Since 2000, the conflict has passed through renewed peace efforts, shifting military campaigns and political fragmentation. It is not the same conflict as the Moro struggle in Mindanao, although both affect parts of the southern Philippines and can overlap geographically.

The NPA’s stated revolutionary objective, its local armed structures and the government’s counterinsurgency strategy have changed over time. A national label can hide substantial variation among provinces: local alliances, land disputes, mining and logging, political rivalries and the presence of state forces shape what happens in particular communities.

## Negotiations and renewed fighting

Peace talks have opened and stalled repeatedly. The 2016–2017 negotiations under President Rodrigo Duterte raised hopes of a settlement, but they collapsed amid disputes over ceasefires, releases of political prisoners and the sequencing of substantive agreements. The government later ended formal negotiations and intensified its counterinsurgency framework. Other administrations have also used combinations of military operations, local reconciliation programmes and political pressure.

These policy changes matter, but they do not by themselves tell us whether the insurgency is declining or ending. Announcements of surrenders may use different definitions; combat deaths, arrests, defections and demobilization are not interchangeable measures. A credible assessment should compare incident-level records and independently documented organizational capacity rather than rely on government or rebel claims alone.

## Local conflict and civilian risk

Clashes between the NPA and state forces have affected rural communities, including farmers, Indigenous peoples, local officials and workers in extractive industries. Landmines and improvised explosive devices, checkpoints, military presence and allegations of coercion can make daily life dangerous. Human-rights organizations have also raised concerns about killings, red-tagging, arbitrary detention and threats against activists. Each allegation requires attribution and careful documentation; neither the label of insurgent nor that of activist is proof of involvement in an armed group.

The NPA has been accused of extortion, recruitment of minors and attacks affecting civilians. Such allegations should be evaluated individually and distinguished from combat with state forces. International humanitarian law applies to parties where the legal threshold for armed conflict is met; human-rights obligations and domestic law also remain relevant. Civilian protection cannot be conditional on political affiliation.

## A fragmented conflict system

The conflict is not static. Command relationships, regional fronts and the willingness of local units to follow national negotiating decisions have varied. A national ceasefire announcement may not stop every local incident, and an isolated attack does not establish that a regional command remains capable of sustained warfare. The archive should record dates, locations, actors and source quality instead of merging all incidents under one undifferentiated insurgency label.

The NPA conflict also needs to be separated from other armed violence in the Philippines. The Moro conflict has its own history of autonomy and self-determination, while the Marawi siege in 2017 was a distinct urban battle involving pro-Islamic State groups and state forces. These events may belong in a common national archive, but they should not be added together as if they were one war.

## Classification and data limits

For the archive, this is a national insurgency overview with regional subrecords where evidence supports them. UCDP state-based conflict data can help establish years and actor dyads; non-state conflict and one-sided violence datasets capture other forms of organized violence. None alone measures displacement, intimidation or all human-rights violations.

Casualty totals vary by source, time period and definition. The archive should not publish a cumulative death figure without a documented method and date range. Annual datasets through 2025 should be audited against the relevant codebooks, while 2026 event reporting remains provisional.
