When astronomers confirm another planet around a distant star, the discovery can sound almost familiar. A new world has joined the catalogue; its radius has been estimated, its orbit has been measured, and its host star has acquired another entry in a growing database. Yet the word *world* carries a weight that the measurement itself does not. It invites us to imagine landscapes, oceans, weather and perhaps living things, even when the available evidence tells us very little about what the surface of that planet is actually like.

In October 2026, NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite, or TESS, crossed a remarkable threshold. The NASA Exoplanet Archive reported 1,012 confirmed planets discovered using TESS data on 15 October, within a wider catalogue of 6,451 confirmed exoplanets. The milestone is not simply a larger number. It represents years of observations, follow-up measurements, independent checks and the work of a scientific community turning small changes in starlight into evidence for worlds that cannot be seen as ordinary images.

TESS often finds planets through the transit method. When a planet passes in front of its star from our viewpoint, the star's measured brightness falls by a tiny amount. A repeating dip can reveal an orbit and help estimate the planet's size relative to its star. But a transit is a geometric event, not a photograph. It tells us that something blocks a fraction of starlight at regular intervals; it does not, by itself, tell us whether the planet has continents, a deep atmosphere, liquid water or biology.

That distinction becomes important when we hear the phrase *Earth-like*. A planet can be similar to Earth in radius and still be radically different in temperature, chemistry, geological history or atmospheric pressure. A small planet close to its star may be intensely irradiated. Another may orbit a dim red dwarf and be tidally locked. A world inside a star's habitable zone receives an amount of energy that could, under some atmospheric conditions, permit liquid water on its surface. The zone is a useful starting point, not a certificate of habitability.

The next stage of the work is therefore less like collecting addresses and more like learning how to read a place from a distance. Astronomers combine transit measurements with radial-velocity observations to estimate mass and density. They use spectroscopy to investigate atmospheric gases, while remaining careful about clouds, stellar activity and instrumental limits. The James Webb Space Telescope can study some exoplanet atmospheres, but even a spectrum is an interpretation that must survive alternative explanations.

This is why a thousand confirmed planets do not amount to a thousand possible homes. They are a thousand additions to the evidence about how planetary systems form and vary. They help researchers understand which kinds of planets are common, which are difficult to detect and which nearby systems deserve closer attention. The catalogue also exposes the limits of our search: the planets we know are shaped by the sensitivity, observing strategy and follow-up resources of our instruments.

There is a quiet scientific virtue in refusing to turn possibility into certainty. We have one confirmed example of a living planet: Earth. That does not make life elsewhere unlikely or impossible; it means that our sample of inhabited worlds is still one. A single example is enough to show that chemistry can become biology, but not enough to tell us how often that transition occurs.

Perhaps the most interesting part of the TESS milestone is not that we have found so many planets. It is that the distance between discovery and understanding has become visible. We can detect a world before we know what it is like. We can estimate its size before we know its chemistry. We can identify promising environments without knowing whether they have ever hosted a living cell.

The search for life beyond Earth will not be settled by a dramatic count on a chart. It will depend on patient measurements, competing hypotheses and the ability to recognise when an observation is genuinely difficult to explain without biology. Until then, every confirmed planet is neither a second Earth nor an empty rock by default. It is a place about which we have learned one more thing, and a reminder of how much remains unknown.
