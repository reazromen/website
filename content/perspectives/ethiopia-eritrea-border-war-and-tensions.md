# Ethiopia–Eritrea: The Border War’s Final Phase and Later Tensions

Ethiopia and Eritrea fought a devastating interstate war from 1998 to 2000 over territory, sovereignty and unresolved border demarcation. Because the Global Armed Conflicts Archive begins in 2000, the main war largely falls outside its time window. The final fighting, the June 2000 cessation-of-hostilities agreement and subsequent border tensions are relevant, but they must not be represented as a continuous war lasting to the present.

## The 2000 endgame

Heavy fighting continued in the first half of 2000 before the parties agreed to halt hostilities in June. A UN peacekeeping mission was later deployed to monitor the temporary security zone. The Algiers Agreement, signed in December 2000, established a framework for ending the war and adjudicating the border. The legal and political dispute persisted even after large-scale combat stopped.

The border commission’s decision did not immediately produce a mutually implemented settlement. For years, relations remained hostile and the border heavily militarized. That condition is often described as “no war, no peace,” but political hostility and military readiness are not themselves evidence of active armed conflict on every day of that period.

## Rapprochement and renewed complexity

In 2018, Ethiopia and Eritrea announced a peace declaration and restored formal relations, opening the border temporarily and creating expectations of a new regional order. Subsequent developments, including the Tigray war and changing alliances in the Horn of Africa, made the relationship more complex. Claims about Eritrean military participation in later conflicts must be tied to specific locations, dates and credible evidence; they should not be folded into the 1998–2000 war automatically.

## How the archive handles scope

The archive records the 2000 end of the interstate war and any later, independently documented border clashes as discrete events. It cross-links to the Tigray war and wider Horn of Africa security context, but keeps their combatants and timelines distinct. This avoids extending the original war beyond the period supported by evidence.
