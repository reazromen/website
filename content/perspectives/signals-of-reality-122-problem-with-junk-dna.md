For years, popular explanations treated much of the genome as junk.

Then a correction arrived:

Actually, non-coding DNA is functional.

Then that correction was exaggerated into a new slogan:

Almost everything must have a purpose.

Both moves are too simple.

The National Human Genome Research Institute defines non-coding DNA as genomic sequence that does not code for amino acids and notes that some non-coding sequences have known functional roles while others have no known function.

That cautious wording matters.

## Protein-coding is not the definition of useful

Only a small fraction of the human genome directly codes for proteins.

But non-coding DNA includes many important elements.

Regulatory sequences.

Non-coding RNAs.

Introns.

Structural regions.

Origins and elements involved in chromosome behavior.

Some sequences influence when, where, and how strongly genes are expressed.

Calling all non-coding DNA junk because it does not encode protein confuses one molecular function with all biological function.

## Biochemical activity is not automatically organismal function

The opposite error appears when any detectable binding, transcription, or chromatin mark is treated as proof that a sequence has an evolved biological function.

Cells are chemically busy.

Molecules interact.

Transcription can be noisy.

A region can show biochemical activity without that activity being important to fitness or organismal phenotype.

The word *function* needs a definition.

Does it mean biochemical activity?

Evolutionary conservation?

A contribution to phenotype?

A sequence maintained by selection?

Different research questions use different criteria.

## Evolution allows baggage

Genomes are historical objects.

Transposable elements expand.

Duplications occur.

Sequences decay.

Some material can persist because removing it provides little advantage.

Evolution does not pay a storage bill per nucleotide in the way a cloud customer pays per gigabyte.

If a sequence is not harmful enough to be removed by selection, it can remain.

Persistence does not prove purpose.

## "Junk" also changes how we investigate

Language shapes attention.

If a region is assumed useless, researchers may ignore it.

If every region is assumed purposeful, researchers may overinterpret weak signals.

Both biases distort investigation.

The better starting point is empirical:

What happens when this sequence is altered?

Is it conserved?

When is it transcribed?

Which molecules interact with it?

Does the effect replicate?

Under which conditions?

Function should be earned by evidence.

## Context can reveal hidden roles

A sequence may appear dispensable under laboratory conditions and matter under stress, development, infection, or another environment.

That is a real limitation of experiments.

"No detected function" means no function detected under tested conditions with current methods.

It does not justify "definitely useless."

But uncertainty cuts both ways.

Unknown function does not justify "secretly essential."

## The genome is not an optimized manual

The software metaphor encourages the idea that every character exists because it was intentionally put there.

Evolutionary genomes do not have that architecture.

They are mixtures of constrained sequence, regulatory structure, neutral variation, repetitive elements, parasitic sequence, decayed remnants, and innovations.

The proportions and roles are active research questions.

The mess is part of the history.

## Replace the binary with evidence

Protein-coding versus junk is too coarse.

Functional versus useless can also be too coarse.

A better vocabulary asks:

functional in what sense?

in which cell?

at which developmental stage?

under which environment?

with what measurable consequence?

under what evolutionary constraint?

The problem with "junk DNA" is not merely that the phrase was rude.

It encouraged us to classify before measuring.

The correction should not be another absolute slogan.

It should be a better question.
