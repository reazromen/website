# The Russo-Ukrainian War Since 2014: From Crimea and Donbas to Full-Scale Invasion

The Russo-Ukrainian war is best understood as one connected interstate conflict whose methods, geographic scale, intensity and international consequences changed sharply over time. It began in 2014 with Russia’s seizure and annexation of Crimea and the outbreak of fighting in eastern Ukraine. On 24 February 2022, Russia launched a full-scale invasion across multiple fronts. The second phase transformed a war that had largely centred on Crimea and the Donbas into a nationwide campaign involving ground offensives, long-range strikes, occupation, mass displacement and extensive international military and economic support for Ukraine.

These phases are connected, but they are not interchangeable. Crimea, the war in Donbas from 2014 to 2022, and the full-scale invasion each have their own chronology and operational characteristics. They should be linked in the archive without being counted as three unrelated wars or flattened into one undifferentiated timeline.

## 2014: Crimea and the collapse of the post-Soviet status quo

The crisis escalated after Ukraine’s 2013–2014 political upheaval and the removal of President Viktor Yanukovych. Russian forces took control of key sites in Crimea in February and March 2014. A referendum organized under Russian military control was followed by Russia’s declaration that Crimea had been annexed. Ukraine and the overwhelming majority of states did not recognize the annexation; the UN General Assembly affirmed Ukraine’s territorial integrity and called on states not to recognize changes to Crimea’s status based on that referendum.

In eastern Ukraine, armed groups seized government buildings and proclaimed separatist entities in parts of Donetsk and Luhansk. Kyiv launched what it called an anti-terrorist operation. Fighting developed between Ukrainian government forces and Russia-backed separatist forces, while Russia’s role and the extent of direct Russian military involvement became central issues in international investigations and diplomacy. The conflict was never merely a local dispute: the movement of weapons, personnel, political support and military power across the border shaped the balance on the ground.

## The Donbas war, 2014–2022

The fighting in Donetsk and Luhansk included battles for cities and transport routes, artillery exchanges, trench warfare and repeated attempts to establish ceasefires. The downing of Malaysia Airlines Flight MH17 on 17 July 2014 killed all 298 people aboard and became one of the conflict’s most consequential international incidents. The disaster and the wider war generated investigations, criminal proceedings, sanctions and diplomatic confrontation.

The Minsk agreements of 2014 and 2015 sought to stop the fighting and establish a political process. They reduced the intensity of some periods but did not deliver a durable settlement. The line of contact hardened, civilians continued to be killed and injured, and communities were divided by checkpoints, mine contamination, damaged infrastructure and restrictions on movement. Negotiations repeatedly failed to resolve the conflict’s core disputes over security, territorial control, political status and the sequencing of measures.

Civilian casualty totals depend on the period and definition used. The UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission documented thousands of conflict-related civilian deaths before February 2022, while noting that verified counts do not necessarily capture every death. The European Court of Human Rights later discussed the scale of verified civilian harm and the evidence concerning military attacks during the period under review. A precise account should identify the date range and source rather than blend figures from different reporting systems.

## February 2022: the full-scale invasion

On 24 February 2022, Russian forces began a full-scale invasion from several directions, including from Russian territory, occupied Crimea and Belarus. Russian leadership announced military objectives that changed in emphasis over time; Ukraine described the operation as an invasion aimed at subjugating the country and defending its sovereignty. The invasion was widely condemned by the UN General Assembly as aggression against Ukraine.

Russian forces advanced toward Kyiv in the north, attacked in the east and south, and attempted to seize major urban centres. Ukrainian resistance prevented the capture of Kyiv. Russian forces withdrew from northern Ukraine in spring 2022 and concentrated more heavily on eastern and southern fronts. The war then developed into a prolonged campaign marked by artillery, missiles, drones, fortified lines, assaults on towns, attacks on energy infrastructure and repeated efforts by both sides to alter the front.

Territorial control changed at substantial human cost. Ukraine regained areas around Kyiv and later liberated territory in parts of Kharkiv and Kherson regions. Russia continued to occupy Crimea and parts of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson regions, claiming annexation of four regions in 2022 despite not controlling all of their territory. These claims have not been broadly recognized internationally. Statements about control must be dated: front lines are not static, and a map from one month can be misleading when used to describe another.

## Civilian harm, occupation and displacement

The full-scale invasion has caused large-scale civilian death and injury, damage to housing and public services, and displacement within Ukraine and across its borders. The UN has documented unlawful killings, arbitrary detention, enforced disappearance, torture and ill-treatment, conflict-related sexual violence and attacks affecting civilian infrastructure. Reports also document abuses against prisoners of war and civilians by different parties; each allegation requires specific attribution and evidence rather than a presumption that all incidents are equivalent.

Verified UN casualty counts are minimums because they include only cases that investigators can confirm. Access restrictions, occupation, destroyed records and ongoing fighting complicate verification. Estimates from governments, media, NGOs and international organizations may use different methods and time windows. This archive therefore avoids presenting one undated casualty number as a final total.

Occupation creates additional risks beyond direct battlefield casualties. Residents may face coercion, restrictions on expression and movement, pressure over identity and citizenship, property seizure, detention and disruption of education. The treatment of deported or transferred children and the return of displaced people have become major international legal and humanitarian issues. These questions should be evaluated through documentary evidence and legal findings, not used as shorthand for the entire experience of every person living under occupation.

## International support and the wider confrontation

Ukraine has received military, financial and humanitarian assistance from a coalition of states, while Russia has obtained varying forms of material and diplomatic support from partners. Sanctions, export controls, energy policy, defence production and military aid have become part of the wider confrontation. This international dimension matters, but it should not turn every supporting state into a direct belligerent by definition. The legal classification of participation depends on the conduct and facts involved.

The conflict has also reshaped European security, NATO planning, arms procurement and debates about deterrence. Yet these broader consequences are analytically distinct from the battlefield record. The archive treats foreign assistance as a relationship layer linked to the primary war, rather than a separate war that duplicates the same events and casualties.

## Law, accountability and contested narratives

The UN Charter prohibits the use of force against another state’s territorial integrity or political independence, subject to narrow exceptions. The international legal response to Russia’s actions has included UN General Assembly resolutions, sanctions, proceedings before international courts and investigations into alleged war crimes. Court decisions and arrest warrants have specific legal scope: they should be described accurately and not as if every allegation has already resulted in a final criminal conviction.

Both governments use strategic communication to shape perceptions of the war. Official statements, videos and battlefield claims may be useful evidence leads, but they require corroboration. Casualty claims, allegations of atrocities and assertions about territorial control should be assessed against independent investigations, satellite imagery, verified video, court records and contemporaneous reporting. Propaganda analysis should not become false equivalence: the quality and weight of evidence differ by claim and source.

## How the archive separates the phases

This master entry covers the connected Russo-Ukrainian war from 2014 onward. The archive should maintain separate linked essays for the 2014–2022 Donbas war and Russia’s full-scale invasion from 2022. The Crimea operation and annexation require their own legal and chronological treatment, while foreign military assistance belongs in a cross-index. This structure preserves continuity without double-counting battles, deaths or interventions.

The archive’s dataset audit remains a separate task. UCDP state-based conflict records, dyad-year records, event data and one-sided-violence records use defined thresholds and coding rules. They do not automatically align with everyday labels such as “war,” nor do they replace legal judgments. The 2026 portion of this essay must be updated against dated reporting and provisional event data rather than presented as a final annual record.
