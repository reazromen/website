# Russia’s Full-Scale Invasion of Ukraine Since February 2022

On 24 February 2022, Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine, expanding the war that had begun in 2014 into a multi-front international armed conflict across a much larger part of the country. Russian forces attacked from several directions, including from Russian territory, occupied Crimea and Belarus. Ukraine resisted, and the war evolved from an attempted rapid campaign toward Kyiv into a prolonged struggle involving fortified fronts, artillery, missiles, drones, occupation and repeated attacks on civilian infrastructure.

The invasion is a distinct phase of the Russo-Ukrainian war, not an isolated event separate from the seizure of Crimea and the fighting in Donbas since 2014. Its operational scale and nationwide impact, however, require their own chronology and evidence record.

## The opening campaign and the defence of Kyiv

The opening phase involved Russian advances toward Kyiv and other major cities, as well as operations in southern and eastern Ukraine. The attempt to take the capital did not succeed. Ukrainian resistance, Russian logistical problems, and the difficulty of sustaining a rapid advance under fire contributed to the failure of the northern campaign. In late March and early April 2022, Russian forces withdrew from northern Ukraine and shifted their main effort toward the east and south.

The withdrawal revealed extensive destruction and evidence of civilian killings in areas that had been occupied. The UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission investigated cases in Kyiv, Chernihiv and Sumy regions and reported that Russian troops had summarily executed or otherwise killed civilians in circumstances that may constitute war crimes. Each incident requires evidence-based assessment, but the findings established that the civilian consequences of the opening campaign were not limited to collateral damage from front-line combat.

In the south, Russian forces captured or occupied territory including areas around Kherson and advanced toward Mariupol. The siege and destruction of Mariupol became emblematic of the war’s effects on densely populated urban areas. Access restrictions, missing records and the scale of damage make complete casualty counts difficult.

## The eastern and southern fronts

After the northern campaign, the war became concentrated in eastern and southern Ukraine. Russian forces sought to expand control in Donetsk and Luhansk regions and consolidate a land corridor connecting Russia with occupied Crimea. Battles around Severodonetsk and Lysychansk in 2022, and later around Bakhmut, Avdiivka and other front-line towns, involved prolonged artillery exchanges, repeated assaults and extensive destruction.

Ukraine conducted counteroffensives, including the rapid recovery of territory in Kharkiv region in September 2022 and the recapture of the city of Kherson in November. Russia announced the annexation of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson regions in September 2022, despite not controlling all of the claimed territory. The annexation claims were widely rejected internationally and did not settle the question of sovereignty or effective control.

Front lines have shifted unevenly. Some settlements changed hands; other areas became entrenched positions where small advances came at high cost. A sound account dates any territorial claim and distinguishes a claimed annexation, military occupation, de facto control and internationally recognized sovereignty.

## A war of missiles, drones and infrastructure

Long-range attacks have struck cities far from the front, affecting housing, energy infrastructure, ports, transport and industrial facilities. The use of explosive weapons with wide-area effects in populated areas has caused civilian casualties and extensive damage. Attacks on Ukraine’s energy system have created risks beyond the immediate blast zone, including power cuts affecting heating, water supply, hospitals, schools and households.

Drones have become central to reconnaissance and strike operations. Their spread has changed battlefield visibility, targeting, air defence and the vulnerability of vehicles and infantry. Missiles and drones have also been used in attacks on infrastructure far behind front lines. The fact that a weapon is unmanned does not alter the obligation to distinguish civilians from combatants and to take feasible precautions; the legality of an attack depends on its target, expected harm, military advantage and the circumstances of the strike.

## Civilian casualties and humanitarian impact

The UN has repeatedly warned that verified civilian casualty figures are likely lower than the true totals. Investigators need access to locations, records and witnesses, and cases may take time to confirm. Figures published at different dates are cumulative snapshots and should not be compared without noting their coverage period. The UN’s 2022 reporting documented thousands of civilian deaths and injuries during the first months of the invasion, with heavy weapons used in populated areas causing a substantial share of the harm.

Millions of people have been displaced internally or have fled abroad. Families have been separated, homes and public buildings damaged, and children’s education repeatedly interrupted. Minefields and explosive remnants of war pose long-term risks to civilians and impede farming, reconstruction and return. Occupation has brought additional concerns about detention, torture and ill-treatment, forced transfers, restrictions on civic life and the treatment of children. These categories should be documented separately and not reduced to a single casualty number.

International humanitarian law applies to all parties. Reports of violations must be attributed to the actor and incident supported by evidence. The existence of violations by one party does not remove the obligations of another, and a documented allegation should not be presented as a final criminal judgment unless a competent court has reached that conclusion.

## International assistance and escalation management

Ukraine’s partners have provided weapons, training, intelligence, budget support and humanitarian assistance. Russia has adapted its military production and obtained support or supplies from partners. Sanctions, export controls and diplomatic measures have widened the economic and political confrontation. This external support is a central part of the war’s context, but it is not a separate battlefield record: the archive cross-links it to the principal conflict to avoid double-counting events.

The conflict has raised difficult questions about escalation, deterrence, security guarantees and the risk of direct confrontation between Russia and NATO states. Such risks should be analysed without treating every diplomatic dispute or weapons transfer as proof that a new war has begun. The archive distinguishes actual hostilities from the political and military relationships that shape them.

## Law, accountability and uncertainty

The UN General Assembly condemned Russia’s aggression and called for withdrawal from Ukraine. International institutions and national authorities have investigated alleged war crimes and other violations. Legal proceedings have different mandates and evidentiary thresholds; an arrest warrant, an interim ruling, a finding of state responsibility and a criminal conviction are not equivalent. Reporting should preserve those distinctions.

Official statements from Moscow and Kyiv often describe battlefield developments in ways that serve military and political goals. Claims about losses, destroyed equipment, territorial control and responsibility for specific strikes should be checked against independent reporting, geolocated imagery, satellite data, humanitarian investigations and court records. Where independent verification is unavailable, a careful article should say so.
