# Yemen’s Civil War: Fragmented Authority and a Prolonged Humanitarian Crisis

Yemen’s current civil war emerged from a failed political transition, regional power struggles and the Houthi movement’s expansion from northern Yemen. The Houthis, also known as Ansar Allah, took control of Sana’a in 2014. In March 2015, a Saudi-led coalition intervened in support of President Abdrabbuh Mansur Hadi’s government. The conflict then widened into a complex war involving Yemeni government forces, Houthi forces, southern separatists, local armed groups, al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula (AQAP) and foreign powers.

## From political transition to war

The 2011 uprising ended Ali Abdullah Saleh’s presidency but did not settle disputes over power, regional representation, military institutions or the economy. A transition process struggled to resolve these issues. The Houthis advanced south and seized the capital in 2014, while alliances shifted, including a temporary partnership with Saleh before their split and his death in 2017. The internationally recognized government itself became divided and reliant on external support.

The Saudi-led intervention brought airstrikes, maritime restrictions and ground operations into the conflict. Iran has provided support to the Houthis, although the scale and form of that support varied by period and is contested in some details. The conflict should not be described as only a proxy war: Yemeni actors have their own political agendas, local constituencies and rivalries.

## Ceasefires and fragmented control

A UN-brokered truce in 2022 reduced some forms of cross-border and domestic violence, but it did not create a comprehensive peace agreement. Yemen’s political and military landscape remains fragmented, with different authorities controlling different areas. The Presidential Leadership Council, southern separatist forces and other factions do not always share the same objectives. Local ceasefires can reduce fighting without resolving governance, revenue, security or representation disputes.

The Red Sea attacks and retaliatory strikes beginning in late 2023 introduced a connected but distinct regional theatre. Those events should be linked to Yemen’s conflict without automatically being treated as the same battlefield or as proof that all parties in Yemen participate in each regional exchange.

## Civilian harm and humanitarian consequences

Yemen’s population has endured airstrikes, shelling, landmines, detention, attacks on infrastructure and severe disruption to food, water, health care and employment. The humanitarian crisis reflects both direct violence and economic collapse, restrictions on imports, displacement and weakened public services. Responsibility for particular incidents should be assessed individually; broad labels cannot replace investigation into who carried out an attack and what was targeted.

Casualty estimates vary substantially because some count direct violent deaths while others include indirect deaths caused by hunger, disease and reduced access to care. These measures must remain separate. The archive should state the source, time period and definition whenever figures are used.

## Prospects for settlement

A durable settlement requires an inclusive political process, arrangements for revenue and public services, security guarantees, mine clearance, accountability and participation by groups not represented in high-level negotiations. External actors can influence the war but cannot substitute for a workable Yemeni political agreement.
