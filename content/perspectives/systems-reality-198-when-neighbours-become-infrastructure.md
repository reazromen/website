A flower does not control the flight of every insect that visits it. A plant cannot simply command the wind to carry its pollen. Yet reproduction for many plants depends on relationships with other organisms and with the physical world. What looks like an individual object is participating in a network of processes that make its life possible.

Infrastructure is often imagined as something built from concrete, cables, pipes and servers. But the word points to a deeper function: the conditions and connections that allow other activities to happen. A bridge is infrastructure for movement. A power grid is infrastructure for machines and homes. A protocol is infrastructure for communication between independently built systems.

Relationships can acquire the same role. A pollinator population becomes part of the conditions under which a plant reproduces. A shared identity service becomes part of the conditions under which applications operate. A package registry, time service or routing protocol can become so widely relied upon that its maintenance affects thousands of systems that its creators never directly meet.

At this point, the neighbour is no longer merely an external participant. Its continued operation becomes an assumption inside the dependent system. The relationship has become infrastructure, whether or not anyone designed it that way.

This transition creates a particular kind of risk. Dependencies tend to disappear from attention when they work reliably. Teams optimise the service they own while treating everything else as permanent background. Then a certificate expires, a supplier changes a policy, a pollinator declines, or a shared library receives a compromised update. Suddenly the supposedly external relationship is revealed as a structural component.

Resilient systems make these relationships visible. They maintain inventories of dependencies, define fallback behaviour, monitor the health of critical partners and plan for graceful degradation. Ecological resilience similarly depends on the diversity and condition of relationships, though ecosystems cannot be managed as if they were software deployments.

The metaphor has a warning as well as an opportunity. A relationship that supports a system can also constrain it. Dependence may create leverage, lock-in or fragility. A system should not try to eliminate all dependence; that is often impossible. It should understand which dependencies are essential, how they can fail, and what alternatives exist.

The most important infrastructure may therefore be neither inside a system nor outside it. It may be the set of relationships through which the system remains possible.
