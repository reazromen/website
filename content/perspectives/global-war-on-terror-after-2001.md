# The Global War on Terror After 2001: A Campaign Umbrella, Not One Conflict

“Global War on Terror” became a broad political and military label after the attacks of 11 September 2001. It covered changing operations, alliances, legal authorities and geographic theatres. It is useful as a policy-history umbrella, but it is not one armed conflict with a single front, a fixed set of parties or a uniform timeline.

## Multiple theatres

The US-led intervention in Afghanistan began in 2001 and evolved through the overthrow of the Taliban government, a long insurgency, international support for Afghan security forces and the Taliban’s return to power in 2021. The 2003 invasion and occupation of Iraq led to a different sequence of insurgency, sectarian conflict, state-building and later operations against the Islamic State. Counterterrorism operations also took place in Somalia, Yemen, Pakistan and elsewhere, with local governments and armed groups playing distinct roles.

The label can obscure these differences. An operation against al-Qaeda, a campaign against the Islamic State, a drone strike and a major ground invasion differ in their participants, legal justifications, intensity and effects. They should not be counted as the same conflict simply because governments linked them to counterterrorism policy.

## Scope and evidence

The archive should identify each operation’s stated objective, legal framework where relevant, participating states, local partners and time boundaries. Official designations are evidence of government policy, not neutral proof that all targeted actors belonged to a unified organization. Civilian casualties and allegations of unlawful conduct require separate source attribution.

## Archive treatment

This page is a cross-index for related campaigns. It links the primary conflict articles and tracks policy shifts without adding another independent war count. The distinction between state-based conflict, non-state conflict, one-sided violence and counterterrorism operations helps avoid double-counting. The end of a named operation does not necessarily mean that the underlying conflict ended.
