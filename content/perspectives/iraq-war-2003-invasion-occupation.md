# The Iraq War: Invasion, Occupation, and the Unraveling of a State

The Iraq War began with the US-led invasion of Iraq on 20 March 2003. The stated case for war centered on claims that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction and posed a security threat, alongside arguments about enforcing United Nations resolutions and removing Saddam Hussein's government. The invasion quickly defeated the Iraqi military and toppled the government. However, the stockpiles of active weapons of mass destruction that had been central to the public case for war were not found. The consequences of the invasion continued long after the initial military campaign.

The war is best understood as several connected but distinct phases: the invasion; the occupation and attempted reconstruction of state institutions; the growth of insurgent violence; the sectarian conflict that peaked in 2006–2007; the later reduction in violence; and the emergence of the Islamic State, which captured large areas of Iraq in 2014. The US-led combat mission formally changed over time, but violence, political instability, and foreign involvement continued.

## The invasion and the occupation

The invasion removed Saddam Hussein's government, but defeating a regime did not provide an agreed plan for the political order that would follow. Decisions made by the Coalition Provisional Authority, including the de-Ba'athification policy and the dissolution of the Iraqi army, had major effects on employment, state capacity, and the security environment. Analysts continue to debate how much each policy contributed to the insurgency, but the loss of experienced personnel and the sudden restructuring of institutions created serious challenges.

The occupation also faced looting, infrastructure failures, shortages, and a loss of public confidence. Insurgent groups attacked coalition forces and Iraqi institutions, while foreign fighters and jihadist networks entered the conflict. Some armed groups were motivated by nationalism and opposition to occupation; others pursued sectarian or extremist aims. Treating every opponent as part of one unified movement obscures the competing agendas that made the conflict harder to resolve.

## Sectarian violence and the limits of security operations

In 2006, the bombing of the al-Askari shrine in Samarra intensified sectarian violence between Sunni and Shia communities. Armed organizations carried out killings, kidnappings, bombings, and forced displacement. Civilians were targeted because of identity, residence, or perceived political affiliation. The state struggled to provide impartial security, and some security institutions were themselves implicated in abuses or factional politics.

The US military surge in 2007, cooperation with Sunni tribal groups, changes in insurgent tactics, and exhaustion among communities all contributed to a decline in violence. No single measure explains the change. Nor did the decline resolve the underlying problems of governance, corruption, representation, or reconciliation. A reduction in attacks can create space for political settlement, but it cannot substitute for one.

## The rise of the Islamic State

The Islamic State in Iraq grew from earlier jihadist networks and expanded during the Syrian civil war. In 2014, it captured Mosul and significant territory in Iraq and Syria, declaring a so-called caliphate. Its rule involved mass executions, enslavement, persecution of religious minorities, destruction of cultural heritage, and severe restrictions on civilians. Iraqi forces, Kurdish forces, local partners, and an international coalition fought to retake territory. Mosul was declared liberated in 2017, although insurgent violence and security operations continued.

The Islamic State's rise cannot be attributed to the 2003 invasion alone: the Syrian war, regional competition, organizational decisions, and failures of Iraqi governance also mattered. But the post-2003 upheaval altered the political and security landscape in which the group developed.

## Human costs and unresolved questions

The war killed large numbers of Iraqi civilians and combatants, displaced millions, damaged essential infrastructure, and transformed the country's political system. Casualty estimates vary substantially depending on methodology and whether they count direct violent deaths, excess mortality, or a specific period. Those differences should be stated rather than hidden behind a single supposedly definitive number. The consequences extended into neighboring countries through displacement, militia networks, and regional rivalry.

The Iraq War remains a case study in the gap between removing a government and building a legitimate political order. Military superiority can win an invasion; it cannot guarantee institutional trust, public safety, or political compromise. The long aftermath shows why postwar planning, protection of civilians, credible intelligence, and accountability are not secondary concerns. They are central to whether the end of a campaign becomes the beginning of peace.
