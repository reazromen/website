Mathematics proves theorems from axioms.

Experiments do something different.

They place ideas in contact with a world that can refuse to behave as expected.

That makes experiments powerful, but it does not usually give scientific theories the same kind of proof that a theorem receives. A theory can survive severe tests, explain diverse observations, make successful predictions, and become extraordinarily reliable without becoming logically immune to revision.

## A prediction exposes a theory to risk

A scientific theory becomes testable when it implies observations that could have turned out otherwise.

If a model predicts a spectral line at a particular wavelength, an orbit with a particular precession, or a relationship between variables, measurement can compare expectation with reality.

Agreement increases confidence.

Disagreement may reveal experimental error, an incomplete model, or a genuinely wrong theory.

The important feature is risk: the theory has said enough that the world can push back.

## One successful experiment is not enough

Many different explanations can predict the same observation.

Suppose an experiment produces a curve exactly where theory A predicted it. Theory B may also predict nearly the same curve under those conditions. A hidden systematic error may mimic both.

The evidential strength comes from networks of tests.

Different instruments.

Different regimes.

Independent groups.

Predictions made before the data were seen.

Tests where rival theories diverge.

A theory becomes compelling not because one graph looks right, but because many opportunities for failure are survived.

## Experiments test bundles of assumptions

An experiment never tests a theory in isolation.

It also relies on instrument calibration, background models, statistical assumptions, auxiliary physics, data-processing code, and the claim that the apparatus behaves as intended.

When a prediction fails, investigators have to locate the failure.

Was the theory wrong?

Was the detector saturated?

Was the calibration off?

Was a parameter misestimated?

This is why anomalous results require follow-up rather than instant revolution.

## Strong theories earn trust by unification

Some theories gain extraordinary credibility because they explain many phenomena with one framework.

General relativity, quantum electrodynamics, and evolutionary theory are not trusted because someone performed a single decisive experiment and stamped them proven. They earned confidence through repeated success across independent domains.

That confidence can become so strong that rejecting the framework would require explaining a large body of successful results at once.

Still, the door to revision remains conceptually open.

New regimes can reveal limits.

Newtonian mechanics remains useful despite relativity because its domain is understood.

## Falsification is not always a clean switch

The phrase "one experiment can falsify a theory" is useful but simplified.

Real experiments have uncertainty.

Predictions depend on parameters.

Theories can have approximations.

Measurements can conflict with one another.

An unexpected result may first challenge an auxiliary assumption rather than the core framework.

Scientific practice therefore involves model comparison, replication, uncertainty analysis, and judgment about which component of the explanatory network failed.

## What experiments can establish

Experiments can do something better than absolute proof.

They can constrain possibility.

A result can rule out a parameter range.

It can eliminate a mechanism.

It can show that a predicted effect does not occur above a measured threshold.

It can demonstrate that one model predicts data substantially better than another.

It can establish reproducible regularities so reliable that engineering safely depends on them.

This is not weak knowledge.

It is operationally powerful knowledge with visible boundaries.

## Theories remain maps

A theory is a representation of structure in the world.

A good one compresses many observations into a small set of principles and then predicts observations not used to construct it.

The better it works, the more tempting it becomes to identify the map with the territory.

Scientific humility means resisting that final step.

Can an experiment prove a theory?

Not usually in the logical sense.

But experiments can make alternatives increasingly difficult to sustain, narrow uncertainty, and build a network of evidence so strong that the theory becomes the best-tested description we have.

That is not proof by deduction.

It is reality checking, repeated until very little room remains for a better explanation.
