A forest looks like a place, but it is also a collection of relationships. Trees compete for light and water. Fungi interact with roots. Insects pollinate flowers, consume leaves and feed other animals. Microbes transform matter that would otherwise remain locked in dead tissue. Remove enough of these relationships and the forest may still look like a forest for a time, even as the processes that sustain it begin to fail.

Now consider a computer system. It has processes, storage, networks, identity services, monitoring and rules about what each part may do. Engineers draw a boundary around these components and call the result a platform. Yet the platform also depends on electricity, cooling, hardware suppliers, software maintainers, network operators, users and the physical environment. The diagram ends before the dependencies do.

Can a system be its own ecosystem? In one sense, yes: a sufficiently complex system can contain many interacting components, competing priorities and feedback loops. But calling it an ecosystem does not make it independent of its surroundings. An ecosystem is not defined by having everything inside it. It is defined partly by the interactions through which living and nonliving elements influence one another.

The boundary is therefore a question, not a fact handed down by nature. We draw it to answer a particular question. If we are studying a cell, we may treat its membrane as the boundary. If we are studying its survival, we must include the nutrients and conditions outside it. If we are studying a city, the administrative border is rarely the full boundary of its water, energy or food systems.

A common engineering mistake is to optimise the parts inside the diagram while exporting costs to everything outside it. A service appears efficient because another team handles its failures. A city appears prosperous while drawing down distant resources. A platform appears resilient until a supposedly external dependency disappears.

A more honest model records the exchanges across the boundary: energy, matter, information, money, labour, risk and waste. It asks which flows are essential, who maintains them, and what happens when they stop.

No map can include the whole universe. But every map can disclose its omissions. A system becomes easier to understand when we stop asking only what it contains and begin asking what it quietly assumes the rest of the world will provide.
