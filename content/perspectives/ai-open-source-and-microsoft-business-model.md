I think the growing adoption of AI and open-source software raises a serious question: can Microsoft’s current business model remain as strong in the years ahead? That does not mean Microsoft is about to disappear. It means the economics of software, the capabilities of small teams, and users’ expectations of control are changing in ways that could challenge the advantages large software vendors have enjoyed for decades.

This is not simply a contest between Windows and Linux. The deeper question is whether people and organizations will continue to depend on a handful of large platforms for most of their work, or whether they will increasingly combine open-source software, their own infrastructure, and AI to build systems that fit their needs.

## When the cost of building software falls

Building and maintaining custom software used to require substantial investment. Organizations needed developers, infrastructure, security expertise, maintenance plans, and long-term support. Buying established software or paying for a subscription was often the most practical choice. Products such as Microsoft 365, Windows, and Azure grew into major businesses in this environment.

AI tools can now help developers write and test code, understand unfamiliar documentation, troubleshoot errors, and connect services. Small teams can attempt projects that previously looked too expensive or complicated. AI does not make software engineering effortless: generated code still needs review, and security, data migration, reliability, and operations require real expertise. But the barrier to getting started is lower.

That shift gives organizations more options. Instead of buying a complete package from a large vendor, a small team may assemble open-source components and build a focused tool around a specific problem. As these alternatives become easier to create, customers may ask harder questions about the price of software subscriptions and the value they receive in return.

## Open source is more than free software

The value of open source is not limited to avoiding a license fee. Its code can be inspected, run in an environment of your choice, modified, and replaced or extended when your needs change. It can also reduce the risk of depending entirely on one vendor’s roadmap.

Open source is not cost-free in the broader sense. Servers, electricity, backups, security updates, support, and people’s time all have a price. A self-hosted system can be cheaper and more flexible in one situation, yet more expensive to operate in another.

Projects such as Nextcloud for file collaboration, Grafana and Prometheus for monitoring, n8n for automation, and Asterisk for voice communications show how much useful infrastructure is available outside a single vendor’s proprietary suite. They are not automatically the best choice for every organization. They do demonstrate that companies do not have to buy every layer of their technology as one closed package.

AI may make this ecosystem more approachable. It can help people interpret configuration files, investigate logs, and connect tools that were designed separately. Some tasks that once required a specialist team may become manageable for a smaller group. However, mistakes in production configuration or security can still cause serious damage, so AI assistance is not a substitute for testing and operational discipline.

## Will local AI reduce dependence on the cloud?

Open models and local inference make it possible to run some AI workloads on personal computers or privately managed servers. Potential advantages include keeping sensitive data within your own environment, reducing per-request API costs, and continuing some work when internet connectivity is limited.

But local AI is not always cheaper or better. Larger models may require expensive GPUs, substantial memory, electricity, and ongoing maintenance. For many organizations, cloud platforms remain attractive because they provide scale, managed security, and ready-to-use AI services. The likely outcome is not an entirely local or entirely cloud-based world, but a hybrid one. Organizations will decide which data stays on their own systems, which tasks use which models, and which workloads are worth operating themselves.

## Where Microsoft is vulnerable—and where it remains strong

It is misleading to view Microsoft only as a Windows company. In its fiscal 2025 annual report, Microsoft reported total revenue of $281.7 billion and Microsoft Cloud revenue of $168.9 billion. The Windows and Devices business generated $17.3 billion. Those figures show how far the company’s business already extends beyond desktop operating systems. [Microsoft’s 2025 annual report](https://www.microsoft.com/investor/reports/ar25/index.html) provides the underlying figures.

This is where the story becomes more complicated. If open source puts pressure on desktop software or applications running on privately managed infrastructure, Microsoft’s cloud business can still benefit from the same trend. Organizations may use open-source applications while relying on cloud providers for hosting, identity management, security, data services, or AI compute. Azure can serve those needs.

Microsoft is also adapting its business around cloud and AI. In September 2026, Reuters reported that Microsoft was moving toward separately disclosing Azure cloud sales as part of a financial reporting shift. [Read the Reuters report](https://www.reuters.com/business/retail-consumer/microsoft-reveal-azure-cloud-sales-financial-reporting-shift-2026-09-02/).

Open source can therefore be both a competitor to Microsoft’s products and part of the workload running on its cloud platform. The company’s future will depend not only on whether people use Windows, but also on which platforms developers, businesses, and AI agents choose to do their work.

## The real risk is losing the ability to lock customers in

A business model comes under pressure when customers can switch to alternatives more easily and no longer see enough value in paying for the existing product. If data can be exported cleanly, applications can move between environments, and AI can help build substitutes, dependence on a single vendor may decline.

That transition will not happen overnight. Large organizations have years of accumulated data, employee training, licensing agreements, identity systems, security policies, and applications that depend on one another. Replacing a product is not just a matter of comparing license prices. The cost of running it reliably, the consequences of failure, and the expertise required to maintain it all matter.

Microsoft has a choice familiar to many large technology companies: try to keep customers tightly bound to its ecosystem, or adapt its products to a world shaped by open source, hybrid infrastructure, and AI. In practice, companies often pursue a mixture of both strategies.

## Who will control the technology?

I see the combined effect of AI and open source as a redistribution of capability. Software development and operations were once concentrated more heavily in large companies and well-funded organizations. Today, small teams, independent developers, and people willing to run their own infrastructure have more practical options.

GitHub’s [Octoverse 2025 report](https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/octoverse-a-new-developer-joins-github-every-second-as-ai-leads-typescript-to-1/) describes the growth of the developer ecosystem and the influence of AI. That growth does not, by itself, prove that any company is heading for decline. It does help illustrate how quickly the environment in which software is built is changing.

I would not predict that Microsoft will lose its business in the near future. Its bigger challenge is to keep answering a basic question: why should customers continue to depend on its products? If those products save time, improve security, reduce complexity, and offer fair value, customers will have good reasons to stay. If open-source software and AI can deliver comparable results at lower cost and with more control, some parts of the traditional business model will naturally come under pressure.

The coming shift may make it less important how large a software company is and more important how well its products solve real problems, how easily customers can leave, and how much control users retain over their data and infrastructure. AI and open source may gradually move some of that control back toward users. Microsoft’s test is not simply survival; it is how successfully it adapts to that change.
