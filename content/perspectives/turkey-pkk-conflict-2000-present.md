# Turkey and the PKK: Insurgency, Cross-border War, and Unfinished Politics

The conflict between the Turkish state and the Kurdistan Workers’ Party (PKK) predates 2000, but it continued through repeated changes in tactics, geography and political context during the archive period. The PKK began an armed campaign in 1984 and has sought different forms of Kurdish political rights and self-determination over time. Turkey designates the PKK a terrorist organization; it is also designated as such by the United States and the European Union. The conflict’s political roots, however, include longstanding disputes over Kurdish identity, language, representation and state policy.

## Ceasefires and renewed fighting

The 2000s included periods of reduced fighting and attempts at political dialogue, followed by renewed violence. A peace process initiated in the 2010s broke down in 2015, after which urban clashes and security operations caused substantial destruction in several Kurdish-majority towns. The Turkish military also expanded operations against PKK bases and positions in northern Iraq. These cross-border operations became a recurring part of the conflict and connected the Turkey–PKK theatre to wider security dynamics in Iraq and Syria.

The PKK is an organized armed actor, but Kurdish citizens, political parties, civil society and diaspora communities are not interchangeable with it. Treating Kurdish identity as evidence of militant affiliation is inaccurate and risks obscuring civilian rights and political diversity. Likewise, attacks by the PKK on security personnel or civilians must be documented as acts by the organization, not attributed to an entire population.

## Geography and civilian impact

The conflict has affected southeastern Turkey and areas of northern Iraq, with further intersections in Syria. Civilians have experienced deaths, displacement, curfews, destruction of homes, restrictions on movement and allegations of unlawful detention or excessive force. PKK attacks have killed security personnel and civilians; Turkish operations have also raised questions about civilian protection, especially where airstrikes or fighting occur near inhabited areas.

Casualty totals vary by period and source. A useful archive should separate deaths in armed clashes from civilian fatalities, and should record the location and actor attribution for each incident. The number of people affected by displacement or damaged infrastructure cannot be inferred from battle-death totals alone.

## Negotiation and political settlement

The conflict has repeatedly shown that military pressure can alter operational capacity without resolving the political questions that sustain it. A durable settlement would need to address security, disarmament, legal rights, local governance, political participation and the safe return of displaced residents. Negotiations are complicated by regional conflicts and by disagreement over the PKK’s future role, leadership and relationship with other Kurdish organizations.

## Scope and sources

This page tracks the Turkey–PKK armed conflict from 2000 onward, including cross-border operations where the PKK is a principal target or participant. It cross-links, but does not merge, the Syrian Kurdish theatre and other armed conflicts in Iraq. Any 2025–2026 claims about disarmament, dissolution, attacks or negotiations must be updated with dated official statements and independent reporting before being treated as settled.
