Place two seeds of the same species in different environments. One may find rich soil, reliable water and pollinators. The other may face drought, poor soil and a plant that shades it from the sun. Their inherited starting points may be similar, but their lives will not unfold in the same way.

Systems are shaped by what surrounds them. Ecologists study how organisms respond to competitors, predators, mutualists and changing habitats. Engineers see a related pattern when identical services behave differently under different traffic, latency, hardware limits or dependency failures. The internal design matters, but it never acts alone.

This is the neighbourhood effect: the influence of a system's local relationships on its behaviour and future possibilities. The phrase does not imply that nearby things always matter more than distant ones. In a network, a remote dependency may be more important than a neighbour physically close by. What matters is the structure of influence: which connections carry resources, information, risk or control.

A system can also change its neighbourhood. An organism modifies its habitat; a city builds roads that redirect trade; a software platform attracts developers who create new extensions. These changes can reinforce themselves. A useful service attracts more users, which attracts more integrations, which makes the service more useful. The same loop can produce fragility if everyone comes to depend on one provider or one shared component.

This is why evaluating a system only in isolation can be misleading. A component may pass every unit test and still fail when deployed alongside a neighbour that uses a different clock, assumes a different identity model or behaves unpredictably during overload. A policy that works in one region may fail when the resource supply, incentives or surrounding institutions change.

To study a system properly, we need to map its consequential relationships. Which neighbours supply essential resources? Which ones compete for the same capacity? Which connections are optional, and which have become hidden single points of failure? What happens if a partner disappears, changes its behaviour or becomes hostile?

These questions also reveal why copying a successful design is rarely enough. A solution is shaped by the conditions in which it works. Transplant it without the relationships that supported it, and the result may be a familiar shape with a different outcome.

A system is not simply its parts plus a background. Its neighbourhood can become one of the causes that determines what the system becomes.
