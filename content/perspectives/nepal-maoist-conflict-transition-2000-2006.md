# Nepal’s Maoist Conflict and Peace Transition, 2000–2006

Nepal’s armed conflict began in February 1996, when the Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist) launched an insurgency against the monarchy and existing political order. The Maoists presented their campaign as a revolutionary struggle against exclusion, poverty and entrenched social hierarchies. Their movement grew in areas where state services and political representation were weak, but its methods included killings, abductions, intimidation and forced recruitment. State security forces also committed serious human-rights violations, including arbitrary detention, torture and enforced disappearance. The years 2000–2006 therefore represent the conflict’s later phase and transition, not its beginning.

## Escalation and political crisis

In 2001, a breakdown in negotiations and major attacks led the government to declare a state of emergency and deploy the Royal Nepal Army against the insurgency. The conflict expanded beyond remote rural districts into a national struggle over political authority. Security operations, Maoist attacks and retaliatory violence disrupted ordinary life, displaced people, closed schools and damaged local economies. Civilians were often pressured by both sides to provide information, supplies, recruits or political support.

The conflict was also shaped by the monarchy’s struggle with elected political parties. King Gyanendra dismissed the government and assumed direct power in 2005, arguing that political leaders had failed to restore security. Instead, royal rule brought major political parties and the Maoists closer together against the monarchy. A twelve-point understanding in November 2005 helped lay the groundwork for a coordinated movement for democratic change.

## The 2006 turning point

Mass protests in April 2006 forced the restoration of parliament and weakened the king’s direct rule. A ceasefire and renewed negotiations followed. On 21 November 2006, the government and the Maoists signed the Comprehensive Peace Accord (CPA), formally ending the armed conflict. The agreement set out a path toward a constituent assembly, management of arms and armed personnel, political transformation and mechanisms to address conflict-era abuses. The United Nations later supported aspects of arms monitoring and the electoral process through its political mission in Nepal.

The peace agreement transformed the Maoists from an insurgent force into participants in formal politics. It also opened a path away from monarchy and toward a republic. Yet the transition was not simply a story of successful negotiations: implementation took years, political disputes persisted, and victims’ demands for truth and justice were not fully resolved.

## Human cost and unfinished accountability

The United Nations has described the war as claiming more than 13,000 lives. The exact toll depends on the period and categories included, and a single number cannot capture the effects of disappearance, injury, displacement, trauma, destroyed livelihoods and interrupted education. Both the Maoist side and state security forces were accused of serious abuses. The peace accord promised investigation, truth-seeking and remedies, but accountability mechanisms faced long delays and criticism over their independence and effectiveness.

Families of people who disappeared continued to demand disclosure of their fate. Survivors sought reparations and acknowledgment. The conflict also left deep local divisions, particularly where communities had experienced killings, coercion or forced displacement. Political inclusion improved in some respects after the peace process, but structural inequalities and disputes over federalism, identity and access to state power remained.

## How to classify this conflict

For this archive, Nepal’s Maoist war is a state-based intrastate conflict that began in 1996. The 2000–2006 page focuses on the final years, the 2005–2006 political realignment and the peace transition. It should be linked to the broader conflict record rather than counted as a separate war. The central lesson is that ending organized fighting is only one part of peace: durable settlement also requires credible institutions, inclusive political arrangements, acknowledgment of victims and a functioning route to justice.
