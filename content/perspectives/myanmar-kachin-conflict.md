# Myanmar’s Kachin Conflict: Autonomy, Ceasefires, and Renewed War

The Kachin conflict is one of Myanmar’s longest-running ethnic armed conflicts. It is centred on the Kachin Independence Organization (KIO) and its armed wing, the Kachin Independence Army (KIA), and their struggle with the Myanmar state over political autonomy, territory and local resources. The conflict predates the 2021 military coup by decades. The coup did not create the Kachin movement; it changed the wider strategic environment in which it operates.

The Kachin case should be read as both a distinct regional conflict and part of Myanmar’s larger conflict system. Treating it only as a front in the post-2021 war erases its history. Treating it as wholly separate ignores how resistance alliances, military deployments and territorial campaigns increasingly connect theatres across the country.

## From ceasefire to renewed fighting

The KIO emerged in the early 1960s, and the KIA became a major ethnic armed organization seeking greater autonomy. A ceasefire agreed with the military government in 1994 reduced large-scale fighting for a time but did not settle the political question. Disputes over the status of Kachin forces, resource projects and a proposed border-guard arrangement contributed to renewed war in 2011.

Fighting after the ceasefire’s collapse displaced civilians and disrupted communities across Kachin State and neighbouring areas. The front line was not always continuous: clashes, artillery, checkpoints and control of roads and resource corridors shaped movement. Civilian life was affected by insecurity, landmines, restricted farmland access and barriers to humanitarian assistance. A ceasefire can lower violence without resolving questions of federalism, command structures and resource governance.

## The 2021 coup and nationwide resistance

After the February 2021 coup, the KIA opposed military rule and cooperated at different times with local resistance forces and People’s Defence Forces. Kachin territory also became a place where some anti-coup activists sought refuge and training. These relationships did not make every resistance group part of one unified command.

Military operations and air power, alongside attacks by armed actors, increased risks to civilians. Control of border crossings, transport routes and areas rich in jade and other resources remained strategically important. Fighting in Kachin also interacted with campaigns in northern Shan State, where alliances among ethnic armed organizations changed the balance of control.

The KIA’s goals and institutions remain distinct from those of newer anti-coup networks. Cooperation can be substantial while leaving differences over territorial authority, federal arrangements, recruitment and the post-war political order unresolved.

## Civilian protection and classification

The human cost includes displacement, deaths and injuries, damage to homes, disrupted education and health services, and danger from landmines and unexploded ordnance. Displaced people may move repeatedly, and access restrictions can prevent aid organizations from reaching communities. Current displacement counts must not be confused with cumulative displacement or refugee counts.

All parties must protect civilians under applicable international humanitarian law. Reports of attacks, arbitrary detention, forced recruitment or other abuses should be attributed to the source documenting them and assessed incident by incident. The archive should distinguish battle-related deaths from one-sided violence, displacement and human-rights violations.

Kachin is a regional actor-and-theatre record linked to the nationwide Myanmar conflict. It should connect the KIA’s historical conflict with the state to post-2021 resistance without adding the same battle twice. A dataset audit should check actor dyads and years in UCDP, compare them with other ethnic theatres, and document changes in coding or intensity.
