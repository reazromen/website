# South Sudan’s Civil War and Recurrent Armed Violence, 2013 Onward

South Sudan became independent in 2011, but its early statehood was followed by a devastating civil war beginning in December 2013. The conflict emerged from a political struggle within the ruling Sudan People’s Liberation Movement and the security forces, then developed into widespread fighting involving government troops, opposition forces and shifting local armed actors. The 2018 peace agreement reduced the nationwide war but did not end insecurity across the country.

## The national conflict

Fighting erupted in Juba in December 2013 amid a dispute between President Salva Kiir and former vice-president Riek Machar. The crisis spread to several states and acquired both national political and local dimensions. Forces associated with the government and opposition committed abuses, while civilians were targeted, displaced or trapped by front lines. Ethnic identity shaped mobilization and patterns of violence, but communities should not be treated as uniform blocs or held collectively responsible for armed actors.

A 2015 peace agreement failed to end fighting. A renewed outbreak of violence in Juba in July 2016 displaced further civilians and led Machar to leave the country. The 2018 Revitalized Agreement established a framework for a unity government and security arrangements, but implementation was delayed and incomplete.

## Violence after the peace agreement

Since the agreement, conflict patterns have included local communal clashes, cattle-related violence, political insecurity, armed mobilization and disputes over state authority. These events do not all belong to one continuous national civil war. Some involve organized armed groups and meet formal conflict thresholds; others are local violence or criminal incidents that need separate classification.

The distinction matters because national peace indicators can improve while communities continue to face killings, displacement and restricted access to services. Localized violence may be intensified by arms availability, competition over land and cattle, climate pressures, weak policing and unresolved grievances. These factors should be investigated rather than used as automatic explanations for every incident.

## Civilian protection and peace implementation

Displacement, food insecurity, damaged infrastructure and limited healthcare have compounded the effects of fighting. Women and children have faced particular risks, including sexual violence and recruitment or use by armed forces. Humanitarian access can be constrained by insecurity and administrative barriers.

A durable reduction in violence depends on implementing security arrangements, accountable public institutions, local reconciliation and meaningful political participation. National agreements need local mechanisms that can resolve disputes without armed retaliation.

## Scope and sources
