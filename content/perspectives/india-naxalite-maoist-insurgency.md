# India’s Naxalite–Maoist Insurgency: Armed Struggle and the Politics of Exclusion

India’s Naxalite–Maoist insurgency is an armed conflict involving the Communist Party of India (Maoist) and Indian state forces, with violence concentrated historically in parts of central and eastern India. The movement draws on the Naxalbari uprising of 1967 and later revolutionary Maoist organizations. In 2004, the People’s War Group and the Maoist Communist Centre merged to form the CPI (Maoist), consolidating an insurgency that sought to overthrow the Indian state through protracted armed struggle.

The conflict’s geography has often included forested and mineral-rich districts where Adivasi and other rural communities face land dispossession, limited services, weak access to justice and disputes over mining and development. These conditions help explain why the insurgency found footholds in some areas; they do not mean that affected communities uniformly supported Maoists. Local people have often faced pressure from insurgents, security forces, contractors and political actors at the same time.

## Armed campaign and state response

Maoist forces have attacked police and paramilitary units, government facilities, roads, railways and political workers. Some attacks have involved ambushes and improvised explosives, with substantial losses among security personnel and civilians. The group has also been accused of killing alleged informants, abducting people and coercing villagers. Such practices can deepen fear and undermine local autonomy.

The Indian state has responded through policing, paramilitary deployments, intelligence operations, road and communications infrastructure, and programs intended to improve governance and development. Security operations have at times been accompanied by allegations of unlawful killings, arbitrary detention and abuses against civilians. The balance between counterinsurgency and civilian protection remains a central issue: operations that reduce armed capacity may still damage legitimacy if communities cannot safely report abuses or access independent justice.

The insurgency has never been uniform across the so-called “Red Corridor.” It has waxed and waned by district and state, shaped by leadership, local alliances, security pressure and changing political conditions. A single national casualty total can conceal the different experiences of regions and the difference between combat deaths, civilian deaths, disappearances and other harms.

## Land, rights, and public authority

The conflict cannot be explained only as a security problem. Disputes over forest access, land acquisition, mining, displacement, wages and the delivery of basic services have mattered in areas where the state’s presence was often experienced primarily through police or extraction projects. At the same time, Maoist ideology and armed organization have imposed their own restrictions, including coercion and punishment of people considered opponents.

A durable reduction in violence requires more than the elimination of armed units. It depends on reliable public services, fair handling of land and forest claims, protection for journalists and human-rights defenders, accountable security forces and political channels that rural communities can use without fear. Development projects that disregard local rights can reproduce the grievances they are intended to resolve.

## Scope and classification

This page covers the CPI (Maoist)-centred insurgency from the early 2000s onward. It does not treat every militant group in India, every protest in tribal areas or every incident of rural violence as part of the same conflict. Earlier Naxalite history is relevant context, while distinct armed organizations in Northeast India require separate entries. The conflict’s intensity and geographical reach changed over time, so all figures should be attached to a defined period and source.
