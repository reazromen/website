# The War in Afghanistan, 2001–2021: Two Decades of Intervention and Insurgency

The war in Afghanistan began in October 2001, when the United States and allied forces launched military operations against the Taliban government after the September 11 attacks. Al-Qaeda, whose leadership had operated from Afghanistan under Taliban protection, had carried out the attacks in the United States. The initial campaign rapidly removed the Taliban government from power, but it did not end the war. A conflict that began as an operation against al-Qaeda and its hosts evolved into a long international intervention, an insurgency, and a struggle over the Afghan state's authority and future.

The war is often described as one conflict, but its phases had different aims and participants. The first phase focused on removing the Taliban regime and disrupting al-Qaeda. A new Afghan political order emerged with international backing. The Taliban regrouped and expanded an insurgency, while the United States and NATO supported Afghan security forces and institutions. The war increasingly involved rural districts, local power networks, air strikes, roadside bombs, night raids, and competition for control of territory and population.

## The limits of military victory

The early collapse of the Taliban government created an impression that a decisive military result might be possible. But removing a government and building a stable state are different tasks. Afghanistan's geography, local political relationships, weak institutions, corruption, dependence on foreign assistance, and the sanctuary and support networks available to insurgents all shaped the conflict. International forces could defeat units and hold major cities, yet durable security required legitimate and capable Afghan institutions able to operate without indefinite outside support.

The Taliban were not a single unchanging battlefield formation. Their leadership, local alliances, tactics, and control varied over time. Afghan government forces and political elites also differed across regions and institutions. The conflict therefore cannot be explained solely as a contest between foreign troops and the Taliban. It was also a struggle over governance, local authority, justice, livelihoods, and the distribution of power.

## Civilians in the middle

Afghan civilians bore the consequences of insurgent attacks, suicide bombings, improvised explosive devices, air strikes, night raids, assassinations, and fighting around populated areas. Taliban and other insurgent attacks deliberately targeted civilians in some cases, while government and international operations also caused civilian deaths. The scale and responsibility for particular incidents have been contested, making independent investigation and transparent casualty records important.

Millions of Afghans were displaced internally or sought refuge abroad over the course of the conflict. Violence disrupted education, health services, agriculture, trade, and access to basic infrastructure. Some people gained access to schooling, media, and public life during the post-2001 period, especially in cities, but these gains were uneven and depended on security, region, gender, and family circumstances. They remained vulnerable as the political and military balance shifted.

## Negotiations, withdrawal, and collapse

The United States and the Taliban signed an agreement in Doha in February 2020 that set out a timetable for the withdrawal of foreign forces and commitments concerning future violence and negotiations. The Afghan government was not a signatory to the US–Taliban agreement. In 2021, as international forces withdrew, the Taliban advanced rapidly across the country. Afghan security forces and political institutions collapsed in August, and the Taliban entered Kabul. The final US military evacuation ended on 30 August 2021.

The speed of the collapse exposed the difference between institutions that appear functional while sustained by external support and institutions capable of surviving a sudden change in that support. It also reflected political bargains, morale, logistics, leadership decisions, and the failure to create a broadly accepted political settlement. No single explanation accounts for every local surrender or every institutional failure.

## The war after the war

The end of the international military mission did not end Afghanistan's humanitarian and political crises. The Taliban returned to power, and restrictions on women and girls, economic isolation, poverty, displacement, and security threats became major concerns. The Islamic State Khorasan Province continued to carry out attacks. Many Afghans who had worked with international forces or the former government faced risks, while the country remained dependent on humanitarian assistance.

The Afghanistan war demonstrates that battlefield dominance does not automatically create a stable political order. Foreign intervention can remove a government quickly but cannot, by military means alone, settle questions of legitimacy, accountability, social order, and national sovereignty. The human consequences lasted far beyond the final withdrawal flight.
