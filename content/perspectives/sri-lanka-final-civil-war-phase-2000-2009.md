# Sri Lanka’s Final Civil-War Phase, 2000–2009: War, Civilians, and an Unfinished Peace

Sri Lanka’s civil war did not begin in 2000, but the period from 2000 to May 2009 contains its final and most consequential phases. The principal parties were the Sri Lankan state and the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE), a separatist organization that sought an independent Tamil state in the island’s north and east. The conflict grew out of decades of ethnic tension, discriminatory state policies, competing nationalisms, anti-Tamil violence, and the failure of political arrangements to secure a durable settlement. The LTTE also used coercion, assassinations, child recruitment and attacks on civilians. Neither the state’s conduct nor the LTTE’s actions can be understood by treating one side’s abuses as cancelling out the other’s.

## From ceasefire to renewed war

A Norwegian-facilitated ceasefire took effect in February 2002, creating space for negotiations and reducing fighting for a time. The ceasefire did not resolve core disagreements over power-sharing, territory, security, and the political status of Tamil-majority areas. Trust weakened amid violations, internal LTTE divisions, political assassinations and disputes over the pace and terms of negotiations. Fighting resumed on a larger scale in 2006.

The government’s military campaign gradually retook territory held by the LTTE. The eastern theatre fell under government control before the final northern campaign. In 2008, the government formally withdrew from the ceasefire agreement. By early 2009, the LTTE had been pushed into a shrinking area in the northeast. The government declared victory in May 2009 after the LTTE’s military defeat and the deaths of its senior leadership.

## Civilians and the final offensive

The final months produced severe civilian suffering. People were trapped in areas of intense fighting, moved repeatedly, and faced shortages of food, medical care and safe shelter. Reports described shelling in populated areas, attacks affecting hospitals and other civilian sites, restrictions on humanitarian access, and LTTE efforts to prevent civilians from leaving the conflict zone. The government disputed allegations about its conduct and casualty estimates varied widely. The exact total killed during the final phase remains contested; figures should not be presented without explaining their time period, method and source.

After the war, large numbers of displaced Tamil civilians were held in government-run camps before many were released or resettled. Families continued to seek information about missing relatives. Land access, militarization, detention, political representation and the rights of survivors remained central concerns. Accountability has been disputed domestically and internationally, with victims’ groups criticizing the pace and independence of investigations.

## What ended, and what did not

The LTTE’s defeat ended its ability to wage a conventional territorial campaign, but it did not resolve the political questions that had sustained the conflict. A military victory can remove an armed organization without establishing public trust, equal citizenship or credible mechanisms for justice. The post-war challenge included rebuilding communities, addressing disappearances, returning land, protecting minority rights and creating a political settlement acceptable across the island.

This article treats 2000–2009 as a dated phase of the longer Sri Lankan civil war, not as a separate war beginning in 2000. That distinction matters for comparison with conflict datasets and for avoiding duplicate counting. The war’s history also cannot be reduced to a single casualty number or a binary story of victory and defeat: the record includes attacks by the LTTE, serious allegations against state forces, civilian resistance and survival, and a long unresolved struggle over truth and accountability.
