# Northeast India’s Insurgencies: Distinct Movements, Borders, and Peace Processes

Northeast India is not a single insurgency. It is a borderland region comprising several states and communities, with histories of contested integration, autonomy movements, ethnic competition, migration disputes and armed mobilization. From 2000 onward, violence has involved different organizations in Assam, Nagaland, Manipur, Tripura and neighboring areas. Their objectives, constituencies, methods and relationships with the Indian state vary. A useful conflict archive must separate these theatres rather than assign every event to one regional war.

## Different movements, different aims

In Assam, the United Liberation Front of Asom (ULFA) emerged around the claim that Assam’s political and economic interests were subordinated to the Indian state. Factions later diverged over negotiations and continued armed struggle. Bodo organizations pursued different forms of autonomy or separate statehood, while agreements and ceasefires changed the landscape of armed activity. In Nagaland, the Naga nationalist movement has a longer history and includes factions with distinct leadership and positions on negotiations. Manipur has seen multiple armed groups with different ethnic, territorial and ideological aims. Tripura’s insurgencies were also shaped by disputes over land, migration, identity and political power.

These movements cannot be collapsed into a common program. Some organizations entered ceasefires or political talks; others continued attacks or split into factions. The same district may have experienced more than one conflict, and groups sometimes fought each other as well as the state. The presence of a ceasefire agreement does not necessarily mean every faction stopped operating or that all violence ended.

## Borders and regional dynamics

The region’s international borders matter. Armed groups have historically moved across difficult terrain and, at times, sought sanctuary or logistical support outside India. Cross-border relationships vary by organization and period, and claims about foreign support require specific evidence. Border geography also shapes trade, family ties, displacement and the movement of security forces. These connections do not make every local conflict an international war.

The state response has included security operations, special legal powers in some areas, negotiations, ceasefires and autonomy arrangements. Official agreements with particular factions can reduce fighting, but may also leave rival factions outside the process or create disagreements about who represents a community. Human-rights organizations have raised concerns about unlawful killings, detention and impunity in the wider region. Armed groups have also been responsible for killings, extortion, abduction and attacks on civilians.

## Civilians and political settlement

Civilians face overlapping risks: insurgent recruitment and intimidation, extortion, security operations, communal violence, displacement and disrupted schooling or employment. The effects differ greatly across time and location. Broad regional casualty figures can obscure whether a death resulted from an insurgent attack, a clash between armed groups, communal violence or a security operation. This archive therefore treats actor and theatre identification as essential, not as a technical detail.

Peace processes have helped transform some organizations into political actors and reduced violence in certain areas. Yet agreements remain fragile when they do not address representation, land and resource disputes, accountability, local security or the concerns of communities not represented at the negotiating table. A ceasefire can be an important achievement, but it is not equivalent to a comprehensive settlement.

## Classification for the archive

This page is a regional index, not a claim that all northeastern insurgencies form one conflict. The archive will create or maintain actor-specific records where dataset evidence and reliable historical sources justify them. It will record each organization’s name variants, operating period, stated goals, ceasefire status and relationship to other groups. Episodes that fall below the archive’s armed-conflict threshold will be treated as political or communal violence rather than automatically classified as war.
