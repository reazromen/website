# Iraq and the Islamic State, 2013 Onward: Territorial War and Its Aftermath

The Islamic State’s campaign in Iraq was a major phase in the country’s post-2003 conflict landscape. The organization grew from earlier jihadist networks, exploited political and security failures in Iraq, and expanded through the Syrian civil war. In 2014 it seized Mosul and large areas of northern and western Iraq, declared a so-called caliphate, and imposed an extremist system of rule. Its territorial project was defeated militarily in Iraq, but the group’s insurgent activity and the effects of its rule did not disappear with the loss of cities.

## Expansion and the collapse of local security

The Islamic State of Iraq and its predecessors survived earlier counterinsurgency campaigns and benefited from regional instability. The Syrian war created space for recruitment, cross-border movement and access to territory. In Iraq, political exclusion, corruption, weak local security and distrust between communities and state institutions contributed to an environment in which the group could expand. These factors help explain the conditions it exploited; they do not excuse the organization’s mass violence or persecution.

In June 2014, the fall of Mosul exposed the fragility of Iraqi security institutions. The Islamic State captured weapons and infrastructure, took control of towns and imposed rule through intimidation, executions, forced displacement and the destruction of cultural and religious sites. Yazidis were subjected to genocide, including mass killings, enslavement and the abduction of women and children. Christians, Shia Muslims, Sunni opponents and other communities also faced persecution.

## The campaign to retake territory

Iraqi government forces, Kurdish forces, local armed groups and an international coalition fought the Islamic State. The campaign involved airstrikes, urban combat, sieges and ground offensives. Mosul, Iraq’s second-largest city, was declared liberated in July 2017 after months of devastating fighting. Iraq announced the defeat of the group’s territorial control in December 2017, although cells continued attacks and security operations persisted.

The military campaign came at a high civilian cost. Explosive weapons, booby traps, damaged infrastructure and unexploded ordnance made return dangerous. Entire neighborhoods required reconstruction. Families faced missing relatives, destroyed documents, stigmatization and disputes over alleged affiliation with the group. Displacement and slow rebuilding complicated recovery long after the front lines moved.

## Justice and the persistence of violence

The UN Investigative Team to Promote Accountability for Crimes Committed by Da’esh/ISIL (UNITAD) gathered evidence concerning crimes committed by the organization, including acts that investigators assessed as genocide, crimes against humanity and war crimes in specific contexts. Accountability remains complicated by the need to preserve evidence, identify victims, establish individual responsibility and ensure fair proceedings. Mass atrocity documentation is not a substitute for due process, and a finding about an organization does not establish the guilt of every person accused of association with it.

The Islamic State’s territorial defeat did not remove the social and political conditions that can support insurgency. Local grievances, uneven reconstruction, security-force abuses, sectarian distrust and regional conflict continue to influence Iraq’s security environment. The group’s remaining capacity varies over time and geography, so claims about current strength should be tied to dated assessments rather than treated as permanent facts.

## How this page fits the archive

The Islamic State phase overlaps with the earlier Iraq insurgency and with the Syrian civil war. It also includes distinct non-state conflict relationships, including fighting between the group and Kurdish-led forces. The archive therefore links the phases and identifies actors rather than counting every theatre or armed dyad as an independent war by default.
