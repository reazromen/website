A cell has no eyes.

It still has to know something about its environment.

Is glucose available?

Is a neighbor signaling?

Has DNA been damaged?

Is oxygen low?

Should the cell divide, move, secrete, repair, or die?

Biology coordinates these decisions through signals.

Not one kind of signal.

Many.

## Molecules become messages through receptors

A hormone circulates through blood.

Only cells with suitable receptors respond in a particular way.

The molecule is not a universal instruction.

Its effect depends on receptor expression, downstream pathways, cell state, and context.

The same ligand can produce different outcomes in different tissues.

Signal meaning belongs to the receiving system.

This should sound familiar.

Communication engineering has the same principle.

A waveform is not meaningful without a receiver and decoding relation.

## Cells transform signals

A receptor binds a molecule.

That event changes another molecule.

A phosphorylation cascade begins.

Transcription changes.

Ion channels open.

The original signal can be amplified, filtered, integrated, or suppressed.

Biological pathways are not passive pipes.

They compute in the broad sense of transforming inputs into state-dependent outputs.

But the software analogy has limits.

The computation is chemistry.

Molecules are consumed, modified, diffused, degraded, and coupled to metabolism.

## Electrical signaling is biological too

Neurons and muscle cells use membrane voltage.

Ion gradients create electrical potential.

Channels open and close.

Action potentials propagate.

Synapses convert electrical activity into chemical release and back into electrical or biochemical effects.

The nervous system therefore mixes signaling domains continuously.

Electricity is not a separate layer pasted onto biology.

It emerges from controlled ion movement across living membranes.

## Mechanical forces carry information

Cells also respond to force.

Stretch.

Pressure.

Matrix stiffness.

Fluid shear.

Mechanical state can change ion channels, cytoskeletal organization, gene expression, and development.

A tissue is not just chemistry in a bag.

Geometry and mechanics participate in signaling.

Biology senses shape and force.

## Noise is unavoidable

Molecules diffuse stochastically.

Gene expression fluctuates.

Ion channels open probabilistically.

Receptor numbers vary.

Small numbers of molecules make randomness visible.

Cells nevertheless make reliable decisions.

They use redundancy, thresholds, feedback, averaging, cooperativity, and population-level responses.

Biological signaling is therefore an information problem under noise.

## Feedback changes the meaning of input

A pathway can amplify itself.

Another can inhibit itself.

One signal can desensitize a receptor.

A pulse can produce a different response from a sustained input even when total exposure is similar.

Duration, frequency, and history matter.

This makes biological communication richer than a simple molecule-equals-message picture.

The temporal pattern can be part of the signal.

## Signaling networks evolved

Pathways were not designed as clean blocks.

Components are reused.

Crosstalk occurs.

One molecule participates in several networks.

Evolution modifies existing interactions.

The result is robust in some contexts and surprisingly fragile in others.

Again, history lives in architecture.

## Biology is an information system, but not only an information system

Signal language is powerful.

It helps us think about detection, noise, amplification, feedback, coding, and response.

But cells also need energy, matter, structure, and chemical kinetics.

Information cannot replace thermodynamics.

A signal must be physically embodied.

Biology runs on signals because living systems survive by detecting differences and responding to them.

What makes those signals meaningful is not an abstract codebook outside the organism.

It is the living network that receives them.
