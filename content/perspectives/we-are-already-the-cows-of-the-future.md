Some time ago, I wrote an article titled [*We May Become the Cows of the Future*](/posts/we-may-become-the-cows-of-the-future.html). I was thinking about a future in which humanity might gradually lose control over the systems it had created. A future where artificial intelligence, algorithms, corporations, and increasingly complex institutions could become so deeply embedded in our lives that we might eventually find ourselves living inside systems we neither understand nor control.

The comparison was simple. Imagine a cow living on a farm. It has food, shelter, and everything necessary to continue its existence. It produces milk, which becomes part of an economic system far beyond its understanding. It does not know where the milk goes, how much money it generates, who owns the business, or how the entire operation is organised. It simply lives within the environment provided to it.

At the time, I thought perhaps humanity could eventually find itself in a similar position.

But recently, I started questioning that assumption. Why were we talking about this as something that might happen in the future? When I look at how we interact with technology today, I find it difficult to believe that we are still waiting for that future to arrive.

Perhaps we are already there.

We wake up, unlock our phones, check notifications, browse social media, search for information, watch videos, communicate with friends, use navigation applications, and increasingly ask artificial intelligence to help us think, write, and make decisions. Most of these activities feel ordinary. Some are even advertised as free.

And that is where the interesting part begins.

We have become accustomed to receiving sophisticated digital services without directly paying for them. Search engines, social networks, video platforms, communication tools, and countless other services have become so integrated into everyday life that we rarely question their economic foundations. We tend to think of them as products designed to make our lives easier.

But infrastructure does not maintain itself. Computing resources cost money. Engineers, researchers, electricity, data centres, and network infrastructure all require investment. If a service is available to billions of people without a direct monetary charge, there must be an economic model supporting its existence.

Sometimes that model is advertising. Sometimes it is subscriptions, enterprise services, premium features, or a combination of different revenue streams. The important point is not that every free service operates in the same way. It is that a zero monetary price does not mean the absence of an economic exchange.

In many digital business models, our participation itself becomes economically valuable.

Consider what happens when we use a social media platform. We create content, share photographs, express opinions, react to other people's posts, build communities, and spend time watching videos. We do these things for our own reasons. We want entertainment, communication, recognition, or simply a way to pass the time. Yet those activities also help create the environment that makes the platform commercially valuable.

More content attracts more users. More users create more interactions. Those interactions generate opportunities for advertising and other commercial activities. The platform becomes more valuable partly because millions of people continuously contribute to it.

In 2000, media theorist Tiziana Terranova explored this phenomenon in her essay [*Free Labor: Producing Culture for the Digital Economy*](https://doi.org/10.1215/01642472-18-2_63-33). She examined how people voluntarily create digital content, knowledge, and communities that contribute to the economic value of online platforms, often without receiving direct payment.

What is interesting is that this observation predates the modern social media economy. The underlying relationship was already becoming visible when the internet was still a very different place.

Over time, however, the relationship has become considerably more sophisticated. We are no longer simply creating content for other people to consume. Our interactions can also generate signals about our interests, preferences, habits, and behaviour. These signals can be analysed to understand patterns, predict preferences, and support commercial decisions.

Shoshana Zuboff explored this transformation through the concept of surveillance capitalism. In her research, she described how human experience can become a source of data from which businesses extract economic value, particularly through the prediction of future behaviour. Her paper [*Big Other: Surveillance Capitalism and the Prospects of an Information Civilization*](https://doi.org/10.1057/jit.2015.5) is one of the foundational works in this discussion.

This raises a question that I find more interesting than the usual discussion about whether companies collect our personal information.

What exactly are we producing when we use these systems?

It is tempting to answer: data. But that answer is incomplete. We produce attention. We produce content. We generate behavioural patterns. We contribute to networks of relationships and interactions. We help train the commercial logic of recommendation systems through the signals we leave behind.

Our ordinary activities become inputs into systems whose economic operations are often invisible to us.

And perhaps the most interesting asymmetry is that these systems can learn a great deal about us while we remain largely unaware of how they work.

Think about a recommendation algorithm. You open a video platform and watch something about astronomy. You watch another video, perhaps one about artificial intelligence, and then something about space exploration. The platform observes your interactions and uses them to decide what might interest you next. Eventually, the experience begins to feel remarkably personal.

But how much do you actually understand about the mechanism behind that experience?

You know that the platform recommends videos. You may even know that it uses algorithms. But do you understand what signals influence those recommendations, how the platform benefits from your attention, or how the system's commercial incentives might shape what you see?

There is a difference between using a system and understanding it.

This difference becomes particularly important when we consider the scale of modern digital infrastructure. We increasingly depend on systems operated by organisations whose internal processes, economic incentives, and decision-making mechanisms are not entirely visible to the people using them.

Frank Pasquale explored this problem in [*The Black Box Society*](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674970847), examining how powerful institutions use opaque systems to make decisions and process information. Nick Couldry and Ulises A. Mejias, through their work on [data colonialism](https://doi.org/10.1177/1527476418796632), examined how everyday human life itself is increasingly incorporated into systems of data extraction.

These ideas are not identical, but they point towards a common question: what happens when the systems that organise our lives become economically valuable precisely because they can observe, analyse, and process our activities?

I do not think the problem is that we fail to understand every technical detail. That would be an unreasonable expectation. I work with technology, infrastructure, and software systems, and even within these fields, no individual understands every component of the systems they depend on. Modern civilisation functions through specialisation. We rely on engineers, scientists, institutions, and countless other people whose work we do not need to reproduce ourselves.

We do not need to understand how every server works to use the internet. We do not need to understand the physics of electricity to switch on a light. We do not need to know how every component of a vehicle functions to travel in one.

But there is a meaningful difference between not understanding the internal mechanics of something and not understanding the system's relationship with our lives.

We should at least understand who owns the infrastructure we depend on, how the services we use make money, what happens to the information we provide, and how automated systems might influence the decisions presented to us. We should understand enough to recognise when convenience comes with trade-offs and when our participation is creating value for someone else's business.

Perhaps the issue is not simply a lack of technical knowledge. It is a gap between the complexity of the systems we inhabit and our ability to understand their consequences.

We have become extraordinarily good at operating interfaces. We can navigate applications, configure devices, communicate through platforms, and use increasingly sophisticated tools without knowing much about what happens behind the screen. The interfaces are designed to make complexity disappear.

And that is part of their success.

But when complexity disappears from our experience, it does not necessarily disappear from the world. The infrastructure, economic incentives, data flows, and ownership structures remain. We simply stop seeing them.

This brings me back to the cow.

The cow does not need to understand the economics of the dairy industry to live its daily life. But that is precisely what makes the analogy uncomfortable. The system is organised around an objective that the cow does not understand. Its participation has value to the farm, but it has no meaningful understanding of the larger economic structure in which it exists.

Humans are different. We can question the systems around us. We can investigate them, build alternatives, change providers, demand transparency, and create new institutions. We have the ability to understand more than we currently do.

Yet we often choose convenience over curiosity, not necessarily because we do not care, but because the systems are designed to make participation effortless. We accept terms we rarely read, use services whose business models we barely understand, and build increasingly important parts of our lives around platforms whose rules can change without our involvement.

There is no reason to assume that all of this is inherently harmful. Digital technology has given humanity extraordinary capabilities. Knowledge is more accessible, communication is faster, and tools that once required enormous resources are now available to individuals. Artificial intelligence is extending these possibilities even further.

The question is not whether we should reject these systems. It is whether we should become more conscious participants in them.

Perhaps the most important change in perspective is to stop thinking of ourselves exclusively as consumers of technology. In many cases, we are also participants in the production of its economic value. We contribute our time, attention, creativity, information, and behaviour. Understanding that relationship does not require us to abandon the technology. It simply requires us to look beyond the interface.

When I wrote that we might become the cows of the future, I was imagining a destination humanity could eventually reach. Now I think the more uncomfortable possibility is that we have already entered that environment, and the transition was so gradual that we barely noticed it.

The future did not necessarily arrive with a dramatic announcement. It arrived through convenience, free applications, endless entertainment, personalised recommendations, and services that became increasingly difficult to live without.

We were looking ahead, imagining what might happen to humanity, while the foundations of that future were quietly becoming part of our everyday lives.

Perhaps we do not need to know everything about the farm. But we should know that we are inside one, understand how it operates, and recognise what our presence makes possible.

Because the real question was never simply whether we would become the cows of the future.

**The question is whether we can recognise the farm while we are still living in it.**
