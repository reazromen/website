A stone can remain alone for a very long time. A living thing cannot. It must draw energy from somewhere, release waste into somewhere, and survive within conditions it did not create. Even the simplest organism is already in conversation with a world beyond its skin.

The word *system* can make us imagine a sealed machine: inputs enter through defined ports, outputs leave, and everything important happens inside. But boundaries are often accounting tools, not walls around reality. A cell depends on its surroundings. A forest depends on sunlight, soil, water, microbes and animals. A city depends on distant farms, power stations, ports and the invisible labour of other cities.

Does every system need a neighbour? The answer depends on what we mean by need. A system may continue for a while in isolation if it has stored energy and resources. A closed system can undergo internal change. But no real system escapes physical constraints, and an isolated system loses access to exchanges that might help it respond to unfamiliar conditions.

Neighbours can be competitors, parasites, partners, teachers or simply sources of variation. Their presence does not guarantee progress. Contact can spread disease or failure as readily as it spreads useful information. The important question is not whether connection is always good, but whether a system can distinguish productive exchange from destructive dependence.

This question reaches beyond biology. A software service may run alone, yet its capabilities are shaped by protocols, libraries, hardware, users and other services. A community may possess deep knowledge, yet become fragile if it cannot exchange ideas with anyone outside itself. An AI system may process enormous amounts of data, but its development still depends on the wider human and technical environment that supplies objectives, tools and feedback.

Perhaps the neighbour is not merely an object outside the system. Sometimes the relationship itself becomes part of the system's effective architecture. The border remains on a diagram, while the actual work happens across it.

To understand a system, then, we should study not only its components but also the exchanges it permits, the relationships it refuses, and the dependencies it has forgotten to notice. Isolation can preserve a design. Neighbourhood can expose it to possibilities the design could never generate alone.
