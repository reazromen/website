# The Insurgency in Pakistan: Militancy, State Power, and Civilian Security

Pakistan's modern insurgency developed through several overlapping conflicts rather than one continuous campaign with a single command structure. Armed violence expanded in the former Federally Administered Tribal Areas and parts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa during the mid-2000s. The 2001 US-led intervention in Afghanistan, militant networks operating on both sides of the border, Pakistan's domestic security policies, and the political status of tribal regions all shaped the environment. The Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP), founded in 2007 as an umbrella for several militant factions, became a major actor in attacks against the Pakistani state and civilians.

The conflict's roots predate the TTP. The Soviet-Afghan war, later regional proxy struggles, the presence of Afghan Taliban networks, and the movement of armed groups across the Afghanistan–Pakistan border created a complicated security landscape. Pakistan's military and intelligence policies changed over time and were contested inside the country. It is inaccurate to assume that all militant groups had identical objectives or that every local community supported them.

## Escalation and military operations

Fighting intensified in the tribal areas as the Pakistani state launched operations against armed groups and militants attacked security forces, government institutions, religious sites, markets, and schools. Suicide bombings and targeted assassinations spread violence into major cities. Military operations displaced large civilian populations, while militant rule and intimidation restricted education, movement, and public life in affected areas.

The TTP's attacks included assaults on civilians and security personnel. The December 2014 attack on the Army Public School in Peshawar, which killed more than 130 children along with other victims, became a defining national trauma. The Pakistani state responded with intensified counterterrorism operations and a National Action Plan. These measures affected militant capacity but also raised enduring questions about due process, accountability, missing persons, and the rights of people living in conflict-affected districts.

## Civilian life and the geography of the conflict

The effects of the insurgency were not confined to combat deaths. Displacement disrupted education, farming, trade, and family networks. Returning residents often faced damaged homes, unexploded ordnance, weak public services, and uncertainty about land and compensation. Communities could be caught between militant coercion and security operations, with limited ability to influence decisions made by armed actors.

The former Federally Administered Tribal Areas were merged into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in 2018, changing their constitutional and administrative status. The integration process promised access to mainstream legal and political institutions but also required major investment and effective local governance. Security gains cannot be sustained by force alone if residents lack fair policing, justice, education, livelihoods, and meaningful political representation.

## Why the conflict persisted

Insurgencies survive when armed groups can recruit, move, finance operations, exploit grievances, or find sanctuary. Geography and border politics matter, but so do local disputes, state capacity, ideology, and organizational fragmentation. Military operations can disrupt networks; they may not eliminate the conditions that allow them to regenerate. Likewise, negotiations can reduce violence in the short term but become unstable when parties disagree about constitutional authority, disarmament, or enforcement.

The withdrawal of international forces from Afghanistan in 2021 changed regional calculations. Pakistan experienced renewed militant violence, and the TTP announced an end to a ceasefire in late 2022. Subsequent attacks and security operations demonstrated that the conflict had not ended. The level of violence has varied by region and year, so any assessment of current conditions should use dated data rather than assume a constant trajectory.

## A conflict, not a single story

Pakistan's insurgency includes state–militant fighting, attacks by different armed organizations, local political grievances, and cross-border dynamics. The term “war” captures the seriousness of organized violence but can obscure differences among these events. A responsible history names the groups involved, distinguishes confirmed facts from disputed claims, and does not treat residents of affected regions as collectively responsible for militant activity.

The central lesson is that civilian security requires more than defeating armed groups. It depends on accountable institutions, trusted local government, rights-respecting policing, reliable public services, and a political framework in which grievances can be addressed without violence. These conditions do not guarantee peace, but without them military success is difficult to make durable.
