Imagine travelling backwards through time, far beyond the existence of human beings, before the first forests, before animals, before even the first recognisable forms of complex life. On the young Earth, chemistry was doing something extraordinary. Molecules were interacting, combining and organising in ways that eventually gave rise to life. We still do not know precisely how that happened. But from that ancient beginning emerged a process that would transform an entire planet: evolution.

Over billions of years, life developed increasingly complex ways of sensing, adapting and responding to the world. Nervous systems appeared. Memory became possible. Eventually, one species developed the ability to study the process that had created it.

That species began building machines.

Today, artificial intelligence can write software, analyse scientific data, recognise patterns and help solve problems that once demanded years of specialised human effort. But perhaps the most interesting question is not what these machines can do. It is what their emergence means for the much longer history of intelligence.

**Could evolution have produced a form of intelligence capable of creating something that evolves beyond its own biological limitations?**

## The intelligence that learned to preserve itself

Human intelligence did not develop in isolation. It evolved through biological processes, but its greatest advantage may have been the ability to share knowledge.

Language allowed information to move between minds. Writing preserved it beyond a person's lifetime. Printing multiplied its reach. Computers transformed the speed and scale at which information could be stored and processed.

Each invention allowed knowledge to accumulate across generations, without requiring every generation to rediscover everything from the beginning.

This is different from biological evolution. Genetic evolution depends on inherited variation and natural selection across generations. Cultural and technological change can happen much faster, through communication, learning and deliberate design.

Yet the processes can influence one another. Evolutionary biologists use the term *niche construction* to describe how organisms modify their environments, sometimes changing the conditions under which future evolution occurs. Humans have taken this remarkably far. Agriculture, cities, medicine and technology have transformed the environment in which human beings live.

Artificial intelligence may be another step in this process: a technology that does not merely preserve information, but actively participates in producing and analysing it.

## When intelligence begins accelerating discovery

Consider AlphaFold, the artificial intelligence system developed by DeepMind. In 2021, researchers demonstrated its remarkable ability to predict protein structures, helping address a longstanding challenge in molecular biology.

Proteins perform countless essential functions inside living organisms. Understanding their structures helps scientists investigate how life works and how diseases develop. AlphaFold did not solve biology, and laboratory experiments remain essential. But it demonstrated something extraordinary: intelligence created by evolution could build a tool that helps investigate the molecular machinery of life itself.

A feedback loop is beginning to emerge. AI can assist scientists with analysing data, engineers with writing software and researchers with exploring complex possibilities. Those capabilities may help accelerate the development of further scientific and technological tools.

Could this process become self-reinforcing? Possibly. But fully autonomous systems that continuously improve themselves remain an open research challenge, not an established reality.

Nor does technological growth escape physical limits. Computation requires energy, materials, manufacturing and infrastructure. Nothing in the universe grows without constraints.

## Can the human mind keep up?

Here lies a curious contradiction. Our technology is changing at extraordinary speed, but the human brain remains a biological organ shaped by millions of years of evolution.

We have limited attention, working memory and time. A new technological capability can become available long before an individual has learned to use it effectively.

Yet the brain is adaptable. Through neuroplasticity, neural connections and patterns of activity change with learning and experience. Human intelligence has also always depended on tools beyond the skull.

In 1998, philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers introduced the influential concept of the *Extended Mind*, questioning whether cognition must always be confined to the brain. We already think with notebooks, maps, computers and scientific instruments. AI adds a new dimension to that relationship.

An engineer can now investigate a complex system while an AI assistant analyses documentation, generates code and explores possible solutions. The result is a collaboration between biological reasoning and artificial computation.

This does not establish that machines possess consciousness. The ability to process information, demonstrate intelligence and experience the world subjectively are different questions.

## What happens next?

Evolution has no predetermined destination. It did not set out to create humans, and it does not necessarily lead towards artificial intelligence. AI is a product of human biology, accumulated knowledge and deliberate engineering.

But its emergence raises a remarkable possibility: intelligence may increasingly operate through systems that combine biological minds with artificial ones.

Perhaps machines will become powerful instruments for understanding life, the universe and even ourselves. Perhaps they will transform medicine, scientific discovery and the way knowledge is created. Or perhaps their development will expose limitations we have not yet learned to recognise.

The universe spent billions of years producing life. Life, through evolution, produced a species capable of asking questions about the universe. That species has now begun building machines that can help it investigate those questions.

We do not know whether this represents a new stage in the history of intelligence, or simply another extraordinary invention in the long history of human civilisation.

But consider the possibility: the same evolutionary process that produced the human mind has now given that mind the tools to create forms of intelligence beyond its original design.

For the first time in Earth's known history, intelligence is not merely adapting to its environment. It is building new environments, new tools and perhaps new forms of intelligence within them.

The next chapter of evolution may not be written in genes alone.
