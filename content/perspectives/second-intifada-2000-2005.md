# The Second Intifada: How a Political Crisis Became a Prolonged Uprising

The Second Intifada, also called the Al-Aqsa Intifada, was a major period of Palestinian uprising and Israeli–Palestinian violence that began in September 2000. It unfolded after years of unresolved disputes over occupation, settlements, borders, refugees, Jerusalem, security, and the future of Palestinian self-government. The uprising was not a single battle or a centrally controlled campaign. It developed into a changing pattern of demonstrations, armed attacks, Israeli military operations, suicide bombings, targeted killings, raids, and restrictions on movement.

The immediate trigger is often associated with a visit by Israeli opposition leader Ariel Sharon to the Temple Mount/Haram al-Sharif compound in Jerusalem on 28 September 2000, followed by protests and clashes. But a trigger is not the same as a complete explanation. The political process established by the Oslo Accords had raised expectations of a negotiated settlement, while core questions remained unresolved and trust between the parties had deteriorated. The failure of the 2000 Camp David summit and the absence of an agreed political horizon formed part of this wider context.

## From protest to armed confrontation

In the opening period, demonstrations and clashes spread across the occupied Palestinian territories and Israel. Israeli security forces used live ammunition in some confrontations, and Palestinian armed groups increasingly carried out attacks against Israeli soldiers and civilians. Over time, suicide bombings in Israeli cities became a defining feature of the conflict, killing civilians and deepening public fear. Israeli forces responded with raids, arrests, targeted killings, curfews, closures, and large-scale operations in Palestinian areas.

The violence was not symmetrical in its capabilities or circumstances. Israel possessed a state military, advanced surveillance, and control over many movement and access systems; Palestinian actors included the Palestinian Authority, established factions, and smaller armed groups with different aims and command structures. These differences matter when examining responsibility, capacity, and civilian exposure. They do not make attacks on civilians lawful: deliberately targeting civilians is prohibited regardless of the actor.

## The human and political costs

Thousands of Palestinians and more than a thousand Israelis were killed during the uprising and its associated violence; civilians made up a substantial share of the victims. Exact totals vary according to the dates and categories used by different datasets. Families faced bereavement, injury, detention, destroyed homes, lost livelihoods, and severe limits on everyday movement. Repeated closures disrupted work, schooling, medical care, and commerce. Israeli communities experienced the effects of bombings and persistent insecurity, while Palestinians lived under military operations and occupation-related restrictions.

The conflict also reshaped politics. The Israeli government intensified security measures and began constructing a barrier in and around parts of the West Bank, whose route and effects became highly contested. Palestinian political institutions suffered severe damage, and the Palestinian Authority's legitimacy and capacity were weakened. Armed factions gained influence in some communities, while the prospect of a negotiated two-state settlement became more difficult to realize.

## Why the uprising did not produce a settlement

The Second Intifada illustrates how a political process can collapse when it cannot resolve the issues at its core and when each new act of violence reinforces the other side's fears. Attacks create demands for retaliation and tighter security; raids, civilian casualties, and restrictions can deepen anger and support for further resistance. This feedback loop does not remove individual responsibility. It helps explain how cycles of violence become self-sustaining.

The uprising declined through 2004–2005 amid leadership changes, changing security practices, and renewed diplomatic efforts. It did not settle the underlying conflict. Questions about sovereignty, borders, settlements, Jerusalem, refugees, security, and equal rights remained central long afterward.

## What the record requires us to remember

The Second Intifada should not be reduced to a story of one provocative visit or one side's violence alone. Its causes developed over years; its events involved different organizations and state institutions; and its consequences fell heavily on civilians. A serious history distinguishes documented events from disputed interpretations, counts deaths with transparent definitions, and recognizes that a ceasefire or decline in violence is not the same as a durable political settlement.
