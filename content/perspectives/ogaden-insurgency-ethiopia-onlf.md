# The Ogaden Insurgency in Ethiopia: ONLF, State Forces, and the Peace Process

The Ogaden National Liberation Front (ONLF) fought the Ethiopian state in the Somali Region, an area with a predominantly Somali population and a history of contested political integration. The insurgency developed amid grievances over representation, security, land and resource governance. The conflict’s history includes armed attacks, counterinsurgency operations and serious allegations of abuses against civilians.

## Origins and armed campaign

The ONLF emerged as a separatist movement seeking self-determination for the Somali-inhabited region. Its armed wing fought Ethiopian security forces, while the government treated the organization as a security threat. The conflict was shaped by the region’s remote geography, cross-border family and trade networks, and the strategic importance of energy exploration and transport routes.

In 2007, an ONLF attack on an oil exploration site at Abole killed Ethiopian and Chinese personnel. Ethiopian forces responded with a major counterinsurgency campaign. Human-rights organizations documented allegations of killings, arbitrary detention, restrictions on movement and destruction of livelihoods during security operations. Each allegation should be attributed to the relevant investigation and not generalized to every operation.

## Civilians and humanitarian access

The conflict affected pastoral livelihoods, trade, access to water and movement across the region. Insecurity and restrictions on aid operations made it difficult to assess needs independently. Civilians could face coercion by armed groups as well as state security measures. The archive should record both the source and the level of corroboration for each reported incident.

## Peace agreement and aftermath

In August 2018, the ONLF and Ethiopian government signed a peace agreement after political changes under Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed. The agreement marked a major shift away from the insurgency, although implementation, local grievances and the region’s political future remained matters of public debate. The archive should not describe the armed campaign as ongoing merely because its underlying issues persist.

## Classification and sources

This entry tracks the ONLF–state conflict and its transition to a peace process. It is distinct from Ethiopia’s later conflicts in Tigray, Amhara and Oromia, which involve different actors and political settings.
