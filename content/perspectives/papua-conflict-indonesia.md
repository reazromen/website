# Papua Conflict in Indonesia: Separatism, Security Operations, and Civilian Displacement

The Papua conflict concerns Indonesian state authority and separatist aspirations in the western half of New Guinea. Its roots lie in the contested history of the territory’s incorporation into Indonesia, political identity, resource extraction, migration, racism and longstanding grievances over security-force conduct. Armed resistance has involved groups associated with the Free Papua Movement, often referred to as OPM, and a range of organizations that Indonesian authorities describe as armed criminal groups. These labels are politically charged and should be explained rather than repeated as neutral classifications.

Papua is geographically vast, and the conflict is uneven. Armed incidents have been concentrated in particular highland districts and corridors, while political grievances and security concerns extend more widely. A single national label can hide differences in actors, local history and the intensity of fighting.

## Political history and competing narratives

Papua’s political status remains contested in public memory and political discourse. Indonesia emphasizes territorial integrity and the legitimacy of the process through which the territory was incorporated. Papuan independence advocates argue that the process lacked genuine self-determination and point to discrimination, demographic change and resource extraction. These competing narratives are central to the conflict and should be described accurately without presenting either side’s political claims as uncontested fact.

The conflict has continued through periods of military operations, political accommodation and changes in administrative arrangements. Special autonomy was intended to address some grievances, but critics argue that it has not resolved questions of accountability, local representation and control over land and resources. Political debate and armed activity are related, but peaceful advocacy should not be conflated with participation in armed groups.

## Armed activity and state response

Armed groups have attacked security personnel and, in some cases, civilians and infrastructure. Indonesian security forces have conducted operations in remote areas where access is difficult and civilian communities can become trapped between armed actors. The designation and structure of armed groups vary across time and place, so incident reporting should identify the specific group or unit where credible evidence permits.

Reports from Papua can be difficult to verify independently because of access restrictions, communication barriers and the risks faced by journalists and local observers. Claims about killings, forced displacement or abuse require source attribution, date and location. The absence of independent access should be recorded as a limitation, not treated as proof for or against a claim.

## Displacement and civilian protection

Escalations have displaced residents from villages and highland districts. Some people seek shelter with relatives; others move to towns or temporary sites where access to food, healthcare and schooling can be limited. Security operations, fear of reprisals, disrupted transport and the loss of livelihoods can prolong displacement even after immediate fighting subsides.

Civilian impact should be tracked separately from battle deaths. A displacement figure may refer to a specific district and date, while another source may provide a cumulative estimate or include people who have returned. Combining such figures without checking definitions creates misleading totals. Humanitarian access and safe, voluntary return are essential parts of any assessment of the conflict’s consequences.

All parties have obligations to protect civilians under applicable law. The state has a particular duty to investigate credible allegations of abuse and provide remedies; armed groups must also refrain from deliberate attacks on civilians and other prohibited conduct. Accountability should be based on evidence and individual responsibility, not ethnic generalization.

## Classification and research limitations

For this archive, Papua is a distinct separatist conflict record with regional and time-specific subrecords where evidence supports them. It should not be equated with all political dissent in Papua or with every crime involving weapons. UCDP’s state-based conflict data can help identify organized armed dyads and qualifying years; one-sided violence and non-state conflict datasets capture other patterns. Human-rights and displacement reporting is necessary to understand harms that those datasets do not measure.

The archive should compare the actors and years in each source, note where event-level evidence is incomplete, and avoid combining competing casualty estimates. Annual UCDP/PRIO data through 2025 should be audited alongside separate provisional 2026 reporting.
