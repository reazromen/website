Imagine trying to count every computer humanity has ever built. You would need to include smartphones, desktop computers, calculators, industrial controllers and even ancient mechanical instruments. Now imagine comparing that number with the estimated number of galaxies in the observable universe.

It sounds like a question with a straightforward answer. But there is a problem: **we do not know how many computers humans have made throughout history.**

## First, what counts as a computer?

For this comparison, let’s count functional computing units: physical machines or embedded units designed to perform calculations or process information. A smartphone counts as one complete device, while an automotive electronic control unit (ECU) can count as a separate embedded computer. We would not also count the entire car as another computer.

This distinction matters. Counting complete consumer devices and counting embedded controllers are two different methods, and they produce radically different totals. We will also exclude people historically employed as “human computers”, because our subject is machines, not people.

Computing itself is much older than electronics. The abacus, for example, is a calculating device that uses movable counters to perform arithmetic. Another remarkable example is the Antikythera mechanism, an ancient Greek instrument built around the second century BCE. Its intricate bronze gears modelled astronomical cycles and predicted celestial events. It is often described as the oldest known mechanical calculator or astronomical computer.

Centuries later, Charles Babbage designed the Difference Engine and the more ambitious Analytical Engine. The latter included ideas resembling modern computer architecture, such as memory and a processing unit, although it was never completed during his lifetime.

Electronic computing transformed the twentieth century. ENIAC became one of the major early general-purpose electronic digital computers. On 21 June 1948, the Manchester Baby became the first electronic stored-program computer to successfully run a program stored in electronic memory.

## How many connected devices exist today?

An October 2025 *Global Digital Overview* report offers a useful snapshot. Its connected-device chart lists four relevant categories, excluding fixed phones. Together, they add up to approximately **32.68 billion reported entries**:

- **8.62 billion** mobile phones (any type)
- **1.73 billion** PCs, laptops and tablets
- **17.4 billion** short-range IoT devices
- **4.93 billion** wide-area IoT devices

The report labels the first category “mobile phones (any type)”, but its chart describes the figures as shares of connections. It does not establish that 8.62 billion represents unique physical handsets.

The same report separately gives **5.78 billion unique mobile subscribers** and **8.79 billion cellular mobile connections**, excluding IoT. These are different metrics and should not be treated as handset counts. The report also lists 770 million fixed-phone connections separately; they are excluded here simply to match the four-category comparison.

There is another limitation: the 32.68-billion sum is not a verified count of unique physical computers. These are connectivity statistics, not a global inventory. The categories may overlap, and one device or person can generate multiple connections. Therefore, adding the figures does not prove that every entry represents a different machine.

## Calculators reveal another part of the story

Consider calculators. Casio reports that it has **sold a total of 1.8 billion calculators over 60 years**. The company's figure covers all calculator models and regions and is current as of January 2025.

That is an extraordinary achievement for a single manufacturer. But it is not the total number of calculators ever made. Other companies have manufactured millions more, while mechanical calculating devices existed long before electronic calculators.

Now add computers manufactured by companies around the world, industrial controllers, scientific instruments, embedded systems and machines that have already been discarded.

There is no comprehensive historical database recording every computer ever manufactured. Many early machines were produced in small numbers, and their records have disappeared. Even deciding what qualifies as a computer changes the final count.

Today's connected-device statistics cannot tell us how many computing machines humanity has built throughout history.

## How many galaxies are in the observable universe?

Astronomers face a different counting challenge.

In a 2016 study, a research team led by Christopher Conselice estimated that the observable universe contains approximately **2 trillion galaxies**. The study's central estimate was 2.0 trillion, with substantial uncertainty: roughly 1.4 trillion to 2.7 trillion.

Astronomers cannot simply photograph and count every galaxy. Many are too faint or distant to observe directly. Researchers combine telescope observations with mathematical models to estimate the galaxies that current instruments cannot individually detect.

So even this enormous number is a scientific estimate, not a complete census.

## So, which number is bigger?

We cannot reliably say.

We have estimates for the number of galaxies and statistics showing billions of connected devices. But those figures measure different things. One describes an astronomical population estimated through scientific observation; the other describes selected categories of modern connectivity. Neither tells us the cumulative number of computers humanity has manufactured across history.

Perhaps the more fascinating part of this question is how quickly computing has evolved. Machines that once occupied entire rooms can now fit inside our pockets. Computers are embedded in cars, factories, medical equipment and everyday household objects.

We may never know exactly how many computing machines humanity has built. But one thing is certain: our ability to calculate, process information and understand the universe has grown dramatically.

And perhaps that is a more interesting story than simply deciding whether computers outnumber galaxies.
