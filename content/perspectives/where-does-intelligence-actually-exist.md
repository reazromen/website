There is something peculiar about watching an insect repeatedly collide with a glass window when an open window is only a short distance away. From our perspective, the solution is obvious. The insect needs only to change direction, yet it continues to pursue the same unsuccessful path. We are tempted to call it stupid, as though the entire explanation for its behavior must reside inside its tiny nervous system. But the scene also tells us something about the limitations of our own perspective. We see the glass, understand its physical properties, and recognize the available exit because we inhabit a world of concepts, memories, and expectations that the insect may not share. Its behavior emerges from the interaction between its sensory capacities, its evolved responses, and an environment containing an obstacle that is almost invisible to it. The question, then, is not simply whether the insect is intelligent. It is whether intelligence can be understood by examining an organism in isolation from the world in which it acts.

We tend to imagine intelligence as something contained within an individual mind, a private resource that distinguishes one creature from another. This picture makes sense when we consider a person solving a mathematical problem or a scientist developing a theory. Yet the natural world repeatedly presents us with organized behavior that cannot be adequately explained by looking at a single organism. An ant follows a chemical trail, another ant reinforces it, and others join the route as they discover a source of food. Through local interactions, the colony can develop an efficient network of paths without any individual ant possessing a complete map of the territory. Honeybees present a related puzzle. A colony can regulate its temperature, coordinate the collection of resources, and construct intricate honeycombs even though no individual bee needs to possess a comprehensive blueprint of the entire structure. The result is not merely a collection of isolated behaviors. It is a collective pattern produced by interactions among organisms, their surroundings, and the rules governing their responses.

Scientists use the concept of emergence to describe how large-scale patterns and capabilities can arise from interactions among simpler components. The arrangement of a flock of birds, the organization of an ant colony, and the formation of certain physical structures illustrate different mechanisms through which local processes can produce outcomes at a larger scale. These examples are not identical, and it would be a mistake to conclude that every organized structure is intelligent. A crystal can form a beautiful geometric pattern without any evidence that it thinks, and a river can carve an elaborate network of channels without possessing intentions. The important distinction is between the existence of organization and the presence of intelligence. Organization can emerge from physical laws alone; intelligence involves additional questions about capacities such as learning, inference, adaptation, and problem-solving. Nevertheless, emergence forces us to reconsider a familiar assumption: the properties of a system are not always reducible, in any useful explanatory sense, to the properties of its individual parts.

This becomes especially interesting when we turn from insects to ourselves. Human beings deliberately design buildings, manufacture tools, and construct machines, so we are accustomed to looking for an architect behind every complex structure. A clay pot carries traces of practical knowledge about materials, heat, shape, and utility. A bridge embodies an understanding of forces, loads, and the behavior of materials. But the knowledge required to produce these objects did not originate entirely in the mind of the person who made them. It accumulated through generations of experimentation, observation, failure, imitation, and refinement. The engineer who designs a modern bridge relies on mathematics developed by others, materials whose properties have been investigated over centuries, manufacturing processes maintained by entire industries, and instruments that extend the limits of human perception. Even our most impressive individual achievements depend on a vast inheritance of knowledge stored outside our individual brains.

Language, books, scientific instruments, and institutions allow information to survive beyond the people who first discovered it. A mathematical result can be preserved in a textbook, examined by someone born centuries later, and incorporated into a technology its original author could never have imagined. Human intelligence is therefore both individual and distributed. An individual brain performs the work of perception, memory, reasoning, and decision-making, but the range of problems a person can solve depends substantially on the external systems available to them. A scientist with a telescope can investigate phenomena inaccessible to the unaided eye; a programmer with a compiler and a well-designed library can build upon abstractions created by many other people. These tools do not simply make an already complete intelligence more convenient. They change the effective boundaries of what that intelligence can accomplish.

The same distinction has become increasingly important in artificial intelligence. When an AI model generates a program, answers a question, or proposes a solution, it is tempting to attribute the entire outcome to the model itself. Yet a deployed AI system may include much more than a model: external tools, databases, execution environments, feedback mechanisms, memory, and procedures for testing and verifying results. A model that generates code in a single response operates under different conditions from a system that can execute the code, inspect an error, revise its implementation, and run tests again. The latter system has access to a feedback loop that changes the process through which a solution is produced. Its capabilities depend not only on the model's learned patterns but also on the architecture that connects the model to information, tools, and consequences.

This does not mean that adding more components automatically creates intelligence, or that a system should be credited with understanding merely because it produces a convincing result. Poorly designed feedback can reinforce errors, unreliable tools can introduce new failures, and a successful test cannot establish that software is correct under every possible condition. We must distinguish what the model can do by itself from what becomes possible through the larger system, and distinguish both from what has actually been verified. These are engineering questions, but they lead to a deeper conceptual issue: when a capability appears only through the interaction of several components, where should we say that the capability exists? Is it a property of the model, of the surrounding architecture, or of the relationship between them? The answer may depend on the particular capability under investigation. More importantly, it requires evidence about the mechanism rather than an assumption based on the apparent sophistication of the result.

The insect at the window offers a useful way to return to this question. We cannot explain its behavior simply by listing everything that exists inside its body, because the same insect might behave differently in another environment. Nor can we explain the event by describing the glass alone, since the glass does not collide with itself. What we observe is a relationship between an organism and its surroundings. Changing the environment may change the outcome without changing the organism, just as changing the available tools may alter what a person can accomplish or changing the feedback mechanisms may alter what an AI system can reliably achieve. This does not erase the limitations of the individual. It shows that those limitations become meaningful through the conditions in which the individual operates. Sometimes the environment is not merely the background against which behavior occurs; it is part of the causal explanation for that behavior.

There is a temptation to push this argument too far and declare that everything is intelligent, from rivers and crystals to planets and machines. But that would replace one oversimplification with another. The fact that physical systems generate patterns does not demonstrate that they reason. Collective organization does not establish consciousness, and a machine's ability to perform a task does not settle the question of whether it has subjective experience. Intelligence, organization, adaptation, information processing, and consciousness are related concepts, but they describe different things. A serious account of intelligence must explain which capability is present, at what level it operates, and what evidence supports the explanation. The goal is not to turn every phenomenon into a mind. It is to understand why certain systems can learn, solve problems, coordinate actions, or adapt to changing circumstances, while others produce orderly outcomes through mechanisms that do not require intelligence at all.

The universe does not owe us a simple boundary between the intelligent and the unintelligent, nor does it guarantee that the categories we find convenient will always be the most useful ones for scientific explanation. We are organisms that evolved to make sense of the world from a particular position within it, and our intuitions naturally privilege the individual, the visible, and the immediately understandable. Yet many of the systems that shape our lives operate across scales that no single observer can grasp at once. An ant colony, a scientific community, a telecommunications network, and an AI-powered application are radically different systems, but each invites us to examine how components interact, how information moves, and how local processes constrain or enable larger outcomes. Their differences matter as much as their similarities, because understanding a system means discovering its actual mechanisms rather than forcing it into a convenient analogy.

Perhaps the most productive question is therefore not whether intelligence belongs exclusively to an individual or exists everywhere around us. It is how particular capabilities arise, which parts of a system contribute to them, and how those capabilities change when the relationships among its components change. Sometimes the answer will be found primarily in an individual organism's nervous system; sometimes collective behavior or external tools will be essential to the explanation. In other cases, what appears intelligent may turn out to be the consequence of physical laws or fixed rules rather than learning or reasoning. The task is to find out which explanation is correct. When we encounter something that appears remarkably intelligent—or inexplicably foolish—we should resist the urge to settle the matter with a label. We should examine the agent, the environment, the information available to it, and the architecture connecting its actions to their consequences. The most revealing discovery may not be that intelligence is everywhere, but that we have often misunderstood where, and under what conditions, it becomes possible.
