Imagine that, at midnight, every human being disappears.

No catastrophe follows. No explosion. Just absence.

At 00:01, does the Moon still orbit Earth?

Do ocean waves still break on coastlines nobody watches?

Does an unstable atomic nucleus decay if no detector records it?

The everyday scientific answer is straightforward: our best physical theories do not require human witnesses for these processes to occur. The Moon's orbit preceded astronomy. Stars formed billions of years before human eyes existed. Fossils record organisms that lived before anything resembling modern scientific observation.

But the thought experiment opens a more difficult question: what exactly do we mean when we say science describes a reality independent of us?

## The world before witnesses

Earth is about 4.54 billion years old. Homo sapiens is vastly younger. Light from distant galaxies can have traveled for millions or billions of years before entering a human-built detector.

This temporal asymmetry is powerful evidence against a childish version of observer-centered reality. We explain present observations by processes that occurred before there were people to describe them.

A dinosaur footprint is intelligible because an animal physically altered sediment. A radioactive isotope ratio can carry information about past decay. The cosmic microwave background can be measured now because radiation from the early universe continued propagating long before there were radio telescopes.

These claims are mediated by present evidence, but what they describe is not supposed to have waited for us.

That is a central intuition behind scientific realism: the world has structure that does not depend on our descriptions of it, and successful science can give us knowledge of at least some of that structure.

## Yet every claim is made from here

The difficulty is that nobody steps outside all observation to compare a theory with "reality as it is in itself."

Evidence arrives through instruments and conceptual frameworks. We calibrate detectors using other procedures. We interpret measurements using theories. Even a statement as simple as "the object is 10 metres long" presupposes a unit, a method and a definition of what endpoints are being measured.

Critics of strong forms of scientific realism use facts like these to ask how far empirical success licenses belief in the unobservable entities posited by theories. History adds pressure: past theories could predict successfully in some domains while containing concepts later discarded or transformed.

The argument is not usually about whether tables vanish when nobody looks.

It is about how confidently we should identify the structures in our best theories with the furniture of an observer-independent world.

That distinction is easy to lose in popular debates.

## Construction does not imply invention

Measurements are constructed.

A temperature scale is constructed. A coordinate system is chosen. A telescope image may assign visible display colors to infrared wavelengths our eyes cannot see.

It does not follow that the target is invented.

Latitude is a human coordinate convention; Earth's shape is not merely a convention. A decibel scale is a representational choice; acoustic pressure changes are physical events. The unit "second" has a defined standard; the processes timed do not begin existing only after the definition.

A useful way to say this is:

**Our access to reality is constructed. Reality need not be.**

This is compatible with admitting that some categories are deeply human. National borders, currencies and legal statuses depend on institutions. If humans disappeared, those systems would not continue in the same sense that tectonic plates would.

So "observer-independent" is not one universal test for every kind of real thing. Social reality contains entities whose existence depends partly on collective practices. Physical realism concerns a different class of claims.

## The alien-science test

Consider a thought experiment.

Suppose an intelligent extraterrestrial civilization develops physics independently. Its senses are unlike ours. It has no words corresponding to "red" or "sound." Its mathematics uses unfamiliar notation.

Would we expect its mature science to contain exactly our concepts?

Probably not.

Would we expect absolutely no overlap?

Also probably not.

If both civilizations build spacecraft, they must confront regularities in motion. If both manipulate electromagnetic phenomena, there should be constraints neither culture can vote away. Their descriptions might differ while some structural relationships converge.

This is one reason structural forms of realism attract interest. Perhaps what science captures most robustly is not a final inventory of things described in uniquely human language, but relational structure that survives changes in representation.

That is a philosophical proposal, not an established experimental result. Its value here is to sharpen the question. We can distinguish the vocabulary used to describe the world from the regularities any successful description must respect.

## Independence is tested through resistance

Reality often announces its independence by refusing to cooperate with our expectations.

A spacecraft misses its target if the trajectory model is wrong. A bridge deforms regardless of whether the engineer likes the calculation. A clinical intervention can fail despite a compelling theory. An astronomical observation can exclude a model researchers hoped would survive.

This resistance is epistemically important.

If interpretation were unconstrained, failed predictions would be difficult to explain. Science progresses partly because the world can make some expectations untenable.

Of course, a failed prediction does not always identify which assumption failed. The instrument might be wrong; the model might be incomplete; initial conditions might be mistaken. Investigation is required.

Still, the failure is not whatever we want it to be.

Something pushed back.

## After midnight

Return to the empty Earth.

Without people, there would be no English sentence "the Moon orbits Earth." There would be no SI units, no telescope archives, no astronomy departments. The representation would be gone.

But the distinction that has run through this series remains:

**representation is not the represented process.**

Scientific realism is a debate about how strongly and in what sense we can move from one to the other. It has sophisticated arguments on several sides, and no short essay settles them.

The modest conclusion is enough.

We should not mistake our concepts for the world.

We should also not mistake the fact that we need concepts for evidence that there is no world beyond them.
