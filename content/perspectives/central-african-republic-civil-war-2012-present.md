# The Central African Republic’s Civil War and Armed Coalitions

The Central African Republic (CAR) has experienced recurring armed conflict rooted in weak state institutions, competition over political authority and resources, and the limited reach of public services outside major towns. A major escalation began in 2012–2013, when the Séléka coalition advanced against President François Bozizé and took power. Anti-balaka groups then mobilized against Séléka and Muslim communities, producing widespread civilian harm. Later phases involved splintered armed groups, government forces and international deployments.

## The 2012–2013 crisis

Séléka was a coalition of armed movements rather than a single organization with uniform command. After taking Bangui in March 2013, the new authorities struggled to control fighters and restore public order. Anti-balaka mobilization developed in response to abuses and the collapse of security, but anti-balaka groups also committed attacks against civilians, including Muslims. Neither label should be used as shorthand for an entire religious or regional community.

Violence led to mass displacement and the destruction of homes, markets and religious sites. Communities faced retaliation, looting and restrictions on movement. Reporting should separate verified attacks from broad claims about collective responsibility.

## Fragmentation and peace efforts

The conflict fragmented into local and regional armed networks. Some groups pursued political representation or control of territory; others profited from taxation, roadblocks, livestock and mineral economies. These motives could overlap and change. Peace agreements and disarmament initiatives produced uneven results, while violence periodically flared around elections, local disputes and attempts to control strategic routes.

CAR’s government has relied on international peacekeepers and bilateral security partnerships at different times. Their roles, legal mandates and conduct need separate documentation. A change in the foreign partners supporting the government does not by itself demonstrate that the underlying conflict has ended.

## Civilian protection and accountability

Civilians have faced killings, sexual violence, forced recruitment, arbitrary detention and disrupted access to healthcare and education. Humanitarian access is difficult in remote areas and can be restricted by insecurity. Accountability requires credible investigation of abuses by all parties and protection for witnesses and survivors.

## Classification and sources

This entry treats the post-2012 crisis as a sequence of related phases, not one unchanged contest between two permanent camps. Local armed incidents should be cross-referenced to actor-specific entries and kept separate where they fall below an armed-conflict threshold.
