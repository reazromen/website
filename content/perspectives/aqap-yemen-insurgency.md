# Al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula and Yemen’s Wider Insurgencies

Al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula (AQAP) has been a major jihadist armed organization in Yemen since the late 2000s. Its campaign against Yemeni authorities and foreign interests developed alongside, but separately from, the Houthi movement’s war with the internationally recognized government and the southern political and military landscape. A useful conflict archive must identify the actors in each theatre rather than treating all armed groups in Yemen as one coalition.

## Origins and campaign

AQAP formed in 2009 from the merger of al-Qaeda networks in Saudi Arabia and Yemen. It exploited weak state control, local disputes and periods of political transition to establish a presence in parts of southern and eastern Yemen. The group carried out attacks against security forces, sought to control territory at various moments and targeted civilians. It also attempted external operations, which drew international counterterrorism attention.

During the political upheaval of 2011 and the subsequent periods of state weakness, AQAP and its local affiliates expanded in some areas. Yemeni forces, local tribal actors and external partners later conducted operations that pushed the organization from some strongholds. Such territorial reversals did not necessarily eliminate its networks, and the group’s activity changed over time.

## Relationship to the wider Yemen war

The Houthi–government conflict, the Saudi-led intervention, southern separatist politics and AQAP’s insurgency overlap geographically and sometimes affect the same communities. But AQAP is not interchangeable with the Houthis, the Southern Transitional Council or other armed factions. Their leadership, political objectives, alliances and methods differ. The archive should attribute a specific attack to AQAP only when evidence supports that attribution.

Counterterrorism operations by Yemeni or foreign forces raise questions about civilian protection, detention, sovereignty and accountability. Armed groups’ deliberate attacks on civilians are prohibited, and state operations must comply with applicable law. Civilian deaths should be recorded with source, date, location and uncertainty, especially when parties offer conflicting accounts.

## Local dynamics

AQAP’s ability to operate has depended on local alliances, grievances, security vacuums and the balance among other armed actors. A purely ideological account misses the importance of local power and political economy; a purely local account can miss the organization’s transnational links. Both dimensions matter, but neither should be used to infer that every local dispute is an AQAP operation.

## Classification and sources

This entry tracks AQAP’s organized armed campaign as a distinct layer linked to Yemen’s broader conflict. It should be cross-referenced to, but not double-counted as identical to, the Houthi war or external intervention.
