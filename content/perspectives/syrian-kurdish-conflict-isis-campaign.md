# Syria’s Kurdish-led Forces, Turkey, and the Campaign Against ISIS

The Kurdish-led conflict theatre in Syria overlaps with the wider Syrian civil war but cannot be reduced to it. Kurdish political and military organizations built local administrations in parts of northern and eastern Syria as state authority weakened after 2011. The People’s Protection Units (YPG) and later the broader Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF), an alliance that includes Arab and other local forces, became key partners in the campaign against the Islamic State (ISIS). Turkey, meanwhile, regards the YPG as closely linked to the Kurdistan Workers’ Party (PKK), which it fights inside Turkey and across its borders.

## The campaign against ISIS

ISIS seized territory in Syria and Iraq in 2014, carrying out mass executions, enslavement, persecution and attacks on civilians. Kurdish-led forces, local Arab partners and the US-led coalition fought the group in several campaigns, including the defense of Kobani and the eventual capture of ISIS’s last major territorial enclave at Baghouz in 2019. The territorial defeat of ISIS did not eliminate clandestine cells, detention and displacement challenges, or the unresolved status of people held in camps and detention facilities.

## Turkey’s cross-border operations

Turkey has conducted repeated military operations in northern Syria, targeting Kurdish-led forces and seeking a border zone it considers necessary for its security. Ankara argues that the YPG’s relationship with the PKK makes the force a direct threat. Kurdish-led authorities and their supporters describe the operations as attacks on their self-government and security. These competing security claims do not settle the legality or proportionality of any particular operation; those questions require incident-specific evidence and applicable international law.

The same areas have also been affected by Syrian government forces, opposition factions and other external actors. Territorial control and alliances changed over time, so an account should identify the date, location, actors and stated purpose of each major operation rather than imply one continuous, uniform front.

## Civilian consequences and political questions

Civilians have faced displacement, shelling, airstrikes, disrupted services, arbitrary detention allegations and uncertainty over local governance. ISIS’s atrocities affected many communities, including Yazidis, Kurds, Arabs and religious minorities. Humanitarian needs also persist among people displaced by successive campaigns and those living in camps or areas with limited services.

A lasting settlement requires security arrangements that address Turkish concerns without exposing civilians to renewed violence, and a political framework that clarifies local governance, representation and the relationship between Kurdish-led institutions and the Syrian state. Counter-ISIS operations, the broader Syrian war and the Turkey–PKK conflict should be linked in the archive but not counted as identical conflicts.

## Scope and sources

This page covers the Kurdish-led Syrian forces, Turkey’s operations against them, and the anti-ISIS campaign where those theatres overlap. It is not a claim that all Kurdish organizations share the same goals or command structure. The post-2024 Syrian transition is a separate and evolving phase that requires dated updates before any present-day control claims are made.
