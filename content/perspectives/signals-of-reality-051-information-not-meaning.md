A file can contain millions of bits and tell you nothing useful.

A single short message can change a decision.

Both statements are obvious in everyday life, yet they expose a confusion built into the word information. In ordinary speech, information often means meaningful knowledge. In information theory, the word has a narrower technical role.

Those meanings should not be collapsed.

Claude Shannon's communication theory gives us a way to quantify uncertainty in messages without asking what the messages mean. That is one of its greatest strengths. It is also the reason Shannon information should not be treated as a complete theory of semantics.

## A random string can contain much information

Imagine a source choosing one of many possible strings with roughly equal probability.

Before the choice, uncertainty is high. After the string is observed, that uncertainty is reduced. In Shannon's framework, the observation can therefore carry substantial information.

But the string may have no intended human meaning.

It can be generated by a random-number source, a cryptographic process, or a test harness. The sequence is informative about which outcome occurred without being a meaningful statement.

This is not a contradiction.

It shows that information-theoretic quantity and semantic content answer different questions.

## Meaning requires relationships

A word means something because it participates in a language, a history of use, a context, and a community of interpreters.

The bit pattern for a word does not contain that entire relation inside itself.

The same bytes can be interpreted under different encodings. The same sentence can function as a joke, warning, quotation, password hint, or example depending on context. A symbol's physical shape alone does not determine its role.

Meaning is therefore relational.

This does not make it arbitrary. Languages, conventions, causal histories, and shared environments constrain interpretation. But the constraint is larger than the symbol.

## Correct transmission can preserve falsehood

Suppose a message is transmitted with zero bit errors.

The communication system has succeeded at one layer.

The content can still be false.

A network checksum can verify that data arrived intact without verifying that the data describes reality correctly. A signed statement can verify an origin while leaving the claim itself open to evaluation. A database can replicate a mistaken value perfectly to every node.

Integrity is not truth.

This is a recurring systems lesson: one layer's success condition should not be promoted into another layer's guarantee.

## Meaning can survive imperfect information

The reverse is also possible.

Human language contains redundancy. A sentence with a missing letter may remain readable. A noisy phone call can remain understandable. A compressed image can lose exact pixel values while preserving recognizable structure.

Semantic interpretation can sometimes tolerate substantial physical variation.

That is because meaning is supported by context, expectation, grammar, world knowledge, and relationships among elements.

The channel does not have to preserve every microscopic detail for the receiver to recover the intended concept.

This is one reason human communication and engineering communication should not be equated too literally.

## Data is not knowledge either

The distinction becomes even more important in modern analytics.

A dataset may be large, high-resolution, and precisely timestamped. None of those properties guarantees that it measures the right variables, represents the relevant population, or supports the conclusion being drawn.

Data becomes evidence only in relation to a question and a model of how the data was produced.

Evidence becomes knowledge only after additional work: checking alternatives, uncertainty, mechanism, consistency, and context.

The pipeline is not:

more data → more truth.

It is closer to:

data → model → inference → test → revision.

Information can increase at one stage while understanding remains stagnant.

## Why the distinction matters for AI

Modern language models make the issue impossible to ignore.

A system can manipulate vast quantities of encoded information and generate text that is fluent and contextually useful. Fluency, however, does not by itself certify factual truth. A plausible answer can contain an unsupported claim.

The evaluator still needs sources, verification, and an understanding of the task.

This is not a unique flaw of AI. Humans also produce meaningful-sounding falsehoods. The broader lesson is that semantic coherence and evidential support are different properties.

## Keep the words separate enough to think

Information theory gave us an extraordinarily powerful language for communication because it did not try to solve meaning at the same time.

We should preserve that clarity.

Information can quantify alternatives.

Meaning connects symbols to concepts, intentions, contexts, and uses.

Truth connects claims to the world.

Knowledge connects justified belief to evidence and method.

These categories interact constantly, but none can simply replace the others.

Information is not meaning.

That is not a limitation of information theory.

It is the boundary that made the theory precise.
