Every time a radio transmitter sends a message, the signal does not stop at the edge of the room, the city or even the atmosphere. It travels outward as electromagnetic radiation, moving at the speed of light until it is absorbed, scattered, reflected or simply continues through space. The same physical process that allows a spacecraft to send measurements across the Solar System also carries a faint, expanding record of technological activity away from Earth.

For more than a century, humanity has been doing this without treating it as a message to the universe. Radio broadcasting, television, radar, satellite navigation, mobile networks and spacecraft communications have all produced electromagnetic emissions. Most were designed for a receiver much closer than another star. Yet the universe does not recognise the intended boundary of a transmission. A signal that was meant for an antenna across a valley still travels beyond the horizon.

This raises an intriguing question: if another technological civilisation were looking in our direction, could it tell that Earth is inhabited by a species that has learned to manipulate electromagnetic waves?

The answer is neither a simple yes nor a confident no. It depends on the transmitter, the frequency, the direction in which the signal was sent, the distance to the observer, the time available to observe and the sensitivity of the receiving instrument. Our radio footprint is real, but it is not a uniformly bright beacon.

## A signal is a travelling event

Radio waves are part of the electromagnetic spectrum, just as visible light, infrared, ultraviolet and X-rays are. They differ in frequency and wavelength, not in their fundamental nature. In a vacuum, all electromagnetic radiation travels at the same speed: approximately 300,000 kilometres per second.

That speed makes radio communication across the Solar System practical, but it also gives every transmission a history. Sunlight takes a little over eight minutes to reach Earth. A radio message sent to Mars may take several minutes to arrive, depending on the planets' positions. A signal travelling to a star four light-years away would need roughly four years to get there. If someone received it, they would be observing Earth as it was when the transmission left, not as it exists at the moment of reception.

A light-year is a measure of distance, not time. It is the distance light travels in one year. When we say that a radio signal has reached a star ten light-years away, we mean that its leading edge has travelled for ten years through space. The signal does not arrive everywhere at once, and no reply can return faster than light. Even the simplest exchange with a civilisation ten light-years away would require at least twenty years for a round trip, before allowing for the time needed to detect, interpret and answer it.

The distinction matters because Earth's technological history is not visible across the galaxy as one continuous present. It is spreading outward as a sequence of increasingly distant snapshots.

## The radio shell around Earth

Early radio and television broadcasts began escaping into space during the twentieth century. Radar systems added a different kind of emission: short, powerful pulses often aimed in particular directions. Later, satellite communications, navigation systems and mobile networks added more sources, although many modern systems are designed to minimise wasted energy and confine transmissions to useful paths.

It is tempting to imagine all this activity as a spherical radio shell expanding away from Earth. That image is useful, but incomplete. Transmitters do not radiate equally in every direction. Some signals are tightly focused; others are broad but weak. Some are continuous, while radar and other systems transmit in brief bursts. The strength of a signal also decreases with distance as its energy spreads over a larger area. For an idealised source radiating in all directions, the received power per unit area follows an inverse-square relationship: double the distance and the signal becomes four times weaker.

The result is a complicated footprint rather than a single expanding broadcast bubble. A distant observer would need to be in the right direction, listening at the right frequency and observing at the right time. They would also need equipment capable of separating an artificial signal from natural radio emissions and instrumental noise.

This is why the phrase *Earth is broadcasting into space* needs a little care. It does not mean that every television programme or phone call can be heard by an alien receiver anywhere in the Milky Way. Many everyday transmissions are already difficult to detect over interstellar distances, even with instruments comparable to our own.

## The surprising reach of planetary radar

A 2025 study published in *The Astronomical Journal* approached the problem by asking how far an observer equipped with technology comparable to ours might detect different human technosignatures. Its modelling compared radio transmissions, atmospheric signals, optical and infrared signatures, and objects on or around Earth.

The most detectable radio example was not an ordinary broadcast. It was powerful planetary radar: intermittent transmissions deliberately aimed at Solar System objects. Under the study's assumptions, radar emissions of this kind could be detectable at distances of up to about 12,000 light-years with a suitably capable receiving system. The same analysis estimated much shorter detection distances for other cases, including about 65 light-years for Deep Space Network transmissions, around four light-years for LTE signals and less than one light-year for Voyager's communications.

Those numbers are not a claim that an alien civilisation has already detected Earth, nor do they mean that all those signals have reached the quoted distances. They are modelled maximum detection ranges under specified assumptions about transmitter power, observing time, receiver sensitivity and signal characteristics. The study also notes that some of the most distant theoretical ranges exceed the number of years those systems have been transmitting. A signal cannot be detected beyond the distance it has physically had time to travel.

The distinction between a powerful radar pulse and a routine broadcast is important. A radar transmitter can concentrate substantial energy into a narrow beam and direct it toward a known target. A television or mobile signal is generally not designed to announce itself to a distant star. The former can be a much stronger technosignature, but it is also intermittent and visible only from favourable directions.

## Voyager: a conversation across a day of light

The Voyager spacecraft offer a different kind of example. Launched in 1977, Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 travelled beyond the outer planets and eventually crossed the heliosphere into interstellar space. They continue to communicate with Earth using radio transmitters and NASA's Deep Space Network, a system of large antennas distributed across three locations on Earth.

Voyager's signal is extraordinarily faint by the time it reaches us. NASA describes the received power as far below what an everyday electronic device would need to operate. Detecting it requires large antennas, sensitive receivers, careful pointing and sophisticated signal processing. It is a demonstration of engineering at the edge of what is practical, not evidence that an ordinary receiver could hear a spacecraft from anywhere in the galaxy.

In November 2026, Voyager 1 is expected to reach a distance of one light-day from Earth: the distance light travels in twenty-four hours. At that point, a radio command will take about a day to reach the spacecraft, and its response will take another day to return. A conversation with a machine will have acquired the rhythm of a two-day exchange.

There is something quietly extraordinary about this. The spacecraft is not travelling at anything close to the speed of light. It has taken decades to reach a distance that a radio wave crosses in a single day. The message sent back to Earth is therefore both a technical signal and a measure of the scale separating human engineering from interstellar space.

The Voyager Golden Record is often discussed alongside these transmissions, but it is a different kind of message. It is a physical record attached to each spacecraft, containing sounds, music, images and greetings. It does not broadcast those contents through space. Someone would have to encounter the spacecraft, recognise the record and possess the means to interpret it. The radio telemetry, by contrast, is continuously part of the spacecraft's communication system.

## Listening is not the same as being heard

The Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence, or SETI, has often used radio telescopes to search for signals that might have an artificial origin. A narrow-band signal, an unusual repeating pattern or a transmission with characteristics difficult to explain through natural astrophysical processes could become a candidate for further investigation.

But SETI is not simply waiting for someone to call. Most searches are passive: they listen rather than transmit. Researchers must distinguish possible artificial signals from interference produced by satellites, aircraft, electronics and terrestrial communications. A signal that looks unusual is not automatically extraterrestrial. It must survive checks, independent observations and attempts to explain it through ordinary causes.

The same caution applies in the opposite direction. If another civilisation were searching for Earth, it would not necessarily find a clear signature. It might detect a powerful radar transmission but miss our weaker everyday communications. It might observe atmospheric changes associated with industrial activity without recognising their cause. It might be looking at the wrong time, in the wrong direction or with a receiver tuned to a frequency we rarely use.

There is no universal radio alarm that announces the presence of intelligence. Technosignatures are evidence to be interpreted, not labels attached to a planet.

## The age of a message

The Arecibo message, transmitted in 1974, is one of humanity's best-known deliberate attempts to send a message beyond Earth. It was directed toward the globular cluster M13, roughly 25,000 light-years away. The transmission was a short binary sequence designed to encode information about numbers, chemistry, DNA, the human form, the Solar System and the transmitting telescope.

Even if a civilisation in that direction could receive and decode the message, a reply would take tens of thousands of years to return. The transmission is therefore less like a conversation and more like placing a note in a bottle and sending it into an ocean whose scale is difficult to imagine.

The Arecibo message also illustrates the difference between an intentional message and an accidental technosignature. One was constructed to be recognisable as information. The other is the by-product of technologies built for communication, navigation, science and defence. Both travel through the same physical universe, but their purposes are very different.

## What exactly are we telling the universe?

A radio signal does not contain a complete portrait of the civilisation that produced it. It may reveal a frequency, a pulse pattern, a direction, a bandwidth or an estimate of transmitter power. With enough observations, those features could suggest the presence of engineered technology. They would not immediately reveal our languages, political systems, histories or private thoughts.

Yet a collection of signals could still tell a story. A civilisation that detects changing radar patterns, satellite transmissions and the radio emissions of a technological planet might infer that the source has developed increasingly complex tools. It could observe a world whose atmosphere has been altered by industry, whose orbit contains artificial objects and whose surface produces patterns unlike those of an untouched planet.

That story would be incomplete and delayed. It would describe a past Earth, perhaps one that no longer exists in the same form by the time its signals reach their destination. The further away the observer, the older the version of humanity they encounter.

We often imagine astronomy as the act of looking outward, collecting light from stars and galaxies to understand what exists beyond us. But electromagnetic radiation makes the relationship reciprocal. The universe sends signals toward Earth, and Earth sends signals outward. Our telescopes listen to the past of distant objects while our transmitters carry fragments of our own past into the future.

We are not merely observing the universe. We are transmitting our existence into it. Whether anyone is listening is another question entirely.
