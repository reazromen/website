# The Kivu Conflicts in the Democratic Republic of the Congo

The provinces of North Kivu and South Kivu in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) have experienced recurrent armed conflict since 2000. The violence involves Congolese state forces, local and foreign-linked armed groups, community self-defence organizations and regional actors. It is not one stable war between two sides: organizations split, alliances shift, and control over territory can change quickly.

## A layered conflict landscape

Eastern Congo’s armed conflicts grew from the regional wars of the 1990s, unresolved security concerns after the 1994 genocide in Rwanda, disputes over citizenship and land, competition over local authority and mineral resources, and weak state protection. The Congolese army has fought multiple armed organizations, while some groups have presented themselves as defending particular communities. Such claims should be attributed and tested against their actions; no armed group speaks for an entire ethnic or regional population.

The Allied Democratic Forces, various Mai-Mai groups, the FDLR, and other organizations have operated in different locations and periods. Their histories and aims differ, and a single regional label can conceal important distinctions. Civilians have been exposed to massacres, sexual violence, forced recruitment, extortion, displacement and attacks on livelihoods. Mineral wealth is relevant to some networks but should not be used as a one-cause explanation for all violence.

## State capacity and regional involvement

The Congolese state has struggled to provide reliable security and justice across remote areas. Military operations sometimes alter territorial control without resolving local disputes or dismantling armed networks. Neighboring states have also been involved in eastern Congo’s security environment, through border operations, support allegations and direct or indirect intervention. Each claim requires careful sourcing and should not be presented as proven solely on the basis of a rival government’s statement.

M23’s resurgence and the later regional escalation require a distinct sub-entry, even though they occur within the broader eastern DRC conflict. The archive should cross-link the two records rather than count them as entirely unrelated wars or erase the broader history.

## Civilian protection and accountability

Displacement has repeatedly disrupted schooling, healthcare, farming and trade. Attacks on civilians and sexual violence require independent investigation, protection for survivors and accountability. Humanitarian agencies’ access can be limited by insecurity and administrative restrictions. Reporting should separate confirmed casualty figures from estimates and distinguish civilians, combatants and unknown-status victims.
