# The War in Donbas, 2014–2022: Front Lines, Ceasefires, and Civilian Life

The war in Donbas was the main sustained battlefield of the Russo-Ukrainian conflict between 2014 and the full-scale Russian invasion of February 2022. It centred on parts of Donetsk and Luhansk regions in eastern Ukraine, where armed separatist formations declared self-proclaimed republics after the political upheaval in Kyiv. Ukrainian government forces fought these formations, which received decisive support from Russia. The conflict involved local political grievances and identities, but it cannot be understood solely as an internal uprising: cross-border military, political and material involvement shaped the war.

This essay treats Donbas as a distinct phase within the broader Russo-Ukrainian war. It does not count it as a wholly unrelated war, nor does it fold the eight years before 2022 into the later nationwide invasion as though the scale and geography were unchanged.

## How the front opened

In spring 2014, armed groups seized government buildings and security facilities in several eastern Ukrainian towns. Separatist leaders announced the creation of the self-proclaimed Donetsk and Luhansk “people’s republics.” Ukraine’s government began an operation to regain control. Fighting spread across towns, roads, checkpoints and transport corridors. The parties’ descriptions of the conflict diverged sharply: Kyiv framed the campaign as defending the state against armed separatism and Russian intervention, while separatist authorities described themselves as resisting the Ukrainian government.

Russia’s role was a central issue throughout. The conflict included locally recruited fighters and formations, but evidence of Russian weapons, personnel and direct military involvement complicated claims that the war was an exclusively domestic confrontation. The exact role of particular units and individuals should be established case by case rather than inferred from a group label alone.

During the summer of 2014, government forces regained territory and separatist forces faced serious reverses. The conflict then intensified around strategic locations, including the approaches to Donetsk and Luhansk. The downing of Malaysia Airlines Flight MH17 on 17 July 2014 killed all 298 people aboard. International investigations and legal proceedings established a detailed record around the aircraft’s destruction; the incident also demonstrated how a regional war could produce consequences far beyond its immediate front.

## Minsk and the freezing of the front

Ceasefire efforts produced the Minsk Protocol in September 2014 and a further package of measures in February 2015. The latter called for a comprehensive ceasefire, withdrawal of heavy weapons, monitoring, prisoner exchanges, and a political process. The UN Security Council endorsed the package in Resolution 2202. The agreements were intended to stop the fighting and create a path toward a settlement, but their provisions were interpreted differently and were not fully implemented.

The ceasefires repeatedly broke down. Battles around Debaltseve in early 2015 showed how intense fighting could continue even as diplomatic agreements were being negotiated. Over time, the front line hardened into a militarized zone marked by trenches, artillery positions, minefields and checkpoints. The OSCE Special Monitoring Mission documented ceasefire violations and attempted to monitor the withdrawal of weapons, but access restrictions and security risks limited what monitors could verify.

The resulting pattern was neither peace nor a continuous high-intensity offensive. Some periods saw fewer casualties; others brought sharp escalations. A conflict chronology should therefore use dated phases and local events rather than describe every year from 2014 to 2022 as equally intense.

## Civilian life along the line of contact

The front line divided communities, families and essential services. Residents faced shelling, mines and unexploded ordnance, damaged homes, disrupted water and electricity supplies, and long journeys through checkpoints to reach relatives, medical care, pensions or work. Schools and hospitals operated under recurring security risks. Older people and those without the means to relocate were often particularly exposed.

Civilian casualty totals require careful dating. The UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission recorded 3,106 conflict-related civilian deaths between 14 April 2014 and 31 December 2021, and estimated that more than 7,000 civilians were injured. The total included the 298 people killed aboard MH17 in July 2014; sources may report that aviation disaster separately, so totals should not be added without checking the methodology. These figures are verified or estimated counts for defined periods, not a complete count of every indirect death caused by displacement, deprivation or the destruction of livelihoods.

Humanitarian consequences extended beyond fatalities. Displacement separated households and weakened local economies. Mine contamination and damaged infrastructure continued to affect people after front-line positions shifted. The conflict also produced detention, prisoner exchanges and allegations of abuse that required independent investigation.

## Political deadlock and the road to 2022

The Minsk process attempted to connect security measures with political arrangements, including local elections and a special status for certain areas. The parties disagreed over sequencing and conditions: whether security and withdrawal should precede political steps, or whether political provisions should advance while the security situation remained unresolved. The wider dispute over Ukraine’s sovereignty, Russia’s role and the status of the separatist entities remained unsettled.

Diplomatic meetings did not eliminate the military balance that had emerged on the ground. Russia denied or disputed aspects of its direct role while supporting the separatist entities; Ukraine and many other governments described the conflict as Russian aggression. The legal and political status of Crimea, annexed by Russia in 2014, formed part of the same broader confrontation but was geographically and operationally distinct from the Donbas front.

In February 2022, Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The Donbas front became one of several major theatres in a much larger war. The subsequent phase should be documented separately because the invasion’s scale, geography, weapon use and civilian consequences were substantially different from the lower-intensity years along the line of contact.

## Classification and evidence

This archive records the Donbas war as a phase nested within the Russo-Ukrainian war. Events should be coded with attention to date, location, actor, type of violence and source. State-based battle deaths, civilian casualties, non-state violence and one-sided attacks are not interchangeable categories. The same incident may appear in several public chronologies, but it should not be counted multiple times in an aggregate total.

The UN’s human-rights reporting and the OSCE’s monitoring record are useful starting points, while legal findings and investigations should be consulted for specific allegations. Official claims by any party require corroboration. Where access was restricted or a figure remains uncertain, the uncertainty should be stated rather than hidden behind a precise-looking total.
