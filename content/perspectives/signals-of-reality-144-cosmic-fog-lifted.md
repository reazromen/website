There was no curtain.

No single instant when a switch flipped from opaque to transparent.

The phrase "the cosmic fog lifted" is a metaphor for a changing interaction rate.

As the universe expanded and cooled, conditions increasingly favored neutral atoms over free charged particles.

Photons found fewer electrons to scatter from.

Their paths became longer.

Eventually, light could cross cosmic distances.

## Recombination is a misleadingly ordinary word

Cosmologists call the relevant era recombination.

The name sounds as if electrons and nuclei had once combined before and were now doing so again.

Historically, the terminology stuck even though this was the first sustained formation of neutral atoms in cosmic history.

The process was also not instantaneous.

Ionization fraction changed over an interval.

The last-scattering surface has thickness in time and distance.

Our maps compress that finite transition into an apparent surface.

## Expansion changed equilibrium

At high temperatures, energetic photons could ionize newly formed atoms.

As cooling continued, neutral hydrogen became increasingly stable.

But the transition did not occur exactly when average photon energy fell below hydrogen's ionization energy because the universe contained an enormous number of photons per baryon.

The detailed recombination history requires statistical mechanics and atomic physics.

This is a good reminder that simple threshold stories often hide population effects.

## Photon decoupling followed the chemistry

As free-electron density dropped, photon scattering became inefficient.

Radiation decoupled from matter.

From then on, most CMB photons traveled largely unimpeded through expanding space.

Their wavelengths stretched.

Their angular pattern persisted.

Today we detect a low-temperature microwave background across the sky.

ESA's Planck mission mapped this fossil radiation at high precision.

## The fog lifted into darkness

There is an irony.

Once the universe became transparent, it entered the cosmic dark ages.

There were no stars yet.

The CMB existed, but as expansion continued it redshifted and cooled.

Neutral hydrogen filled much of space.

The first luminous stars would form later.

So transparency did not immediately create a star-filled sky.

It created the possibility for photons to travel.

Sources came later.

## Transparency enabled a new observational universe

Before decoupling, direct electromagnetic observation from today cannot penetrate.

After decoupling, photons can carry information from structures forming across cosmic time.

The transition therefore divides two observational regimes.

Earlier physics is inferred through imprints.

Later structures can eventually be imaged more directly.

The universe did not become simpler.

Our messenger gained range.

## "Fog" has limits as an analogy

Ordinary fog consists of droplets scattering visible light in an otherwise familiar atmosphere.

The early universe was a hot plasma whose density, composition, geometry, and radiation field were radically different.

The analogy captures one property:

short photon mean free path.

It should not make us imagine clouds floating in preexisting empty space.

Space itself was part of the expanding universe.

## A change in matter changed what could be known

This is the most interesting point.

The information available to future observers depended on atomic physics.

When electrons became bound, the universe changed its transparency.

That chemical transition determined the oldest electromagnetic horizon we can see.

The cosmic fog lifted because matter changed state.

And in doing so, it opened a channel that stayed open long enough for photons from the young universe to reach detectors billions of years later.
