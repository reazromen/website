# Insurgent and Cross-Border Violence in India’s Eastern Borderlands

India’s eastern borderlands are not a single insurgency. Nagaland, Manipur, Assam and neighbouring areas have hosted armed organizations with different political objectives, social bases, territorial claims and relationships with the state. Several movements also developed cross-border connections with communities and sanctuaries in what is now Myanmar. A useful archive must keep those campaigns distinct while explaining how geography links them.

## Different movements, different timelines

Naga nationalist organizations have pursued competing visions of sovereignty and political settlement. In Manipur, several valley-based and hill-based organizations have operated with different demands and alliances. Assam’s insurgent history includes the United Liberation Front of Asom (ULFA) and other groups whose support, internal cohesion and negotiation status have changed over time. Bodo movements and other regional mobilizations also require their own chronology rather than being treated as interchangeable with Naga or Manipuri campaigns.

Ceasefires and peace talks do not automatically mean that every faction has disarmed. Splits, renamed organizations and rival leaderships can make a broad label misleading. Conversely, a splinter group should not automatically be counted as a separate war: the question is whether it has an identifiable organization, campaign and period of violence.

## The border dimension

The India–Myanmar border connects armed actors, displaced communities, trade routes and security operations. Instability in Myanmar after the 2021 coup changed the operating environment in several border districts, but not every cross-border movement or refugee flow is evidence of a new armed conflict. Each claim needs dated, attributable reporting.

## How this entry is classified

This is a regional index essay, not a claim that all eastern Indian insurgencies constitute one conflict. The archive should maintain actor-specific records for campaigns that meet organized-violence criteria, then cross-link them where they share territory or security responses. Country-level dataset presence is only a starting point; conflict identity and active years must be checked separately.
