Look at a road.

The experience arrives all at once: surface, distance, color, edges, motion, perhaps a vehicle approaching from the side. It does not feel as if your brain is solving a data problem.

But the input is nothing like the finished experience.

Light reaches the retina. Photoreceptors respond over limited spectral ranges. Retinal circuits transform those responses before signals leave the eye. The optic nerve carries neural activity, not a miniature photograph. Visual pathways in the brain extract and combine information about contrast, orientation, motion, depth and other features.

Somehow, from changing electrical activity, you experience a stable world.

The word **reconstruction** is useful here, but it needs care. The brain is not necessarily rebuilding a hidden perfect image pixel by pixel. Nor does neuroscience support the idea that perception is simply invented from nothing. Perception is constrained by sensory input, body state, prior experience and the structure of the environment.

What feels immediate is the result of a process.

## Start with the missing data

Each eye has a blind spot where the optic nerve leaves the retina. We normally do not experience a black hole in our visual field.

Why not?

Partly because the two eyes provide overlapping information, partly because visual processing uses surrounding structure, and partly because our experience is not a raw map of receptor occupancy.

The blind spot is an easy demonstration that "what the retina sampled" and "what appears in awareness" are not identical.

Eye movements make the mismatch even stranger. Our eyes make rapid saccades several times per second. The retinal image shifts dramatically, yet the room does not appear to leap around. The visual system maintains useful continuity across those movements.

Perception is therefore already doing something a camera file does not do automatically: integrating information across time, movement and context.

## Depth is inferred from clues

A flat retinal surface receives light from a three-dimensional environment.

Depth has to be estimated.

Binocular disparity supplies one cue because the two eyes see slightly different projections. Motion parallax supplies another: as you move, nearby objects sweep across the visual field faster than distant ones. Occlusion, perspective, shading and familiar size can also contribute.

No single cue is infallible.

That is why visual illusions are scientifically useful. They reveal assumptions that usually work. A drawing can create a compelling depth impression even when we know the paper is flat because the visual system is responding to relationships that commonly indicate depth in natural scenes.

An illusion does not prove that perception is useless.

It proves that perception is an inference optimized for a world with regularities.

Most of the time those regularities are informative.

## The world helps the reconstruction

Calling vision "reconstruction" can accidentally make the brain sound like a lonely simulator guessing in darkness.

But the environment participates.

Edges in visual scenes often correspond to changes in material, illumination or geometry. Moving objects produce structured optical flow. Light reflected from surfaces varies in ways constrained by physical optics. The brain did not invent these patterns.

Perception works because sensory signals carry information about causes in the environment.

The relationship is not one-to-one. Different physical scenes can sometimes produce similar retinal patterns, and the same object can produce very different patterns under different lighting. That ambiguity is precisely why context and prior knowledge help.

The nervous system exploits statistical regularities to make a useful estimate of what produced the signal.

## A reconstruction can be better than a raw reading

Suppose a camera sensor contains a few defective pixels.

Displaying the raw values preserves the defect. An image-processing system might interpolate those pixels from neighbors and produce a more useful estimate of the scene.

Has it become less truthful because some values were reconstructed?

Not automatically.

The answer depends on purpose, method and transparency. In scientific imaging, correction steps need documentation because processing can also erase or create apparent features. But reconstruction is not synonymous with fabrication. When the measurement process is understood, inference can recover information more accurately than treating every raw value as sacred.

The same principle appears in astronomy, medical imaging and signal processing. Raw detector output often requires calibration, noise correction or inversion before it corresponds to the quantity scientists want.

"Raw" is a stage in a pipeline, not a synonym for reality.

## Memory extends the construction

Walk into your kitchen with your eyes closed.

You can probably predict roughly where the table is.

Now perception is working with more than current light. Memory supplies a model of the space. Touch and proprioception can update it. If someone moved the table, your prediction may fail dramatically.

That failure is informative.

The internal model is useful because it can be corrected.

This is one of the most important distinctions in the entire discussion. A constructed perceptual model is not necessarily a prison. Because it generates expectations, mismatches can revise it.

The nervous system's exact mechanisms are far richer than the simple engineering language of "model" suggests, and different theories of perception disagree about how central prediction is. We should not turn one computational metaphor into an anatomical fact.

But the broad observation is secure: perception combines incoming sensory evidence with processing shaped by prior structure.

## From reconstruction to reality

At this point an easy philosophical mistake appears.

If the experienced world is reconstructed, perhaps nothing outside the reconstruction can be known.

But that conclusion is too fast.

A measurement can be indirect and still reliable. Vision can be processed and still track objects well enough to catch a ball, cross a road and build a telescope. Independent senses and instruments can test one another. Expectations can fail. Models can be revised.

Mediation does not imply isolation.

It gives us a research program: understand the mediators.

For the first twenty essays in this series, we have moved through maps, paintings, measurements, models, language and observers. Again and again the same distinction appeared: the representation carries evidence about something without becoming that thing.

Now the problem becomes physical.

What actually enters the eye when you look at the road?

Not the road.

Light.

And that is where the next part of the investigation begins.
