Haripada is not introduced as a hero. He belongs to the large population of people whose days are organised around work, modest rooms, familiar streets and the hope that tomorrow will not be much worse than today. That is exactly why the song’s unusual turn works. Anjan Dutt takes a recognisable, almost invisible urban man and places him inside a premise that brushes against science fiction. The extraordinary event does not erase his ordinary life; it makes the narrowness of that life easier to see.

Dutt’s early songs often work like short stories set to music. Rather than building around a general statement about love or despair, they begin with a person whose habits and circumstances can be pictured. “Haripada” follows that method. Its central figure is not a mythic rebel or a romantic ideal. He is a small, familiar person, the kind who can be overlooked by a city that measures people by money, status and usefulness. The song’s speculative element—an encounter with something beyond the everyday—opens a window in that routine.

The contrast matters. Science fiction often uses the unfamiliar to ask what a society takes for granted. Here, the fantastic premise is funny and surprising, but it also gives the listener a way to look again at the person at the centre. If a man’s life is so routine that the world barely notices him, what would it take for anyone to pay attention? The song does not need to turn Haripada into a winner or a chosen one. Its charm comes from the collision between an extraordinary event and a person who has not been trained to expect anything extraordinary.

The musical storytelling is deliberately direct. Dutt’s vocal presence feels closer to a narrator telling a tale than to a singer delivering a grand emotional confession. That approach allows the listener to follow the plot while hearing the social observation beneath it. Humour keeps the song from becoming a lecture, and the everyday character keeps the fantasy from floating away into abstraction. The result is both playful and grounded.

“Haripada” also belongs to a wider change in Bengali popular music associated with Dutt and other contemporary songwriters of the period. Their subjects could be clerks, students, lovers, unemployed young men and people negotiating the ordinary pressures of city life. These were not distant cinematic figures. They were recognisable people whose stories could carry disappointment, absurdity and tenderness without needing to be polished into moral examples.

Listen again to the premise and ask why it feels so effective when attached to someone so unassuming. The song’s imaginative leap is not only a joke about an unlikely encounter. It is a way of interrupting the routine that makes a person disappear into the background. For a few minutes, Haripada becomes the centre of the universe—not because he has become a different person, but because the song finally asks us to look at him.
