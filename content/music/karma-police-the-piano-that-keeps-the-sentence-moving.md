“Karma Police” begins with a piano pattern that feels almost ordinary. The chords arrive in a measured sequence, and Thom Yorke’s voice sits close to the arrangement. Nothing in the opening announces a dramatic rupture. The song gains its tension through repetition and the sense that the narrator is trying to make a system behave according to rules that may not be as fair or stable as he believes.

Released on Radiohead’s *OK Computer* in 1997, the track belongs to an album preoccupied with technology, bureaucracy, alienation and the pressures of modern life. The title borrows the language of an imaginary authority, but the song does not settle into a clear story about who deserves punishment. Its narrator sounds certain enough to make a complaint, yet the repeated phrase gradually becomes strange, as though the demand for order is beginning to expose its own instability.

The piano is an anchor. Its pattern is simple enough to remain in memory, and that simplicity lets the other parts change the song’s atmosphere without breaking its identity. Bass, drums and guitar add weight in stages. The arrangement’s development is less about introducing a new musical world than allowing the existing one to become increasingly difficult to inhabit.

The ending is especially important. The song gradually loses the clean boundaries established at the beginning, with the guitar and production moving toward distortion and disintegration. This is not merely a loud finale. It changes the listener’s relationship to the opening pattern. What first sounded controlled begins to feel like a structure that cannot hold itself together.

That movement gives the song an interpretive ambiguity. Perhaps the narrator is exposing a world that has become intolerable; perhaps the desire to police others is itself part of the problem. The music does not choose one answer. It lets the piano’s orderly beginning and the ending’s collapse remain in tension.

Listen to the track from the first piano chord through the final stretch. The same basic song seems to move from a complaint into an escape from the very structure that made the complaint possible. “Karma Police” shows how arrangement can carry an argument without turning it into a lecture: the music begins with rules, then lets us hear what happens when those rules stop feeling convincing.
