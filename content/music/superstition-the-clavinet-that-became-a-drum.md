The opening of Stevie Wonder’s “Superstition” is so rhythmically complete that it can feel as if the song has begun before the drums arrive. The clavinet riff has a sharp attack and a short decay. Notes are not allowed to blur together for long; each one lands like a small physical gesture. The part functions as melody, accompaniment and percussion at the same time.

Wonder wrote and recorded the song for *Talking Book*, released in 1972. The clavinet—a keyboard instrument whose strings are struck rather than sustained like an organ—was processed through amplification and effects to give it a biting, almost guitar-like edge. But the instrument’s sound is only half the explanation. The riff is arranged so that its gaps matter as much as its notes. Silence becomes part of the groove.

The rhythm section gives the pattern weight without burying it. The bass line anchors the harmony, while the drums reinforce the song’s pulse and add movement around the riff. Each part has a distinct job. Because the clavinet already supplies so much rhythmic information, the arrangement does not need to make every instrument busy. The result is dense in energy but clear in outline.

The lyric concerns superstition and the habit of treating signs or omens as if they control events. Wonder’s delivery is playful and insistent, but the groove gives the warning a kind of irresistible pleasure. That is a productive contradiction: the song tells us not to be governed by irrational fear while making us want to follow every rhythmic cue. The music demonstrates how pattern can capture attention without requiring a listener to believe a single word.

“Superstition” also shows why a distinctive sound is not enough by itself to create a classic riff. The clavinet’s tone is instantly recognisable, but the part would be less effective if it were less economical. It repeats, shifts its emphasis and leaves room for the other instruments. The sound has character because the composition gives that character a clear role.

Listen first to the clavinet alone in your attention, then widen the focus to the bass and drums. The riff seems to bounce between them, even though it is one composed part in a larger arrangement. “Superstition” turns a keyboard into a rhythm machine—and shows that the most memorable musical ideas often work on several levels at once.
