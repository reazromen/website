On the morning of 18 August 1969, Jimi Hendrix played “The Star-Spangled Banner” at Woodstock near the end of a festival already running behind schedule. The performance became one of the defining images of the era, but its power is not captured by the familiar shorthand that calls it simply a protest song. Hendrix's interpretation is more complicated than a single label allows.

The melody remains recognisable, but the guitar changes its emotional weather. Notes bend, sustain and break into feedback. Distortion turns a clean musical line into something unstable; the whammy bar lets pitches fall and rise as if the instrument were struggling to keep its footing. The sounds can evoke the violence and anxiety surrounding the Vietnam War, but they are not literal sound effects with one fixed translation. Their force comes from the way they interrupt a melody associated with ceremony, unity and national identity.

Context matters. Hendrix had already been performing the anthem in other settings before Woodstock, so the moment was not an improvised political gesture invented on that morning. The festival's scale, the exhausted audience and the conflict surrounding the war gave this particular performance its historical weight. Yet Hendrix himself offered a notably plain explanation afterward: he was American, and he played the anthem he had been taught at school. That understated comment sits beside the much larger meanings listeners have found in the music.

Musically, the performance is an argument made through tone. Hendrix does not abandon the melody; he keeps returning to it, then bends it out of shape. The tension between recognition and disruption is the point. We hear something familiar, but the sound refuses to let familiarity remain comfortable.

That is why the performance still invites disagreement. Some hear condemnation, some hear grief, some hear a complicated act of belonging, and others hear Hendrix exploring what an electric guitar can express. The interpretations need not be identical for the musical fact to remain: tone, feedback, phrasing and context can turn a well-known melody into a question rather than an answer.

At Woodstock, a guitar became more than a lead instrument. It became a way to make the sound of a country feel as divided, expressive and unresolved as the moment in which it was heard.
