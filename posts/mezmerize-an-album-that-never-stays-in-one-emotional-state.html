ALBUM NOTE · SYSTEM OF A DOWN · MEZMERIZE

Mezmerize — An Album That Never Stays in One Emotional State

Some albums create a mood and protect it. Mezmerize seems almost suspicious of that idea. It keeps changing its face—funny, violent, melodic, political, ridiculous, mournful—and somehow the instability becomes the thing holding it together.

By Reaz Romen✓ Site verified
Album: 2005Published 2026-09-268 min read
System of a DownMezmerizealbum-notelistening

I think one of the easiest ways to describe an album is to describe its mood. Dark. Warm. Angry. Sad. Euphoric. Melancholic. We do this because it makes albums easier to remember as complete objects. But Mezmerize resists that kind of summary almost immediately. It does not seem interested in holding one emotional temperature long enough for you to get comfortable. The record can be mournful, then explode; it can become absurd, then political, then melodic enough to feel almost sweet, then suddenly ugly again. The strange thing is that none of this makes it feel incoherent to me. The constant change is the coherence.

The opening already tells you what kind of record this is. Soldier Side (Intro) appears quietly, almost like the album is preparing to become something reflective, and then B.Y.O.B. arrives with a completely different physical force. That transition is not just loud versus soft. It changes the emotional contract. You are moved from mourning into aggression, and then even inside B.Y.O.B. the aggression refuses to stay pure: there is satire, catchiness, almost party music sitting inside a song about war. System of a Down can make a section feel ridiculous without making the subject ridiculous. That distinction matters. The humor does not cancel the anger; sometimes it makes the anger sharper.

This keeps happening across the album. Revenga can sound furious and theatrical at the same time. Cigaro pushes macho absurdity so far that the exaggeration becomes the point. Violent Pornography can be disturbingly catchy while attacking the thing it is describing. Radio/Video feels almost celebratory in places, yet there is something slightly sad living behind the melody. Then Question! moves into a completely different space again, where instability becomes almost spiritual. I wrote separately about how that song became connected in my mind with uncertainty and a strange kind of calm. Hearing it inside the album makes me notice that Question! is not an interruption of Mezmerize. It is another version of the same principle: the record keeps changing emotional state before you have finished naming the previous one.

There is a philosophical thing hiding inside that structure. We often behave as if emotions should arrive one at a time. If something is serious, we think we should respond seriously. If something is tragic, laughter can feel inappropriate. If we are angry, beauty can seem like a distraction. Real experience is rarely that clean. A terrible situation can contain something absurd. Grief can coexist with boredom. Fear can arrive beside curiosity. A person can be furious about politics and still notice a melody. Sometimes the mind changes emotional state faster than language can keep up. Mezmerize sounds, to me, closer to that messy reality than an album that chooses one atmosphere and stays loyal to it.

That is probably why the record's short running time matters. It is only eleven tracks and roughly thirty-six minutes, so the changes do not have much empty space between them. There is very little time to reset. One emotional idea collides with the next. Contemporary descriptions of the album kept noticing the same thing in musical terms: abrupt rhythmic changes, aggressive riffs, melodic turns, Armenian and other non-metal influences, two voices moving against and around each other. But I think the important result is not that the record is technically complicated. The important result is that your emotional footing keeps moving. The arrangement becomes psychology.

There is a 2005 interview where Serj talks about sequencing by saying the songs have to “dance” with each other. I like that description because sequencing here is not about making every song resemble the one before it. Dancing requires difference. One thing moves, another responds. Daron has also talked about not approaching music through formal time-signature knowledge so much as knowing how he wants it to feel. That makes sense when I listen to Mezmerize. You can analyze the meters, guitar parts, vocal harmonies and transitions, but the first thing the record does is not theoretical. It changes how your body expects the next few seconds to behave.

The Serj and Daron vocal relationship is a big part of this. If the whole album were carried by one voice, some of these emotional changes might feel like separate songs doing separate jobs. Instead, the two voices let contradictory states exist at once. One can sound severe while the other becomes melodic. One can push the line forward while the other turns it into something almost theatrical. Sometimes they reinforce each other, sometimes they feel like two interpretations happening simultaneously. It gives the album the feeling of an argument that has somehow learned harmony.

By the time the record reaches Old School Hollywood and then Lost in Hollywood, the emotional movement becomes almost obvious in the titles themselves. One is strange, synthetic and comic in a way that can initially feel detached from the rest of the record. Then Lost in Hollywood closes the album with something far more exhausted and disillusioned. Again, I do not hear the earlier absurdity and the later sadness as contradictions that need to be solved. They make more sense beside each other. A place can be glamorous and ridiculous and lonely at the same time. A culture can sell fantasy while producing disappointment. The change in tone is part of the observation.

Listeners still disagree dramatically about which parts of Mezmerize matter most. In fan discussions one person's essential song is another person's skip, while tracks such as Question!, Radio/Video, Revenga, Sad Statue, Lost in Hollywood and even the deliberately stranger songs keep attracting completely different kinds of attachment. I like that disagreement because it fits the album. A record that refuses to stay emotionally still probably should not produce one clean consensus about where its center is.

I do not think Mezmerize is interesting because it contains many styles. Plenty of albums contain many styles. What interests me is how quickly those styles become emotional states, and how little warning there is before one replaces another. The album does not say: now we are angry, now we are funny, now we are sad. It simply moves. Maybe that is why it can feel so alive even after repeated listening. You are not returning to one mood. You are returning to the movement between moods.

And maybe that is the connection I keep finding between the album and Question!. The song became important to me at a moment when fear and calm somehow existed together. Mezmerize seems built from that same refusal to keep emotions separated into neat containers. It understands that something can be beautiful and disturbing, ridiculous and serious, heavy and strangely light, sometimes within the same minute. The album never settles long enough to tell me what I am supposed to feel. I think that is exactly why I keep listening to it.

Context for this note includes System of a Down interviews from 2005, contemporary reviews, and recurring listener discussions. Mezmerize was released May 17, 2005; the official 11-track release runs about 36 minutes.
